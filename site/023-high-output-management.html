High Output Management (Grove): leverage, one-on-ones, and what a manager produces

Andy Grove's 1983 manual as the third antidote – the breakfast factory, the limiting step, leverage, the one-on-one, task-relevant maturity as the answer to the Peter Principle, paired indicators, and why the book reads like a factory floor because it came from one.

27 August 2026

Chapter One: The Three-Minute Egg

Grove opens his book with breakfast, and I will too, because it is the best teaching example on this shelf and he wrote it in nineteen eighty-three for people who ran wafer fabs. You are a waiter, and your job is to deliver a breakfast of a soft-boiled egg, buttered toast, and coffee, hot, together, on time. The egg takes three minutes to boil. The toast takes a minute or so. The coffee is already made. What do you do first? You start the egg, because the egg is the limiting step, the operation that takes longest and that everything else must be arranged around. You put the bread in while the egg boils, and you pour the coffee last, so that all three land on the tray together. Production management in one paragraph, and Grove spends the next twenty pages getting more out of it: what happens when the egg-boiler breaks and you have to buy eggs pre-boiled, which costs more but saves capacity; what a customer waiting in line represents in inventory; where in the process you would inspect, and whether inspecting every egg is worth it, or whether you should sample. Then he scales it. The breakfast factory serves a thousand breakfasts an hour. Now the egg is a continuous boiler, the toast is a conveyor, and the questions are the same, and they are the questions of the plant he actually ran.

The question this book answers, and the reason it belongs in the shelf's antidote season, is this: what does a manager produce? The cynical canon spent a month answering that question from the outside, and the answers were blame, cover, retinue, and paperwork. Grove's answer is from the inside, and it is a formula. A manager's output is the output of the organisation under him plus the output of the neighbouring organisations under his influence. Nothing the manager does with his own hands counts. Only what the people under him make. And from that formula follows everything else in the book, including the parts that will make a hospital manager wince.

Chapter Two: Who He Was

András Gróf was born in Budapest in nineteen thirty-six to a Jewish family. He survived scarlet fever at four, which left him nearly deaf; he survived the German occupation, hidden with his mother under a false name while his father was in a labour battalion; and he survived the Soviet siege. In nineteen fifty-six, when the Russians put down the uprising, he walked across the border into Austria at twenty, with the clothes he stood in, and reached New York the following January. He learned English, took a chemical engineering degree at City College while working as a busboy, and a doctorate at Berkeley, and joined Fairchild Semiconductor in sixty-three. When Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore left Fairchild five years later to found Intel, Grove was their first hire. He ran the operations for two decades, became president in seventy-nine and chief executive in eighty-seven, and by the time he stepped down in ninety-eight Intel made the processor in most of the world's computers. Time magazine made him its man of the year in ninety-seven. He wrote a memoir of the Hungarian years, Swimming Across, and a second management book, Only the Paranoid Survive, and he died in twenty sixteen.

The biography is in the book in one way that a reader should know about. Grove came to management as a physical chemist with a hearing aid, and he treated it as an engineering problem: a set of processes with inputs, throughputs, limiting steps, and measurable outputs. That is the book's strength and the whole of the case against it, and I will come back to both. For now, hold onto the fact that a manual on management written by a refugee who had been a busboy contains not one page about ambition, politics, or self-presentation. Whyte's organization man, from episodes six to eight, spent his career managing his image. Grove's manager spends it managing a fab.

Chapter Three: Leverage

Once the manager's output is defined as the output of the team, the manager's problem becomes one of allocation: where does an hour of the manager's time produce the largest increase in the team's output? Grove's word for that is leverage, and the book is a catalogue of high-leverage and low-leverage activities. A manager who spends an hour training a new member of staff who will then work for a year at a higher level has produced, on Grove's arithmetic, something like two hundred hours of improved output from a single hour of input. A manager who spends the same hour redoing a subordinate's work, because he can do it faster, has produced sixty minutes of output and taught the subordinate that the work will be redone. The first is leverage. The second is what most managers do most of the day.

He is specific about negative leverage, and this is the section I would give to any new consultant. A manager who is depressed depresses the people around him, and their output falls; a manager who is late to his own meetings costs each attendee the waiting time, so a manager who is ten minutes late to a meeting of eight people has spent an hour and twenty minutes of the organisation's time; a manager who interrupts is the highest-leverage negative activity there is, because an interruption does not cost the interrupted person the minute it takes, it costs them the twenty minutes it takes to get back to where they were. Grove's remedy for the last is what he calls batching: the manager sets aside fixed times when interruptions are welcome, and in return is left alone the rest of the time. The hospital equivalent is the consultant who is available at the board round and the afternoon huddle and unreachable in between, and whose juniors, after a week of it, have learned to save their questions and ask better ones.

The idea of leverage carries an implication that Grove states and most managers avoid: the manager's calendar is the manager's output. Every hour spent in a meeting that did not need him, every report read that changed no decision, every task done personally that a subordinate could have done, is output the team did not get. The shelf spent an episode on Parkinson's law, that work expands to fill the time available; Grove is the practitioner's reply, that a manager who knows what his output is can tell when the expansion is happening, because it shows up as hours spent at low leverage.

Chapter Four: The Meeting Is the Work

Grove's second heresy, for a reader raised on the belief that meetings are where work goes to die, is that meetings are the medium in which managerial work is done, the way the operating theatre is the medium of surgery. The mistake is to treat all meetings as one thing. He divides them in two. The process-oriented meeting is regular, scheduled, and expected: the one-on-one, the staff meeting, the operations review. The mission-oriented kind is called to make a specific decision and should be rare, because it is expensive and because if you need a lot of them your process meetings are failing.

The one-on-one is the centre of the book, and it is the practice that Silicon Valley took from Grove and that most hospitals have never heard of. The manager meets each direct subordinate, alone, regularly, for an hour or so. The subordinate sets the agenda and does most of the talking. The manager's job is to listen, to coach, and, above all, to learn what is going on, because the one-on-one is the manager's best source of the information his formula depends on. Grove's instruction to the manager is that when the subordinate has finished on a topic, ask one more question, since the thing the subordinate did not intend to mention is usually behind the last answer. He is exact about frequency: weekly for a subordinate new to the job or the task, fortnightly or monthly for the experienced one, and he calls the meeting the subordinate's meeting, not the manager's. The hour belongs to the person being managed. I know of no hospital that runs this. Consultants see their registrars on the ward round, in a crowd, with the patient present, and the annual appraisal is the only scheduled hour they will ever spend alone, and it is spent filling in a form. Grove would say the hospital has decided not to know what its people know.

He has a set of rules for the decision meeting that are worth carrying too, because they answer the shelf's Jackall problem directly. Jackall's managers, from the first episode, kept decisions vague so that blame had nowhere to land. Grove's rule is that before the meeting ends the group must know what decision was made, who will act on it, by when, and, if the decision was not made, when it will be. The decision is to be made at the lowest level competent to make it, by the people closest to the information, and the more senior people in the room are to keep quiet until the junior ones have spoken, since a senior view expressed early ends the discussion. He calls the whole thing the ideal decision-making process, and he is candid that Intel did not always manage it. The difference from Jackall's world is that at Intel the failure to reach a decision was counted as a failure.

Chapter Five: Task-Relevant Maturity, and the Answer to Peter

Here is the chapter that the shelf has been waiting for since episode thirteen. Laurence Peter's principle, that every employee rises to their level of incompetence, was the funniest book on the cynical shelf and the one whose mechanism the shelf found most robust: promotion by performance in the current job guarantees eventual promotion into a job the person cannot do. Grove read Peter, and he agrees with the mechanism, and his answer is not to abandon promoting on performance, because he can see no alternative, but to change what a manager does with the person who has just been promoted.

The tool is what he calls task-relevant maturity, and it is a claim that the right way to manage someone depends on how well they can do the specific task in front of them, not on their competence in general. A superb engineer moved into a manager's job has low task-relevant maturity in management on Monday morning, whatever her maturity as an engineer, and she should be managed the way a beginner is managed: with close structure, clear instructions, frequent check-ins, the weekly one-on-one. As she gains maturity in the task, the structure comes off, and the same manager manages her by objectives, then by little more than occasional conversation. Then she is moved to a new task, or the task changes under her, and the structure goes back on. The management style tracks the person's competence at the task, and it changes both ways.

Read that against Peter. Peter's promoted employee fails because the organisation, having promoted her on the strength of her old competence, manages her as if she still had it. It leaves her alone. Grove's promoted employee is managed as the novice she now is, and either learns the new job under structure or is found out early, in the one-on-one, before the failure becomes the department's. And Grove adds the second half of the answer, which is about what happens to the ones who are found out. He calls it recycling, and he argues that moving a person back to the level at which they performed well should carry no disgrace, because the alternative, leaving them in the job they cannot do because a demotion would embarrass everyone, is exactly Peter's final placement. Intel, he says, did this, and the person often flourished. Whether it is possible in a hospital, where a step down in title is a public event and the professional culture treats it as a wound, is a question the book cannot answer, but at least it names the problem as one of the organisation's courage rather than the employee's ceiling.

Chapter Six: Indicators, and the Gaming of Them

The book's chapter on measurement is the one that will matter most to a clinician, and I want to read it against the shelf's long argument about targets. Grove ran a factory, so he measured everything: output per shift, yield, inventory, defects. He knew what happens when you measure one thing, which is that people produce that thing and let everything else go. His rule was that every indicator be paired with a second indicator that measures its opposite failure. Measure the number of vouchers processed by an accounts clerk and you will get many vouchers processed badly; so pair it with the count of errors found. Measure the inventory level and stockouts follow; pair it with the frequency of stockouts. Measure the janitor's square feet cleaned and pair it with an inspection of the rooms.

A hospital manager will recognise the failure the pairing is meant to prevent, because the health service has lived through it. Measure the four-hour wait in the emergency department and you get patients moved into corridors at three hours fifty-nine, or held in ambulances outside the door so the clock has not started. Measure length of stay and the result is discharges at eight in the evening with no transport home. Every one of those is an unpaired indicator, and the shelf will come to the theorists of that failure, Ridgway, Goodhart and Campbell, in an episode of their own. What Grove offers is the practitioner's version, from a decade before Campbell's law had a name in management: the measured thing will be produced, so measure two things that cannot both be gamed in the same direction. The four-hour wait paired with the rate of readmission within a week. Length of stay paired with the discharge-hour distribution. It is not a complete defence, because a clever enough organisation can game two indicators, and Grove's own book makes the point that the indicator is a tool for the manager's attention and not a substitute for it. But it is the difference between an organisation that has thought about gaming and one that has published a target and awaited the consequences.

Grove's other contribution to measurement is the one the Valley later took from him and renamed. He describes Intel's system of management by objectives, in which each person has a small number of objectives for the quarter, each with key results by which it can be seen to have been met or not, and the objectives at each level are derived from the level above. He is emphatic that the number must be few, since a person with ten objectives has none, and that the key results must be things that can be checked, not aspirations. John Doerr, who worked at Intel under Grove, carried the method to Google in nineteen ninety-nine, and it has spread under the name objectives and key results to most of the technology industry and, in the last decade, to hospitals. Grove's own chapter on it is shorter and better than the books that followed, and it contains a warning the followers dropped: the objectives system is a tool for focusing attention, and when it becomes a tool for evaluation, when people are paid by their key results, the pairing problem returns, because now the results will be gamed.

Chapter Seven: The Door

The book's sequel is the best evidence for the book, and I want to spend a chapter on it, because it shows the methods under load. In nineteen eighty-five Intel was a memory company. It had invented the dynamic random-access memory chip and built the firm on it, and by the mid-eighties the Japanese manufacturers were making the same chips better and cheaper, and Intel was losing money on every one. Grove tells the story in Only the Paranoid Survive, from ninety-six. He was in his office with Gordon Moore, the co-founder and then chairman, and he asked him a question: if the board threw them both out and brought in a new chief executive, what would that person do? Moore said the new man would get Intel out of memories. Grove's reply was that in that case they should walk out of the door, come back in, and do it themselves.

Two things about that story belong on this shelf. The first is that the decision had, in Grove's account, already been made by the people below him. Intel's production planners had been shifting wafer capacity from memory to the microprocessor for over a year, on the arithmetic of the margins, without any strategic announcement, so that by the time the executives decided to leave the memory business the factories had half-left already. Grove calls the middle managers who see the change first the Cassandras, and the chapter is a plea to listen to them: the one-on-one from the first book, applied to the survival of the firm. The people who knew were the people with low rank and high contact with the data, and the organisation's job was to let their knowledge move upward before it was too late. That is Hirschman's voice mechanism, run inside a company that had built the channels for it.

The second is the walk to the door. Grove's insight was that he and Moore could not make the decision as themselves, because the memory business was their identity, and that the way to make it was to imagine themselves replaced. It is an exit-and-re-entry, in Hirschman's vocabulary, performed inside one afternoon, and it is the most practical trick in either book. A clinical director who cannot close a service she built, a research group that cannot drop the line of work that got it funded, a department that cannot stop a clinic nobody attends, all need the door. The question is what a stranger, appointed tomorrow with no history here, would do on their first morning, and then whether there is any honest reason not to do it today.

The sequel also records a failure, and Grove's handling of it is the best short case in management I know for the idea that the manager's output is the organisation's. In ninety-four a mathematician in Virginia found that the Pentium processor divided certain numbers wrongly, at the ninth decimal place, in about one calculation in nine billion. Intel's engineers, correctly on the numbers, judged that no ordinary user would ever encounter it, and the company's first response was to replace the chip only for customers who could show they needed the precision. The response was a technical answer to what had become a trust question, and it was destroyed on the internet and in the newspapers within weeks; IBM stopped shipping Pentium machines; and Intel wrote off four hundred and seventy-five million dollars to replace every chip on request. Grove's own account is that he had missed the change from a company selling components to engineers who understood a defect rate to one selling a brand to people who did not, and that his indicators, all of which were engineering indicators, could not show it. The pairing rule from the first book had been broken by its author: the defect rate had no partner measuring what the customer believed. He learned it at the cost of half a billion dollars and wrote it down, and the writing down is why I trust him on the rest.

Chapter Eight: The Case Against

The objections to this book divide into complaints about its style and objections to its world. The style objection is the one every reader makes: the book reads like a manufacturing manual, because it is one, and the metaphors of the fab, throughput, yield, inventory, inspection, sit awkwardly on work that is not a fab. A Goodreads reviewer's complaint that the task-relevant maturity chapter could have been one sentence, learn about your subordinates so that you know how to manage them, is unfair to the chapter and fair to the tone. And the maturity idea itself is not Grove's; it is a restatement of the situational leadership model that Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard had published in the seventies, which Grove acknowledges in passing, and which the academic literature has found hard to confirm in controlled studies. Grove's contribution was to show it working in a company, which is evidence of a kind, and not the kind the model's critics were asking for.

The world objection is more serious. Grove's manager is a manager of engineers making a physical product with a measurable yield, in a company where the people under him reported to him, where he set their objectives and could move them between jobs. The book has no theory of the professional who does not report to the manager, and so it has nothing to say about a hospital. A clinical director does not set the consultants' objectives, cannot recycle one of them to a lesser post, and cannot run a one-on-one as the subordinate's meeting with a colleague who is not a subordinate. Grove's leverage arithmetic assumes an authority that the medical director does not have, and a manager who applies the book without noticing this will spend a year wondering why the fab metaphors have produced resentment and no throughput.

And then there is Intel. The company Grove built did, for twenty years after his book, exactly what he said a well-managed organisation would do. It also, in the two decades after he left, missed the mobile processor, fell behind in fabrication technology for the first time in its history, and lost the position that Grove's methods had won. Ben Horowitz, who wrote the foreword to the twenty fifteen reissue and did more than anyone to make the book a Valley scripture, would say that the methods were abandoned, not refuted. I think that is probably right, and I think it concedes something Grove did not say: that a management system is only as durable as the manager who insists on it, and that the book contains no mechanism for making it survive him. The shelf's cynical canon was, among other things, a study of what happens to good systems after the founder goes. Grove's book is a study of the founder's system. Both are true. The second does not answer the first.

Chapter Nine: What a Clinician Can Take

Set the fab metaphors aside and what is left is a small number of practices that the shelf's diagnosis says a hospital needs and that a hospital can actually run. The first is the formula. A consultant who has just become a clinical director and still thinks of her output as her clinics and her lists has not yet become a manager, and the fastest way to become one is to ask, every week, what the department produced and what she did that made it produce more. Most of what she did will fail that test, and the failing items are where the leverage is hiding.

The second is the one-on-one, run as Grove describes it, the subordinate's agenda and the manager listening, and I will constrain it to the relationship where it can actually be run: the consultant and her registrars. Half an hour, fortnightly, alone, no form, the registrar talks. It would be the only hour in the training in which someone senior heard what the junior actually thought was going wrong, and the shelf's whole Hirschman episode was about the cost of not having that hour. The third is the pairing of indicators, and any manager who reports a number can do it from tomorrow: for every metric that goes to the board, name the way it will be gamed, and report the metric that would catch the gaming beside it.

And the fourth is the answer to Peter, the one that costs the most. The department's newly promoted registrar, the new consultant, the new lead, has low task-relevant maturity in the new job whatever they were before, and the kindness of leaving them alone is Peter's mechanism at work. The structure goes on for the first year. It comes off when the maturity is there. Grove would add that the person who does not get there should be allowed a way back that is not a humiliation, and that the organisation which cannot offer one has chosen Peter's final placement for its own convenience.

Chapter Ten: Verdict, Dose, and a Question

The verdict is that this is the most useful book on the shelf for a person who has just been put in charge of something, and that it should be read with its world in mind. Read the twenty fifteen edition, with Horowitz's foreword and Grove's own preface from ninety-five, which adds the parts about email and the middle manager's exposure to the market that the eighty-three edition did not have. Read chapters one and two, the breakfast factory, first, because the whole method is in them. Read the one-on-one chapter twice. Skip nothing, since the book is short, but read the chapter on interviewing with the knowledge that hospitals do not hire the way fabs do.

The dose is different from the shelf's usual warning, and different from Ostrom's too. Grove is not poison at any dose, but he is a drug with a specific indication, which is a manager who has the authority his methods assume. A reader without that authority who takes the whole book will try to run a fab in a building full of people who do not work for her, and the book will get the blame for the result. Take the formula, the one-on-one, the paired indicator, and the maturity idea, and leave the throughput arithmetic to the people with a throughput.

The question to carry into the week is Grove's own, and it is the best single management question I know. Take your calendar for last week and, for each hour, ask what the output of your team was increased by that hour. Not what you did; what they produced because of it. The hours that have no answer are the hours in which you were not managing, and there will be more of them than you can comfortably count, and the arithmetic, once done, is hard to un-know.

Next time, the fourth and last antidote, and the one that turns the shelf's instruments on the reader's own life: Clayton Christensen's How Will You Measure Your Life?, from the last class he taught his Harvard students each year, on what a theory of business has to say about a career, a family, and staying out of jail. Thanks for listening.
