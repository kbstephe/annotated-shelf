The Power Broker (Caro) Part Two: what the roads cost, and who stopped Moses

Part two of Robert Caro's biography of Robert Moses: the Cross-Bronx route that took fifteen hundred homes it did not have to, the relocation statistics nobody checked, Jane Jacobs's park fight as a counter-theory, and the one document Moses could not draft his way out of.

30 October 2026

The letters arrived in the Bronx on the fourth of December, 1952, in hundreds of mailboxes at once. Each was signed by Robert Moses, as City Construction Coordinator, and each told its reader that the building where the family lived lay in the path of the Cross-Bronx Expressway, that the city would take it and tear it down, and that the family had ninety days to leave.

A woman named Lillian Edelstein remembered afterwards that it was a mild day for the season. People stood outside asking each other whether they had got the letter. There had been rumors for years that a road was coming, and people had stopped believing them. They had assumed it would run along the edge of Crotona Park. It did not.

Last time the question was how a man who never won an election came to hold more power than the mayors and governors who outranked him, and the answer was that power lived in documents: the bill he drafted, the definition nobody read, the bond covenant that made his authority a contract. This time the question is what he spent that power on, and whether a contract that no governor could break could be broken by anyone at all. The first half of the answer is a single mile of road in one neighborhood. The second half is a meeting in a townhouse, sixteen years later, that lasted fifty minutes.

Chapter One: The Staging Area

Caro takes his time over East Tremont before he lets the road arrive, and the time is the argument. The neighborhood sat in the middle of the Bronx, on a subway line that ran straight down to the garment district, and in 1950 it held something like forty-four thousand Jews along with about five thousand Irish and five thousand Germans and Slavs, by Caro's own hedged count. It was a place of old apartment houses without elevators, with courtyards, and with a scattering of small frame houses that let in the light.

Moses called them tenements and walkups and, when those words did not produce the right horror in his listeners, slums. The people who lived in them disagreed in unusual detail. A Mrs. Silverman told Caro she had grown up in real tenements on the Lower East Side and these were not that. Her foyer was an L-shape, big enough that she once served dinner for eighteen in it. She paid a hundred dollars a month, which was on the high side, and another woman with the same number of rooms paid sixty-two.

Rent control and cheap land held those rents down. They mattered because weekly take-home pay in the garment trade was, on average, well under a hundred dollars, and because these families had a rule of their own that overrode the usual rule of thumb about rent: when you had children, you sent them to college, whatever it cost. So the rent was the most they could pay, and one illness or one death meant a family was gone. In a city whose postwar vacancy rate Caro puts at about one percent, there was nowhere comparable at a comparable price. The new buildings near Pelham Parkway rented a two-bedroom for three hundred and fifty. The alternatives within their means were in the black and Puerto Rican slums, or back on the Lower East Side.

Caro then makes the argument that goes beyond the families. For a hundred years East Tremont had done a job for the city. It was where newcomers who had lived in American slums found their first decent apartment, learned to live beside people from other villages and other countries, and organized politically before moving up the line. A staging area. In 1950, he says, it was integrated and doing this for the newest arrivals, the black and Puerto Rican families coming to the city, as it had done for the Irish and Italians before. His claim is that if the city wanted its newcomers to become citizens rather than a walled-off and resentful population, places like this had to survive, and that if left alone this one would.

You can hear the book's method in that setup. He does not describe the harm and then explain what was harmed. He first makes you want to live there, and only then lets the letter come.

Chapter Two: Two Blocks South

The women of East Tremont did what people do. They formed a committee, which became the East Tremont Neighborhood Association, and it found a leader in Lillian Edelstein, a housewife who by Caro's account had never led anything. They did not argue about whether the road should exist. They argued about where.

A civil engineer, who agreed to study the question only on condition that his name stay off it, drew up the alternative: a complete engineering study showing that the road could run through the park edge, swung two blocks to the south and in some places one. The study said it met every state and federal standard for an expressway. It would not be longer than Moses's route and its curves would not be sharper. Moses's route took one hundred and fifty-nine buildings, fifty-four of them apartment houses, and fifteen hundred and thirty families. The committee costed the two routes. Moses's mile would cost more than ten million dollars more, before the price of relocating anyone.

The alternative would take six old brownstones, which held nineteen families, and one other structure, a depot of the Third Avenue Transit Company at the corner of the park.

Fifteen hundred and thirty families, or nineteen and a garage for buses.

The committee took this to the Board of Estimate, the body whose vote Moses needed, since the city had to acquire the land for each road. Around the table sat the mayor and the city's other top officials. Henry Epstein, a Deputy Mayor and an older lawyer the mayor respected, had at first told the tenants the alternative was the better route. The mayor, Robert Wagner, who had been sworn in under the chandeliers in part one's opening scene, had during his campaign told the association in writing that he would vote against any resolution to take the property.

The vote came, and Caro lets it go fast. The Bronx borough president, James Lyons, moved the question, saying it was an engineer's problem and not a layman's, and that all the engineers unanimously supported the route. Each member voted yes in turn. Wagner voted last, and he voted yes. Lillian Edelstein had been sure they would win because of Wagner; she was crying at the table. A lawyer from the neighborhood, Arthur Katz, asked the mayor what the relocation plans were, quoting his own promise. Wagner said he did not know. He turned to a man from Moses's office, who said the work would go to a private firm he described as highly efficient.

What had happened to Epstein, who had been the tenants' best hope? He had been asked to oversee a fair study of the two routes, had told Lillian Edelstein not to worry, and at the last moment reversed himself in a letter backing Moses's route. Caro asked Moses about it years later, in a cottage at Oak Beach, with the Robert Moses Causeway visible through the window. Moses first said it was a complicated matter with personal stuff in it and a couple of agitators. When Caro prodded him, he said Epstein had written the letter, yes, after he was hit over the head with an ax. Caro asked what kind of an ax. Moses said he would not tell him what they did to Epstein.

The reason for refusing the alternative in the first place is a separate question, and Caro is straight about how little he can prove. Moses's given reasons, he says, were demonstrably false. A reporter named Joseph Ingraham, who by Caro's account was occasionally on Moses's payroll, remembered asking Moses's aide George Spargo about it in a Bronx bar while they waited out a rainstorm at a ribbon-cutting. Spargo said a relative of the borough president owned a piece of property that the other route would have taken, and Moses had promised it would not be touched. Residents heard another version, that the depot was the issue, with hidden interests in the transit company held by borough politicians. Caro found no evidence either way, and says that in the Bronx the ownership of property by politicians was habitually concealed through intermediaries, so his failure is not conclusive. What he is sure of is the arithmetic. For whatever reason, Moses chose to tear down one hundred and fifty-nine buildings housing fifteen hundred and thirty families instead of six buildings housing nineteen families and a terminal.

Moses's own summary, years later, was that the opposition had been a political thing that stirred up the animals, that Jim Lyons, the borough president, did not know which way to turn, and that he just stood pat.

This is the part-one machinery used on the other side of the table. The board voted unanimously because the question had been turned into an engineers' question. The anonymous engineer's study, which met every standard, was on the table, and the vote was taken as if it were not.

Chapter Three: What the Road Did

The road itself was an extraordinary piece of engineering, and Caro gives it room because he does not want the reader to think the man was merely cruel. General Thomas Farrell, who had built the Burma Road and was consulting for Moses, stood on a bluff and saw a wall of apartment houses that went on for seven miles. The road had to dive under the Grand Concourse, through a ridge of hard rock that held a subway line, while water mains and transit lines stayed in service. Its designer, Ernie Clark, said they took the rock out from under the girders with a teaspoon. Then Caro turns to the second half of the thought. A seven-mile road through a built-up borough is a feat of arranging the ground. It is also seven miles of people.

By the time the one mile through East Tremont was finished, in 1960, the neighborhood had mostly gone. The construction years were long, and families stayed on through terrible winters in half-empty buildings because the alternatives were worse. Then the noise. Caro describes what you hear if you sit beside the cut for five days. The big diesel trucks, slowed by traffic, hit each incline and had to downshift and grind their way up, so that the sound came out of the trench between brick walls as out of an echo chamber. People who lived within a block called it unbearable, and one said you should hear those trucks at four in the morning.

And then the part that is not engineering. The community's leaders told the young couples to hold on for another year or two, until new housing was available. After a while, one of them said, they could not keep telling them to wait, and the couples no longer believed it. A man Caro quotes, Ed Korn, said it was not only that good housing stock had been torn down. It was the manner. People woke up one morning to find that Moses had announced something and there was nothing they could do. They had fought and learned that you could not do anything against him, and what the neighborhood lost after that was the sense that anyone was listening.

A woman named Cele Sherman said that to her East Tremont was friends. One Rosh Hashanah she walked down Clinton Avenue to Southern Boulevard, crossed over and walked back, and did not meet one person she could say hello to. What was the sense of staying? She and her husband left too. By 1965, Caro says, the apartment houses that had been so precious were gutted hulks, with the pipes ripped out and sold for scrap and the doors gone, and he describes the lobbies at a length I will not reproduce.

The causal chain Caro draws runs from the route to the manner of the taking, to the loss of the sense that anyone listened, to the departure of the young couples, to the blight, and each link depends on the recollections of people who were there. It is vivid and plausible, and it is a chain of testimony with no denominators. How many of the departures would have happened anyway? Caro himself mentions the middle-income housing programs of the mid-1960s as what gave couples like the Shermans somewhere to go. A skeptical reader would want a comparison neighborhood with no road through it, and Caro does not supply one. I do not think the story is wrong. I only want to say what kind of evidence is carrying it.

Chapter Four: The Count

East Tremont is one neighborhood. For the highways alone, Caro writes, Moses put two hundred and fifty thousand people out of their homes. Add Lincoln Center and the United Nations and the university campuses and a dozen big urban renewal projects, and the figure is almost certainly close to half a million. He says no accurate total exists. The one detailed study by an outside agency counted three hundred and twenty thousand in the ten years from 1946 to 1956.

A disproportionate share were black and Puerto Rican and poor, and the housing built to replace what was torn down was mostly for the better off. So the dispossessed went into the already crowded slums, or into borderline areas that turned into slums as they arrived, and the slum clearance programs, he says, created new slums about as fast as they cleared the old.

How did the city let that go on? The answer is a story about statistics. The sites were urban renewal projects under Title I of the federal Housing Act of 1949, which let a city condemn whole districts, evict the tenants and sell the cleared land to private developers at a loss the taxpayers covered. The federal rules required that families be relocated into decent, safe and sanitary housing. Moses, as chairman of the Mayor's Slum Clearance Committee, issued the numbers showing the rules were being met, in a stream of glossy brochures.

A man named Lawrence Orton, a veteran of the City Planning Commission, read the brochure for one of the early sites, called Manhattantown, six square blocks of old apartment houses on the Upper West Side. The brochure said that of three thousand six hundred and twenty-eight families on the site, nine hundred and ninety-eight would be able to pay the rents in the new development. Orton did not believe it, though he could not prove it without a study. The part he could check was the promise about public housing. Families who could not afford the new apartments, Moses said, would get first priority in the city's public housing, where vacancies would be waiting. Orton knew public housing was already full. He looked at what was scheduled to be built in the next year or two and found it was a small fraction of the number of families Moses said would move in. Then he saw how the figure had been produced. With every project, Orton told Caro, Moses would say not to worry about the people living there, because there was always room in public housing. But the same vacancies that were alleged to be available for one project had already been allocated to a previous one, or to ten previous ones.

It is not a lie about any one project. Each brochure, read alone, is a plausible statement that a certain number of apartments will be free. The falsehood exists only in the sum, and the sum was something nobody in the process was assigned to compute. The press printed each brochure on its own. The planning commission saw each project in its turn. And nobody lined the promises up against the single stock of apartments that backed all of them.

When someone finally went and asked the families, the numbers fell apart. The people being evicted from Manhattantown were paying an average of ten dollars a month per room. The new rents were going to be about thirty-four dollars a room, roughly a hundred dollars for a small three-room apartment. Of four hundred families interviewed, exactly one said it could pay that. Nearly three hundred wanted public housing, and when the Women's City Club checked three years later, only fifty had got in. A study of relocation on the sites was finally released. A week later, Hortense Gabel went before the Board of Estimate for seventeen civic groups and asked it to read the study before approving another Title I project, at Washington Square. Moses called the study bunk, and the board approved the project.

Caro's explanation for why the press never took up the story connects to the first half of the book. The fate of poor people had never been news in New York, and housing was a field whose complications discouraged reporters. Beyond that, he says, Moses's legend draped over Title I a comforting cloak. If the official report said there was nothing wrong, the press, almost obligated to carry it, saw no reason to dig.

So the paperwork power that part one traced to the 1924 bill and the Triborough covenants has a second form here: control of the numbers a city uses to decide what it is doing. A draft sets defaults, and a statistic sets what everyone believes has been checked.

Orton's check is portable. When a plan promises to put people or cases or work into a capacity, ask what else has been promised the same capacity. Suppose a department head tells five research teams that each can have a half-time data manager from a pool of two, and every team, reading its own letter, thinks the staffing is settled. Nobody lied in any letter. The promises add up to two and a half full-time jobs against two people, and the shortfall appears only on the day somebody adds the letters up. The numbers are made up to show the shape, but the sum is all there is to the test: count the promises against the stock, not one promise at a time.

Chapter Five: Eleven Feet

The people in the road's path were not the only ones who paid. Caro's argument is that the roads were built so as to make the city's transit problem worse, and he rests it on decisions that each looked small at the time.

The first is the Bronx-Whitestone Bridge, which opened in 1939 three months ahead of schedule. The Regional Plan Association, a good-government group that had backed Moses's earlier proposals, said it should not be built unless it made provision for railroad tracks. The tracks did not have to go in at once. The bridge could be built strong enough to carry a second deck later, at a small increase in cost, and if it were not, a whole new bridge would be needed, which might make a rail link too expensive ever to be built. Moses refused even to consider it.

The second is the Long Island Expressway. Caro gives the arithmetic the regional planners made in the 1950s. In 1955 there were ninety thousand drivers commuting from Nassau and Suffolk counties and from Queens, and the existing highways could not handle them. By 1985 there would be another ninety thousand. A lane of expressway, in the best conditions, carries fifteen hundred cars an hour. The new road would have three lanes in each direction, so its maximum rush-hour capacity was forty-five hundred cars against a need of ninety thousand. A dozen Long Island Expressways, Caro writes, would not make a noticeable dent.

What would make a dent was transit on the median, which the planners reckoned could carry forty thousand passengers an hour. Even a lane reserved for buses would carry about thirteen times the people of a normal lane. Lee Koppelman, who ran the Nassau-Suffolk Regional Planning Board, tells Caro that the planners had been screaming for years about mass transportation, and that without it the road was guaranteed to fail before it was built.

And then Koppelman's own yardstick. He believed that once Moses was gone, buses would be allowed on all of Long Island's highways, including two hundred miles of parkways. Then, driving the old Wantagh Parkway one day, he noticed that a bridge over it looked low. He pulled over and measured the clearance with his arm at the curb, found a store, bought a yardstick, and measured the next one. Eleven feet. A bus was about twelve, and a practical operator would not run buses on a road with less than fourteen feet at the curb, because a single blocked lane would trap every bus at the next overpass. He went back to his office and checked the drawings for the parkways being rebuilt. The new bridges were higher at the center, because Moses wanted curved overpasses, and the same at the curb.

Caro then lets Sid Shapiro, who worked for Moses, explain, with a smile Caro describes as quiet. Moses had told his designers to build the bridges so the clearance would work for passenger cars and nothing else, because after he was gone someone would try to put buses on the parkways. He had written legislation barring buses and commercial vehicles, but you could change a law, and you could not change a bridge once it was up. Shapiro recalled a bus that strayed onto the Grand Central Parkway and had its roof rolled back like a sardine can.

Caro's reading is that the parkways were designed for a sparse, wealthy Long Island, and that by the time of the bridges the vision no longer matched anything. By 1967, engineers measuring the first five miles of the Southern State Parkway found that rush-hour traffic never for long got above sixteen miles an hour, and sometimes ran as low as three. Yet Caro also records that Shapiro overstated the case. A bridge can be rebuilt, and when five miles of the Northern State Parkway were rebuilt in 1969, they got fourteen-foot clearance, because that made the job eligible for federal money.

I should say what I remember of the dispute that grew up around this. It is from memory, not from text I held, and it is the one I deferred at the end of part one. The political theorist Langdon Winner used the overpasses in a famous essay in 1980 as his example of an object with politics built in. A later scholar of technology, Bernward Joerges, argued that the story rested on very little. As I recall the objections, they were that it rests on the recollections of two men years afterward, and that buses were already barred from the parkways by statute, so the bridges were not needed to do that work. What the book itself shows is narrower: a planner, a yardstick, a drawing, and an engineer's statement about intent. It does not show Moses saying it. Read as a claim that Moses designed the bridges to exclude buses, the evidence is one man's memory of another man's remark. Read as a claim that, whatever he intended, the roads were built so that buses could not use them, the evidence is the yardstick.

Chapter Six: The Other Side of the Park

Now to the counter-theory, and it did not come out of Caro. The Power Broker, published in 1974, cites Jane Jacobs's book only in its notes, which I checked, and never discusses her in the text. Her Death and Life of Great American Cities came out in 1961, and it is the other side of the East Tremont story, written about a neighborhood that had been defended.

The defended neighborhood was Greenwich Village, and the object was Washington Square Park. Jacobs tells the history in a chapter on what she calls the erosion of cities, the slow trading of streets and parks for room for cars. In the 1930s, she writes, Moses as Parks Commissioner tried to remove the road that ran through the park, but only in exchange for trimming the park's sides to widen the streets around it into a high-speed ring. It was known locally as the bathmat plan, describing what would be left of the park, and it was fought and defeated. In the mid-1950s he came back with a depressed highway cutting through the center of the park, to carry traffic between midtown and an expressway he was planning to the south, part of a plan she calls his Radiant City, a vision borrowed from Le Corbusier of towers set in open land, served by freeways.

At first, the community opposed the highway, expecting a stalemate. Then two women, Shirley Hayes and Edith Lyons, took what Jacobs calls a remarkable intellectual step. They proposed to close the park to cars entirely, without widening the streets around it to make up for it. A road closed and nothing built to replace it. The city's planning commission turned them down and approved a minimum roadway instead. The traffic commissioner forecast an immediate increase of millions of cars a year on the nearby streets. Moses predicted that the citizens would be back begging him to reopen the road and build the highway, and the mess they were in would serve them right. The community used what Jacobs calls rather tough political pressure and got the road closed, first on trial and then permanently.

The traffic counts afterwards showed no increase around the perimeter. Most showed a slight reduction. Bus running times, which are sensitive to total traffic, did not change. The millions of cars the commissioner had forecast had not gone anywhere else. Jacobs's explanation is a principle she calls the attrition of automobiles. A city has no set quantity of car trips, any more than of bus riders. The number varies with the relative speed and convenience of the ways of getting around, so that making a road easier produces more cars, and making it harder produces fewer, and she gives the second process, deliberately pursued, as the only realistic way to reduce the number of vehicles. She is, however, upfront about a gap. The commissioner, who had the means to count origins and destinations, appeared uninterested in finding out where the vanished cars went, and Jacobs does not know either. She says only that they did not show up in the places the forecast said they would.

Her case consists of one park, a handful of counts, and a forecast that failed. That is not nothing: a forecast said the sky would fall, the thing was done anyway, and the sky stayed where it was. But a trialist would want the missing comparison, the trips that did not happen, and she says herself that nobody measured them. Later transport economics took up the question with better data, and as I remember it, an American study of metropolitan areas around 2011 found that vehicle travel rose roughly in step with road capacity. I did not read it for this episode, so take it as a pointer, not a citation. The direction is the one her principle predicts. The East Tremont chapter and the Washington Square chapter are the same experiment run in opposite directions, with the same man holding the hypothesis.

The rest of her argument is about why the places she defended worked, and it reads like a diagnosis of East Tremont. On the street, she says, a city that has to handle strangers needs three things: a clear line between public and private space, buildings turned to face the street, and the sidewalk in fairly continuous use, so that there are always eyes upon the street. Beyond the street, four conditions generate the diversity a district needs: more than one primary use, short blocks, a mixture of old and new buildings, and a dense concentration of people. Now set Cele Sherman's walk down Clinton Avenue against that list. A cut with walls on both sides is a blank side. A neighborhood whose young couples have gone is a street without continuous users. The mechanism of loss is the one Jacobs described, and she needed no word like blight to explain it. People were the safety, the safety was the street, and the street was not a feature of anyone's plans.

This is the idea from the two episodes on Seeing Like a State, where Jacobs appeared as the witness for what a plan cannot see, and Scott named the knowledge mētis, the local, practical knowledge of how a particular place works. The state simplifies the neighborhood so it can see it, the simplification says tenements and slums, and the people in the foyers know it is false. What Jacobs adds is that the knowledge has an economic basis a planner could learn if he looked. The last part of her book says that a city poses a problem of organized complexity, which in my reading means the parts depend on each other so closely that you cannot change one without changing the others.

One more thing in Jacobs belongs to this series. In the chapter on the districts a city needs, she writes that there are only two ultimate public powers in shaping American cities, votes and control of the money, and that Moses, whose genius at getting things done largely consisted in understanding this, had made an art of using control of public money to get his way with those whom the voters elect. That is part one's thesis from the opposite bank, thirteen years before Caro published. Her remedy follows from it: where the voters are fragmented into small units, public money can negate them. By her figures a district of thirty thousand in New York amounts to nothing, and Greenwich Village, with about eighty thousand residents, was on the small side but viable. East Tremont had a committee, a leader, an engineer and a written promise from a candidate, and the promise did not hold. I would not stretch the comparison further than the two books stretch it, because the fights differed in the issue, the decade and the mayor's stake.

Chapter Seven: The Case Against Both

Moses's defenders have a serious case, and it begins from a fact Caro does not deny. New York in the 1940s and 1950s was a city whose population was moving outward, whose roads were not built for what was coming, and whose public life was in the hands of men who could not build anything at all. Moses built. He built the pools and the parks and the parkways that part one counted, and the roads that people did drive on. The case, in short, is that cities need someone with the authority to put a road through the middle of what exists, and that the displaced were a cost of something the region needed.

The objection that bothers me most is that Caro's villain is also his organizing principle. A book of this length, about a figure of this size, wants a single cause, and the cause is character: a man who stopped caring about the people the projects were for and began to care about the power the projects could give him. Historians of the revisionist school, most prominently in a large exhibition and catalog around 2007, argued that Moses was more constrained than Caro suggests, that the federal government and the engineering profession and the postwar consensus were pushing the same way, and that a different man would have built similar roads. If so, the claim that the expressways were Moses's personal choice is overstated.

Caro's evidence has a response to some of this. The Bronx-Whitestone refusal in 1939 predates the federal highway program, and the East Tremont route was chosen by Moses's office over a study that showed a cheaper one. The strongest form of the personal-choice claim is not that Moses invented the postwar road consensus. It is that where the consensus left him a choice, he took the one that cost the fewest of his own powers.

Jacobs has her critics too, and I know them second-hand. Lewis Mumford answered her book in the New Yorker with a review whose title mocked her remedies as homely, and the sneer carried an objection: that she had mistaken one kind of neighborhood for the general case and had no theory of the region. Later critics have added that the vitality she protected drew in people who could pay for it, and the Village became too expensive for those who made it lively. What held up best in her argument is the least dramatic part: predictions of catastrophe from changing a road should be tested against counts.

Here is how I would weigh the two books. Caro is right about what was done, and about how, because the how is documented in the actions of a board and a bureau and a brochure. He is on less certain ground about why, and about what followed from what, because the causal links run through testimony. Jacobs is right about what a street needs, but her policy conclusions came from fewer cases than she makes them sound. Caro tells you how a plan beats a place. Jacobs tells you what the plan did not know about the place. Together they are worth more than either one's conclusions.

For the reading, The Power Broker runs well over a thousand pages, and part one covered the rise. For this half, the East Tremont chapters, the chapter called The Meat Ax, and the Title I relocation sections can be read without the rest. Of Jacobs, the opening chapters on sidewalks and the chapter on erosion and attrition. One afternoon each is the dose.

Chapter Eight: The Trustee

Part one ended with the covenants. In the late 1930s the Triborough Authority's bond contracts made Moses's position an irrevocable contract with the people who bought the bonds, so that no governor or mayor could take his power without breaking a contract the courts would enforce. That is why he was beyond reach for thirty years, and why his fall is the neatest part of the book. Caro takes it in two moves.

The first move is Moses's own. For three decades he had won fights with one weapon: he threatened to resign. Governors and mayors who feared what the public would say about losing him gave way. He used it on Nelson Rockefeller over the terms of some of his state posts. Rockefeller, in Moses's account of the meeting, held up the paper that would extend the term in one hand and told Moses that now was the time for his brother Laurance to take over the Council of Parks. Moses said it was out of the question, and wrote a letter that was, in Shapiro's words, not exactly a resignation if you looked at the wording, with a tentative here and a perhaps there. The next day Rockefeller accepted the resignations from the park posts and hoped he would stay on at the Power Authority. After thirty years of issuing that challenge, Caro writes, he had issued it to a man who would take him up on it.

The second move is structural. Moses's strength, Caro argues, had never been only the size of the pie but the number of pieces. He held twelve public jobs at once, state and city, with chairmanships whose six-year terms were staggered, so that no governor could remove him except over a period of years. Each post propped up the others, and by resigning in anger from the state posts he had knocked the props out himself. What remained was Triborough, with about thirty million dollars a year and no other jobs to hand out. People who choose sides by what a man can give them could see he had one thing left to give.

Even so, what remained was safe, because it rested on the covenants. Caro puts it in nearly the language of part one: the contracts were sacred under law, and not even a governor backed by the legislature could break them, because bondholders could sue and the courts would uphold them. But no individual bondholder would sue, since the cost would be out of proportion to the injury, and a lone suit looks to a court like an admission that only that holder was hurt. So the contracts had appointed an agent to sue on behalf of all the bondholders. A trustee.

The trustee was the Chase Manhattan Bank, and Chase was the only large bank in the country still controlled by a single family. The Rockefellers'.

The rest goes quickly. In 1967 Rockefeller proposed merging the region's transit agencies, including the Triborough Authority, into one Metropolitan Transportation Authority headed by William Ronan. In June of that year Chase Manhattan filed a thirty-six-count complaint against the merger, with Thomas Dewey as its lawyer. It said the merger would cause irreparable injury to the bondholders, and that the aims of Triborough and of the transit systems were in basic conflict, since Triborough lived on car traffic and the subways and railroads had to draw riders away from it. The argument was never tested. On the ninth of February, 1968, the Governor and his brother David, the bank's president, met at the Governor's townhouse for fifty minutes. They signed a three-page stipulation, drawn up beforehand by their lawyers, that dropped the bank's opposition. It was taken to the judge who would have heard the case and sealed. The Triborough board with Moses as chairman was replaced by the MTA board with Ronan as chairman.
