Cholera and Childbed Fever (Snow and Semmelweis): two accidents of administration that proved a cause

Vienna's two maternity clinics in 1847 and London's two water companies in 1854 each split a population by an accident nobody chose. Here is how Semmelweis and Snow turned those accidents into proof, what each design could not show, and why belief came late for both.

19 October 2026

Chapter One: The Two Doors

In 1846, if you were a poor woman in Vienna about to give birth, there were two doors you could go through, and the choice was made for you by the day of the week. The General Hospital ran two maternity clinics under one roof. The first trained doctors. The second trained midwives. Admissions alternated between them every twenty-four hours, starting at four in the afternoon, except that the first clinic took a double shift once a week, so it ended up with four admission days to the second clinic's three.

The women knew which door was which. The man who has left us the numbers, a young assistant physician named Ignaz Semmelweis, describes women on their knees, wringing their hands, begging to be discharged rather than sent to the first clinic. That year four hundred and fifty-nine of the four thousand and ten mothers admitted to the first ward were dead within days. In the second, the toll was one hundred and five of nearly thirty-eight hundred. In percentages, 11.4 against 2.8. Over six years, he says, the first lost about three times as many women as the second, and in 1846 close to five times. The cause of death was childbed fever, an infection that began in the womb and spread through the abdomen and the blood, and that took women who had delivered healthy babies a few days earlier.

Everyone in the building could see the gap. What nobody could do was explain it, and the way Semmelweis tells the story, in a book he published in 1861 called The Etiology, the Concept and the Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever, the explanations come on stage one at a time and are each knocked down by a test. Those tests are the whole method in miniature, years before anyone had a name for it.

The first explanation was the one the medical world of the day reached for: epidemic influences. Atmospheric, cosmic and telluric changes, he says, the phrase everyone used, moving over whole regions and settling on women made vulnerable by childbirth. His reply is the question of a man who counts. If the air of Vienna was poisoned, why did it select the women in one wing of one building and leave the women in the other wing, delivering the same week into the same air? And why did women who gave birth in the street, on the way to the hospital, and then came in with the baby in an apron, fall ill far less often than the women who delivered on the ward? Those street births, he notes, were supposed to be the unfavourable case, and they did better.

An explanation that cannot be acted on is also a poor one, and Semmelweis makes that point with some relish. The commissions that investigated the first clinic called its mortality an epidemic, and then proposed remedies for it. You cannot remedy the cosmic and telluric conditions of a city. The word epidemic, he says, was being used to mean a large number of deaths rather than a cause, and that confusion was why the true cause went unfound for so long.

The next explanations were tried rather than argued. Delivery position first: the first clinic used the back, the second the side, so Semmelweis had that ward switch to the side, purely because that was what the better ward did, and nothing changed. The priest. When a woman on that ward was dying, the priest bringing the last sacrament had to walk through five other wards to reach the sickroom, ringing a small bell, and Semmelweis writes that the sound unnerved even him; he appealed to the priests' humanity and they agreed to go by a side route without the bell. The gap stayed where it was. Then there was the theory adopted by a commission at the end of 1846: the doctors' students, many of them foreigners, examined the women too roughly, and the injury caused the fever. The number of students was cut from forty-two to twenty, foreigners were mostly excluded, and examinations were reduced to a minimum.

The deaths did fall, for four months. Then in April fifty-seven women died, and in May thirty-six more. The rough-student theory had looked confirmed for exactly as long as it takes a number to wander downward by chance, and it was refuted by the same series that seemed to support it. If Semmelweis had stopped at the dip in the winter, he would have published a success.

When a falling number is the only evidence for an intervention, the number has to keep falling, and has to fall against something that did not get the intervention. Semmelweis had that something, because the second clinic ran alongside the first, in the same city, drawing on the same women, in a hospital with one kitchen. The gap itself was the control. What he lacked was a hypothesis that could explain why the gap existed and then predict a way to close it.

Chapter Two: The Death of a Pathologist

The hypothesis came from a funeral. In early March 1847 Semmelweis went to Venice for a holiday, to recover, he says, from what the maternity hospital had done to his spirits. He came back on the twentieth to take up his post again and learned that a professor he admired, Jakob Kolletschka, who taught forensic medicine and did autopsies with his students, had died. A student's scalpel had cut his finger during a dissection. The wound inflamed, the inflammation ran up the arm through the lymph vessels and veins, and he died of an infection that spread to the lining of the lungs, the heart, the abdomen and the brain. A few days before the end an abscess had formed in one eye.

Semmelweis read this the way he must have read hundreds of post-mortem reports on women: the same organs, the same membranes, the same abscesses in distant places. He writes that the image of Kolletschka's illness pursued him day and night, and that he could not stop seeing it as the same disease he had watched kill women in the first clinic. The cause in the professor's case was known. The cut had been contaminated with material from a corpse. So if the diseases were identical, the cause had to be identical, and the question to ask about the maternity ward was whether corpse material was getting into the women.

It was, and the route was the examining hand. The doctors and students of the first clinic came to the labour room directly from the dissecting room. Soap and water did not remove the smell of the dead from their hands, and for Semmelweis the smell that lingered was proof that something else lingered with it. The midwives, who trained on the second clinic, were not taught by dissection. That was the difference between the doors.

Every earlier explanation had been about the women, or the air, or the building. This one was about the examiner, and it did something the others did not do: it predicted. If the contamination came from the hands, a chemical that destroyed organic decay on the hands should bring the first clinic's mortality down to the second clinic's. From about the middle of May 1847 every student and doctor had to wash in a solution of chlorinated liquid before touching a patient. Within weeks the supply changed to chloride of lime, which was cheaper.

His own tables give the result. May 1847, with the washing introduced only in the second half, still killed thirty-six of two hundred and ninety-four, or 12.2 percent. For the seven months after, from June to December, fifty-six women died among one thousand eight hundred and forty-one, 3.0 percent. Over the year as a whole the first clinic lost one hundred and seventy-six women, a little over five percent of those admitted, and the second thirty-two, a little under one percent. In 1848, with a full year of washing, the tolls were forty-five and forty-three, and each ward came to 1.3 percent. The gap had closed. In March and in August of 1848, he writes, not one woman died in the first clinic.

Look at what the design did and did not do. It was not a randomised trial; nothing was drawn from a hat. It was a before and after with a concurrent comparison group, and the comparison group did most of the work. The first clinic's mortality fell at the moment of the change, and the second clinic's, which got no change, stayed where it had been. A secular drift in the city could not explain the first without disturbing the second. And the assignment of the women, by the day they happened to arrive, was close enough to arbitrary that it is hard to imagine a rule linking the day of the week to a woman's susceptibility to fever. The day schedule is, in modern words, a natural experiment: an allocation that was made for administrative convenience by someone who had no interest in the outcome, and that happens to divide a population in two.

The worked example sits in the autumn. After the first months of success the rate crept up again. In September, October and November, thirty-four women died among seven hundred and eighty-six. Semmelweis describes what he found. In October a woman with a rotting cancer of the uterus had been admitted to the bed where the morning round began. The examiners washed their hands with soap after her, not with the chloride solution, because they thought the chemical was only needed to remove the corpse particles and that a hand, once cleaned, could not be contaminated again. Of the twelve women delivered in the following period, eleven died. In November a woman with a decaying knee joint was admitted, and although her genitals were healthy, the stench of the rot filled the room; and nearly all her neighbours died, eleven women in November and eight in December by his count.

From these two disasters he extended the rule. The agent was not specifically cadaveric. It was any decomposing organic matter, whether from the dead or the living, carried on the hand or, in the knee case, he thought, through the air. So the chlorine wash had to be used between one patient and the next, as well as on entering the ward.

A man who had an ideology would have explained away the autumn. He treated it as an experiment that went wrong in an informative way, revised the rule, and the mortality fell again. A theory that is only ever confirmed is compatible with anything. This one had been made to fail in public, with the dead counted, and the failure fixed the dose.

Whether the revised theory was right is another matter. He thought the agent was putrid animal-organic matter, which was a decomposition chemistry of the day. We would say bacteria, and he would have had no way to see them. That limits what he can say about mechanism, but not about the intervention, which is a separate question, and which worked. The comparison is to scurvy, where the right treatment was found and lost for lack of a mechanism that could defend it. Semmelweis had the opposite trouble: he had a mechanism he could state, and it was wrong in detail, and the right action was attached to it.

Chapter Three: Five Hundred Deaths in Ten Days

Seven years later and a thousand miles to the west, a London physician was working on the same kind of problem with a different disease. John Snow was an anaesthetist. He gave chloroform to the Queen in childbirth, and he is better known now for the street in Soho where he stopped an epidemic. He had written in 1849, in the first edition of a pamphlet, that cholera was caused by a poison that entered the body by mouth, multiplied in the intestine, and left in the evacuations, and that it spread when the evacuations of one person got into the drinking water or onto the hands of another. The prevailing view was that cholera was carried by foul air, a miasma rising from filth. Snow thought the miasma view could not explain why cholera began in the gut instead of the lungs. It was an argument from the facts of the illness.

In August and September of 1854 a catastrophe arrived on his doorstep. The Broad Street outbreak, in the streets around Golden Square, began in the night between the thirty-first of August and the first of September. In his second edition, which he published the next year as On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, he writes that within two hundred and fifty yards of the corner of Cambridge Street and Broad Street there were upwards of five hundred fatal attacks in ten days. The mortality in that small area, he says, probably equalled anything caused in this country even by the plague, and it was much more sudden. Most of the population fled. In less than six days the worst streets had lost more than three quarters of their inhabitants.

He suspected the water of the much-used street pump in Broad Street almost at once, and then talked himself out of it for a few days. On the evening of the third of September he examined the water and found so little organic impurity that he hesitated to conclude anything. What turned him was the pattern of the deaths. He asked the General Register Office for the list of cholera deaths registered in the three local sub-districts in the week ending the second of September. There were eighty-nine. Six had occurred early in the week, and the rest, he decided, belonged to the outbreak. He went door to door for eighty-three of them.

Eighty-three deaths, and what the families said. Sixty-one drank the Broad Street water, constantly or occasionally. Six did not, by their families' account. For six he could learn nothing, because everyone connected to them was dead or gone. The last ten had lived nearer to some other pump, and the reasons he found for them read like a ledger: five families preferred the Broad Street water and sent for it anyway, three were children who went to school beside the pump, and two deaths belonged to the cholera already in the district before the explosion.

The cases that matter most are the ones that should not have happened under the miasma theory and did not happen to people who avoided the pump. The Poland Street workhouse stood nearly surrounded on three sides by houses where people were dying. It held five hundred and thirty-five inmates, and five of them died of cholera. It had its own well. A brewery in Broad Street employed more than seventy men, and none of them suffered, or at most two were unwell. The proprietor told Snow the men drank malt liquor allowance and did not touch water, and there was a deep well on the premises as well. At the percussion-cap factory a few doors down, with about two hundred workers and two tubs of Broad Street water kept on the premises for anyone who wished a drink, eighteen died. Then the pair that Snow, citing the physician who brought them to him, thought the most conclusive of all: a widow in Hampstead, miles from the epidemic, who had not been near Broad Street for months but liked the taste of its water, so that a cart brought a large bottle to her every day. She drank from the bottle on the thirty-first of August and the first of September, and died within two days. A niece who had visited, and drunk some of it, went home to a healthy part of Islington and died too. There was no cholera at the time at either place.

This is a different kind of evidence from Vienna's. It is a case series arranged so that the exceptions become the test. If the pump was the cause, then the people who should be spared are those who had no access to it even when they lived beside it, and those who should die are the ones who lived far away and drank it. Both predictions came true, in cases small enough that you could write them out in a paragraph each. Snow wanted that: the worked case run to its ending, with the particular woman and the particular bottle.

On the eighth of September, he writes, the handle of the pump was removed, after he put his findings before the parish board of guardians. In the book he gives the daily attacks: a hundred and forty-three on the first, a hundred and sixteen on the second, fifty-four on the third, falling further each day, twelve on the eighth, one on the twelfth. And he adds that the attacks had so far diminished before the use of the water was stopped that it was impossible to decide whether the well still contained the poison. Take the pump handle story, which is told as the moment the epidemic ended, and set it beside what Snow himself wrote. He did not claim that removing the handle ended anything. He said the data could not distinguish it from an epidemic that was already burning out, helped by the flight of three quarters of the population. A man trying to win an argument would not have written that sentence. It sits in the middle of a paragraph about something else.

The question it leaves is the one an honest skeptic would ask. A dense cluster of deaths around a pump shows that the pump is near the deaths. Snow answered with the drinkers and the abstainers, but the skeptic can say that the households which said they drank the water were asked after the fact, by a man who already had a theory, in the aftermath of a catastrophe. What would an unambiguous test look like? It would need a population divided into two groups, similar in every other way, one drinking the suspect water and one not. Snow had been waiting for one for five years.

Chapter Four: The Grandest Scale

The two groups were already living in the south of London, in streets that looked from the outside like any others. Two water companies served the area, Southwark and Vauxhall, and Lambeth. Both drew from the Thames. In 1852 Lambeth moved its works up the river to Thames Ditton, beyond the reach of London's sewage, while its rival went on drawing from the polluted stretch near the city. In 1849 the customers of both had drunk the same river. By the next outbreak they did not.

What made the situation extraordinary is how the pipes were laid. The two companies had been in competition for customers, and each had laid mains down nearly every street, courts and alleys included. A house took water from one company or the other according to the decision of the owner or occupier at the time of the competition, and Snow says that in many cases a single house had a supply different from the houses on both sides. Each company served rich and poor, large houses and small. So neighbours with the same income, the same landlord class, the same drains and the same air got different water. In his words the experiment was on the grandest scale: three hundred thousand people of both sexes and every age and rank were divided into two groups without their choice and, in most cases, without their knowledge.

A design like this earns the title natural experiment. The exposure was assigned by something with no plausible connection to the outcome, the commercial rivalry of two firms years earlier. Nobody sorted these households by susceptibility. An investigator designing the study could hardly have arranged it better.

The task left was labour. The Registrar-General records gave addresses of cholera deaths, but not the water company. Snow started with two sub-districts in Lambeth in the middle of August and found that of forty-four deaths, thirty-eight were in houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall, four by Lambeth, and two had their own pump wells. He told William Farr, the statistician at the Registrar-General's office, who was impressed, and Farr had the registrars of the south districts record the water supply of every cholera death after the twenty-sixth of August. For the weeks before that, Snow went to the houses himself. Tenants rarely knew which company they paid, because landlords paid the rates, so he used a chemical test. The two companies' water differed enormously in salt, a trace of the sewage. Silver nitrate clouded the Lambeth water faintly and the Southwark and Vauxhall water heavily; by his figures the first held under one grain of common salt per gallon and the second about thirty-eight. If a resident could not say, he took a phial of the tap water home and tested it that evening. A medical man, John Whiting, did the same in Bermondsey and Rotherhithe.

The first four weeks of the epidemic, to the fifth of August, produced three hundred and thirty-four deaths in the districts served by either company. In two hundred and eighty-six, the house was supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall. In fourteen it was supplied by Lambeth. The remainder were the people who dipped pails straight into the Thames or the tidal ditches, the few who used pump wells, and four whose source was unknown. The Parliament had returns for how many houses each company supplied: forty thousand and forty-six for Southwark and Vauxhall, twenty-six thousand, one hundred and seven for Lambeth. So the rate per ten thousand houses was seventy-one against five. Fourteen to one.

Over the first seven weeks the table widens to three rows, and the third row is what makes it an argument rather than a statistic. Southwark and Vauxhall houses: one thousand, two hundred and sixty-three deaths, three hundred and fifteen for every ten thousand houses. Lambeth houses: ninety-eight deaths, thirty-seven. And the rest of London, a quarter of a million houses, with fourteen hundred and twenty-two deaths, fifty-nine. The Lambeth customers lived street by street among the sufferers and still fared better than London as a whole. If the south had simply been an unhealthy district, they would have been unhealthy too. Snow reads the third row as showing that Southwark and Vauxhall's water was a harm, and that Lambeth's was no worse than the average.

The difference narrowed as the epidemic advanced, and he says why: as cholera spread, it travelled by the other routes too, among the customers of both companies. The Registrar-General's returns to the middle of October, covering the next seven weeks, gave five hundred and thirty-seven deaths in each ten thousand Southwark and Vauxhall houses against a hundred and fifteen for Lambeth. Nearly five to one. The pattern held, weaker.

Compare this to the Vienna design. In both, a population arrived at one of two exposures by an administrative rule, the day of arrival in one case and the landlord's choice of company in the other. In both, the researcher had a mechanism to guess at, and the natural experiment did what a mechanism could not: it showed that the exposure alone, with everything else equal, moved the outcome. In both, the work was the counting, house by house or bed by bed, and the arithmetic was nothing more than division. And in both, the accident of assignment is carrying an assumption nobody tested. Snow says there is no difference in the condition or occupation of the people receiving the two supplies. That is an assertion, supported by his knowledge of the streets. He did not prove it with data on income. Semmelweis says nothing in the first clinic changed except the washing, and that too is an assertion, backed by the narrative of what he saw.

Both assumptions are plausible, and plausibility is all either man had. The randomised trial, which this series is heading toward, exists to remove the dependence on a lucky accident being as good as it looks. Snow and Semmelweis had to find their experiments lying about. Anyone designing one has the luxury of making the accident on purpose, with the coin.

Chapter Five: Who Believed Whom

The question this episode was set up to answer is why one of these men was believed and the other was not. The answer I expected to give does not survive a look at the evidence.

The tempting version runs like this. Snow won because the blame fell on somebody else, a water company, whom the public could be angry at. Semmelweis lost because it fell on the physician, the very person he needed to convince. It has a good deal in it. Semmelweis's claim was that doctors were killing their patients with their hands, and a profession that has not yet been given a reason to wash is a hard audience for the news that it is the vector. The man who took over his post, Carl Braun, wrote against his view, and the book counts four hundred deaths in the first clinic in 1854, after Braun's brother had succeeded him as assistant. This is the shape of the pellagra and beriberi stories earlier on this shelf, where the proof was refused because the diagnosis indicted the doctrine of the people who had to accept it.

But the story is not true of Snow. The sources I held are Snow's own book and Semmelweis's. The history of how each was received I have only from the general account, in Nuland's biography of Semmelweis and Johnson's The Ghost Map, neither of which I could read in full text, so treat what follows as the outline rather than the detail. As I understand it, Snow was not believed either, not in his lifetime. The official inquiries into the Broad Street outbreak, as I recall, did not adopt the pump explanation, and the miasma view held in the official mind; Snow died in 1858 with it still holding. The sanitary reform that followed ran on the miasma theorists' own belief in drains and clean water, so the remedy arrived by a route that did not credit him. What settled the cause was Koch's identification of the cholera bacillus in the 1880s, long after both men were dead.

So the premise fails. They were each right early and each unbelieved, and the difference between them is not that one won the argument at the time. The difference is in the afterlife. Snow's is told as a triumph, with a map and a pump handle, because the data lent themselves to a picture and the cause he named matched what was found later. Semmelweis's comes down as a tragedy, because of what he did next.

Here the text of his book is the evidence, and it is a harder read than the numbers. The Etiology runs to close to four hundred pages. It came out fourteen years after the finding, and as far as I can tell from the book, most of what had been published in between was written by his friends. His friends did the announcing: Skoda read a paper on the discovery to the Vienna Academy of Sciences in 1848, the editor Hebra put two articles in print, and the Academy gave Semmelweis and a colleague money for animal experiments, which in the colleague's report turned out ambiguous. When Semmelweis finally wrote the book, he wrote it as a prosecution. He argues against his opponents by name. A professor, Scanzoni, had written that if childbed fever proved contagious, every state lying-in hospital would be a den of murderers. Semmelweis agrees that the sentence is true and terrible, and then charges Scanzoni with joining the guilty by opposing the washing. The book makes a legitimate argument, that a preventable disease which is not prevented is the responsibility of whoever could prevent it. It makes that argument in a tone that guarantees nobody on the receiving end will read the footnotes.

The book holds one story that shows what the finding cost the man who believed it. In December 1847 a student of Michaelis, the director of the Kiel maternity hospital, wrote to him from Vienna about the washing. Michaelis answered in March 1848. His hospital had been shut from July to November because of childbed fever, and on reopening the first three women taken in fell ill. He introduced the chlorine wash at once, and afterwards, bar one case in February, all were well. He added that the previous summer his own cousin had died of the fever after he examined her, at a time when he had been dissecting women who died of it, and that transmission had seemed certain to him ever since. Semmelweis reports that he later learned Michaelis was dead, run down by a train near Hamburg, sunk in melancholy over her death, and writes that he tells it as a monument to the man's conscience. A finding that says the hands are the vector makes every examiner a suspect, and Michaelis was the examiner who accepted the charge.

I should give the case against Semmelweis its due, because the usual telling flatters him. His own theory was loose. He started with corpse particles, widened it to any decaying matter, then to the air, and he held, according to the text, that childbed fever was not contagious in the sense others meant, while insisting it came from outside. His animal experiments were inconclusive and he did not follow them up in a way that would convince a skeptic. In his arithmetic, the 1847 improvement is real, but a critic could say that the rate in the first clinic had varied wildly for years, and that a series that moves from thirteen percent in a month to under three in another has the sort of noise in which any intervention can look good. What Semmelweis could offer in reply was the second clinic, the closing of the gap, the deaths that returned in the years after he left, and the autumn failures that taught him the rule. Each piece is a weak argument and together they are a strong one. In his time it was heard as assertion. Snow's weak points were mostly his own admissions: the pump data could not separate the handle from the decline, the equivalence of the two water-company households was a premise he could not prove, and the microscopist Hassall found nothing in the pump water that could be called the poison. He asked readers to accept a cause no one could see, on the strength of how deaths were distributed. The question both books leave a sceptic is what, in 1854 or 1861, could have been checked by someone who did not trust the author, and the answer is the tables, which is where the next paragraph goes.

What neither man could do, then, was persuade by authority, and what each did instead was to give the sceptic a table to attack. The most underrated virtue in both books is that the denominators are on the page. Snow printed the three hundred and thirty-four deaths of the first four weeks in an appendix, so that anyone who wished could verify the water supply of each. Semmelweis printed the monthly births and deaths for the first clinic, month by month, bad months included. A reader in 1861 who wanted to dispute the numbers had to dispute them in public.

Chapter Six: What the Accident Cannot Give

The two designs have a joint limitation, and the series that begins here will take eight more episodes to deal with it.

The accident has to be as good as random, and you do not get to choose it. Snow could not have arranged two water companies. If the Lambeth Company had moved its pipes into the wealthier streets, the comparison would have been poisoned, and the data would not have warned him. Semmelweis had one more accident working in his favour: the women assigned to the two clinics were assigned by the calendar, and the one place where self-selection crept in, the women who delivered at a midwife's and had themselves driven to the hospital, he caught himself, and noted that it ran against his own conclusion rather than for it. Both men found the biases that might have flattered them and said so, which is a habit not a method.

Where the exposure is not handed over by an administrative accident, you cannot do this. Most of the questions medicine now asks have no lying-about experiments. There are no two water companies with interleaved pipes for a treatment effect of ten percent; that sort of effect hides under the differences between the people who receive a drug and the people who do not. The tools the next episodes describe, randomisation and concealment of the allocation and a number set in advance, are what you build when the world does not give you a Lambeth. Fisher, next, takes up the problem of making the accident on purpose.

Routine data is full of Vienna. Two sites in a programme diverge, two cohorts run through different workflows, one clinic rolled out a change a quarter before another. The usual response is to explain the gap with whatever the person looking already believes. The Vienna response is to ask first what rule sent people to each door, and whether that rule could be linked to the outcome. If the rule is a calendar, an administrative boundary or a contract signed years earlier, the gap is a candidate experiment, and the next thing to look for is a third group, as Snow had in the rest of London, that says whether the apparently good arm is good or the apparently bad arm bad. His Lambeth customers did not merely beat the Southwark and Vauxhall customers; they beat the city as a whole, and that is why the table could say which water was the harm. Which of the gaps in a trialist's own routine data came from a rule nobody chose for its effect on the outcome, and which of them has a third group beside it?
