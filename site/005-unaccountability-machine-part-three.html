The Unaccountability Machine, Part Three: Repairing the Feedback

The final part on Dan Davies's book: what repair would actually take, the critics given their full say, the synthesis with Rao and Jackall extended, and a verdict with a dose.

24 August 2026

Chapter One: The Industry That Chose the Other Road

Two parts of diagnosis have earned one part of treatment, and the treatment discussion should start with an industry that actually took it – one worked example, run to its end, and then held up against everything this series has described. This example is mine, assembled in Davies's terms rather than taken from his book, and it is commercial aviation. On the evening of December twenty-eighth, nineteen seventy-eight, a United Airlines DC-8 approached Portland, Oregon, with a hundred and eighty-one people aboard, and a landing-gear indicator light failed to come on. The captain, prudently, entered a holding pattern to troubleshoot. Then he kept holding. For the better part of an hour the aircraft circled a city full of runways while the captain worked the gear problem, and the flight engineer's remarks about the fuel gauges grew from observations into hints into something just short of a warning – and never crossed into the sentence that mattered, because one did not say that sentence to a captain. The engines flamed out from fuel exhaustion six miles short of the airport. Ten people died in a crash that had at no point involved anything being wrong with the airplane's ability to land. The cockpit voice recorder preserved the whole slow catastrophe: a subordinate who saw the error, and deference that attenuated the alarm to a whisper. Portland was not unique; a string of accidents in those years showed the same signature, a fatal fact known inside the cockpit and unable to travel four feet to the left. The industry faced a version of the Post Office's choice. It possessed a continuous stream of feedback about its own failures, and a hierarchy configured to attenuate that feedback into silence. What it built instead has become the standard story of institutional repair, and the details are the interesting part. Crew resource management retrained the cockpit so that challenging the captain became procedure rather than insubordination – a deliberate re-wiring of who may send information upward. Confidential reporting systems let any crew member file a safety report, in the American case to NASA, an agency outside the airline entirely, with limited immunity attached – so that the fear of blame, the force that scripts every helpline, was structurally disconnected from the act of reporting. Investigations were assigned to a board whose findings cannot be used to establish liability, on the explicit theory that an investigation which can punish will be lied to. And none of it is accountability in the political sense: no one demanded a named villain per crash. It is the opposite bet: that if you want the truth about a system's failures, you must make the truth safe to tell, and that punishing individuals for systemic errors does not produce fewer errors, it produces quieter ones. Commercial aviation is now so safe that its fatal-accident rate is measured per tens of millions of flights. The industry did not get there by finding better people. It got there by re-plumbing the feedback – which is to say, aviation is what it looks like when an institution reads Beer's diagnosis and actually fills the prescription.

Chapter Two: What the Book Itself Prescribes

So the repair is possible; an entire industry is the proof. What does Davies himself prescribe? Here honesty about the book requires a change of tone, because the answer is: less than the diagnosis deserves, and the critics noticed. The book's constructive argument runs at the level of principle. Sinks should be visible: an organization ought to know where it has delegated decisions to process, and treat each such delegation as a design choice someone owns, rather than sediment. Feedback links, once severed, should be rebuilt – the person affected by a policy needs some path to a person able to change it, and the two tests from part one, who holds the override and is anyone listening downstream, are the audit questions. Institutions need their System Four back: a funded, empowered function whose job is to perceive what the operational machine is structurally unable to see, including the machine's own victims. And the objective function is a choice: a corporation wired to a single metric is not a law of nature but a nineteen-seventies design decision that can, in principle, be revised. All of this follows from the diagnosis, and all of it is stated in the book more as direction than as blueprint. Tim Harford, whose Financial Times review was among the most sympathetic the book received, put the underlying principle in one sentence that is better than a paragraph of mine: without feedback, nothing ever gets better. But here the case against begins: a direction is not a mechanism, and a diagnosis that ends in principles is exactly the kind of output that the machines this book describes have proven magnificently capable of absorbing without changing.

Chapter Three: The Case Against, at Full Strength

This series steelmans before it criticizes, and it has spent two and a half episodes steelmanning; the critics now get their innings, stated as hard as the sources state them. The first count is the one just opened: the remedy is thin. Ed Smith, in the New Statesman, having praised the diagnosis as provocative and often brilliant, observed that Davies exhumes Stafford Beer with enormous care and then gives us surprisingly little of him as an actual solution – the rescue mission recovers the body, not the working science. On this count I think the defense is partial at best. One can argue, as I did last episode, that the thinness honestly reflects the state of a defunded discipline; but a book subtitled why big systems make terrible decisions raises the expectation that it knows what better ones would look like, and mostly it gestures. Aviation shows the prescription can be filled; it is not Davies who shows it. The second count is Smith's deeper objection, and it deserves slow statement because it is the best single criticism of the book I have found. Davies's whole framework, Smith argues, is built on the wrong noun. Accountability is an audit concept – it is about the traceability of decisions after the fact, and it is zero-sum: assigning more of it to me assigns less of it to you. What institutions have actually lost, Smith contends, is responsibility, which is something else entirely: the internalized, forward-looking sense of ownership that makes a professional fix a problem no rulebook assigned to them. And responsibility, unlike accountability, is not zero-sum – a whole ward, a whole cockpit, can hold it simultaneously without dividing it. The distinction bites, because you can imagine an organization that passes every one of Davies's audits – sinks mapped, overrides named, feedback channels built – in which no human being feels responsible for anything: a perfectly instrumented machine, fully accountable, morally vacant. The re-plumbed cockpit worked, on this reading, not mainly because the channels changed but because the culture that flowed through them did. The third count is the one conceded last episode and worth logging formally: the economics chapter over-claims. The sink predates Friedman, flourishes in ministries that have never had a shareholder, and would survive his doctrine's repeal. And a fourth count, mine: the book's charm is a risk factor. The accountability sink is such a satisfying concept – it explains one's last twenty customer-service calls in three words – that it invites the identity dose this series keeps warning about: the reader who sees sinks everywhere, files every institutional encounter under a diagnosis, and mistakes the filing for understanding. The Moral Mazes episode issued the same warning about Jackall, and the failure mode here is the same one wearing systems-theory clothes: taken as identity, the sink concept teaches that resistance is a category error – the machine decided, no one can be reached, why call the helpline at all – and a person who has fully internalized that lesson has done the machine's attenuation for it, free of charge. The book's own framework says to distrust any idea that reliably produces resignation in the people who hold it; applied to itself, that test is worth running. A framework this fluent should be handled like any potent diagnostic: indicated uses, known interactions, maximum dose.

Chapter Four: The Shelf, Rewired Once More

Episode two of this feed proposed a synthesis: Davies's sink as the industrialized successor of Rao's hand-built heads-I-win-tails-you-lose schemes, with Jackall's blame hydraulics as the missing middle chapter. Three episodes of slow walking let that synthesis carry more weight now, and one extension of it. Jackall's corporation conserved blame – it flowed downhill into a designated person. Davies's machine dissolves blame – it flows into process and vanishes. What the slow walk adds is where the conserved quantity went, and the answer is Beer's: what the sink actually destroys is not blame but information. Every severed feedback link is a channel down which the organization has chosen not to know something. The Post Office did not merely avoid blame for sixteen years; it purchased, at the price of scripting one helpline, the ability not to know what seven hundred prosecutions were built on. Blame was always the decoy. The real transaction in every sink is epistemic – and that is why the repair, wherever it has actually worked, has been epistemic too: aviation did not redistribute blame better, it made knowledge cheaper than denial. Which folds Rao's cynical layer back in with a colder eye. The sociopath layer, in this vocabulary, are not the people who escape blame; they are the people who position themselves where the unattenuated information still flows, and who hold override authority they are careful never to exercise on the record. And the extension explains, at last, why Rao's Powertalk belongs in this story. Episode two called it the unattenuated channel – the private language in which the powerful exchange the full-variety information the official channels compress away. The three-part version can now say what that arrangement is: it is a private algedonic wire, built by and for the people at the top, running alongside an official network they have allowed to go deaf. The organization does not lack the capacity to know; the capacity has been privatized. Beer wanted the alarm wire to serve the whole system. The sociopath layer builds one and keeps it. Find the accountability sink, this series said in episode two, and you have found the org chart. The three-part version is sharper: find what the organization has arranged not to know, and you have found what it is actually optimizing – the purpose of a system is what it refuses to hear.

Chapter Five: The Test Applied at Home

Before the verdict, one more application, constructed rather than drawn from the book, because a framework should be tested on ground the tester knows – and the ground this listener knows best is clinical research. Run Beer's anatomy over a randomized trial, and something unexpected appears: the apparatus of trial oversight is one of the few places where modern institutions have deliberately built the machinery this book says is missing. Consider what a data safety monitoring board actually is. A trial's investigators are, in the machine's terms, System One through Three: running operations, coordinating sites, controlling quality – and structurally incapable of judging their own trial's emerging harms, not because they are dishonest but because they are attenuated by their own hopes and blinded by design. So the institution wires in an external organ: an independent board that sees the unblinded data the investigators must not see, meets on its own schedule, answers to no one on the payroll, and holds the one authority that matters – the authority to stop the machine. That is a designed sink, in exactly Harford's sense. Responsibility for the stopping decision has been deliberately taken away from the people closest to the work, and everyone can point to the charter when asked who decided. But it is a sink with its feedback intact: the board exists precisely to receive the signal – accumulating harm – that the operational layers cannot be trusted to hear, and it is welded to an override. Serious adverse event reporting is the algedonic channel from last episode, a pain signal that regulation forbids anyone to attenuate on its way up. And the machinery goes one step further, in a detail worth savoring because it answers the corruption worry from part one: the stopping rules themselves are written in advance. A trial's statistical boundary for early stopping is chosen and documented before anyone knows which arm is winning – which means the single most consequential decision the system can face has been made by people who could not yet know whom it would favor. That is a System Five acting while it is still impartial, binding its future self against its future temptations; Ulysses at the mast, rewritten as a statistical analysis plan. The apparatus is imperfect, and every researcher can name trials where the wiring failed. But the design is Beer's design, discovered independently by a profession that had watched what happens without it, and it suggests a rule the book itself never quite states: the question is not whether to build sinks – complex work will always delegate decisions to structures – but whether each sink is built with its senses on, its alarm wire uncut, and its stop authority in hands that can be found. A field that can name where its own machinery is missing those three things has, in this book's terms, already begun the repair.

Chapter Six: Verdict, Dose, and a Question for Monday

The verdict, then, on the book this series has lived inside. The Unaccountability Machine is the rare popular book whose central concept enters the language because the language had a hole where it belonged, and its second half – the Beer exhumation – is more ambitious and more flawed than its famous first: a genuinely important intellectual rescue that stops short of teaching the rescued science to work. Read it whole, but read it as a diagnosis with a bibliography, not a diagnosis with a cure. The dose: one careful read for anyone who runs anything, from a research group to a health system; a re-read of the cybernetic chapters for anyone designing an organization from scratch, because the five-systems anatomy is the closest thing to a checklist the book contains. Three kinds of reader need it most: the person newly given authority over a process they did not build, who must decide which of its sinks are load-bearing; the person on the receiving end of a machine, who needs the vocabulary to see that the wall in front of them is architecture rather than malice; and the designer of anything – a trial, a dashboard, a reporting line – who is, whether the job title says so or not, choosing right now which information the future will be unable to hear. Pair it, against its own thinness of remedy, with the aviation-safety literature, where the prescription has case law; and pair it against its own charm with Smith's correction, that a system can be perfectly accountable and no one in it responsible – carry both nouns, and ask which one an institution is actually short of, because the treatments differ: an accountability deficit is fixed with wiring, and a responsibility deficit is fixed, if it can be fixed at all, with culture, hiring, and time. And the question to carry into Monday, this series' standing custom, assembled from all three parts. When you next hit the wall – the policy that is the policy, the denial no one signed – do not begin with who is to blame, because part one showed that door is welded. Ask instead: what has this organization arranged not to know, and what would it cost anyone here to find out? If the answer is that knowing is expensive and not-knowing is free, you are not looking at a mistake. You are looking at the machine, running as built – and now you know the two points where machines like it have actually been rebuilt: make the truth safe to tell, and give someone, someone findable, the authority to act on it. Next time, the book this whole shelf descends from, the one Rao read chapter by chapter before writing a word of the Gervais Principle: William Whyte's The Organization Man. Thanks for listening.
