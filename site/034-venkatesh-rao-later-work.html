Venkatesh Rao's later work: Breaking Smart, Premium Mediocre, and the ethics of being slightly evil

A body-of-work episode on Venkatesh Rao since the two-thousand-nine Gervais Principle: the venture-capital-funded case for software eating the world, the millennial coping aesthetic he named and then argued the economy needed, the playbook for surviving a diagnosis you can't unsee, and the later argument that mediocrity might be the only honest response to all three.

07 September 2026

Chapter One: The Hackerspace

Picture someone hanging around a hackerspace on a weekday afternoon. Laptop open, code editor on the screen, the right hooded sweatshirt, the right vocabulary picked up from people who actually build things. Ask what they're working on and the answer is vague in a specific way, the way people get when the true answer is nothing yet, but a version of something is coming, probably, once the right opportunity shows up to make learning it worth the trouble. Nobody in the room is being fooled, including the person doing the sitting. Everyone there can tell the difference between somebody paying dues on the way to real skill and somebody occupying the costume of skill while waiting to see if it pays to fill it in. What's strange is that the second posture isn't treated as failure. It's treated as reasonable, almost respectable, a rational response to an economy that keeps asking people to prove commitment before it will pay for the training that commitment requires.

The writer who built a whole essay around that scene, and a term that outran the essay by years, is Venkatesh Rao, and the essay isn't even the reason this particular audio feed exists, though it's close. Long before this show had a numbering scheme or a queue, its very first outing was built on a different Rao essay entirely, an older one about office hierarchies that this show has quoted from more than any other single source over the following thirty-three episodes without ever getting to call it one of them. Tonight closes that loop: not the origin essay itself, which predates the feed and stays out of its numbering, but everything Rao wrote after it, tested against everything this show has learned reading Jackall and Whyte and Goffman and the rest in the years since he wrote it.

Chapter Two: The Pyramid Before This Show Had a Name

In two thousand nine, on a blog called Ribbonfarm that Rao had started two years earlier, he published a reading of the American version of the television show The Office that turned into a six-part theory of how organizations actually distribute power. He named it, a little indirectly, after Ricky Gervais, the British comedian whose original format the American show adapted, and the theory sorts every organization into three tiers. At the top sit Sociopaths, in Rao's specific and deliberately provocative sense: people who hold a personal code, act on it, and never bother justifying it to anyone below them. In the middle sit the Clueless, competent enough to be useful and loyal enough to believe the firm's official story about itself long after the story has stopped being true. At the bottom sit the Losers, who aren't failures in any social sense, just people who traded ambition for a steady paycheck and made their peace with the trade. Rao admits, without much embarrassment, that he lifted the three labels from a business cartoon by Hugh MacLeod and knew going in that the words wouldn't quite fit what he meant by them. He used them anyway, because inexact labels people actually remember beat exact ones nobody does.

The mechanism underneath the labels is the part that outlasted the labels: Sociopaths knowingly promote the Losers who overperform into middle management, because an overperforming Loser is a threat, while grooming the underperforming ones who show promise into new Sociopaths, without ever putting the arrangement in writing. Everyone else gets left exactly where they are. It's a theory about how a hierarchy launders its own self-interest into something that looks, from the middle, like a meritocracy. When this feed picked up its current numbering seventeen years later, its first numbered episode went looking for that same laundering mechanism inside Robert Jackall's fieldwork on real corporate bureaucracies, and found the fit uncomfortably close, all without ever getting to credit the essay that had already done the naming. What neither that essay nor this feed's own opening episode could ask, because both were written from inside the trap rather than after fifteen years of watching one writer live through it, is the question this episode can finally ask: once you've built the clearest available theory of how power launders itself, what do you actually do with it. Rao's own answer changed at least four times.

Chapter Three: A Sociopath's Own Instruction Manual

The first answer came fast, and it was personal rather than theoretical. In two thousand ten, a year after the Gervais Principle, Rao started an email newsletter called Be Slightly Evil, and kept it running for three years before folding the archive into a short book under the same title, subtitled, with a straight face, A Playbook for Sociopaths. If the Gervais Principle diagnosed the trap from outside, Be Slightly Evil is written from inside it, as tactics: how to act like one of Rao's own Sociopaths without becoming the cartoon villain the word suggests. His version of the term keeps the moral content and drops the melodrama. A sociopath, in his working definition, has a personal sense of right and wrong, takes the consequences of acting on it, and doesn't waste energy explaining the choice to people who were never going to agree with it anyway. It's Nietzsche filtered through an office memo. The list grew past two thousand two hundred subscribers before Rao retired it in two thousand thirteen and folded the archive into the short book, adding a five-thousand-word closing essay called "Inside the Tempo" that ties the newsletter's tactics explicitly back to the year-earlier book's account of timing, a capstone that treats the two projects, without quite saying so directly, as one argument written in two different registers.

The same year, Rao also published Tempo, a full-length book making a more academic version of the same case, built around a single military borrowing: the OODA loop, a decision cycle the fighter pilot and strategist John Boyd designed for aerial combat, observe, orient, decide, act, repeated fast enough that an opponent is always reacting to a situation that has already changed. Rao's contribution is to insist that beating an opponent on tempo has nothing to do with sheer speed. What matters is thinking in the right unit of time for the situation at hand rather than a generic one, since a fighter pilot's useful cycle runs in seconds while an institution's runs in fiscal quarters or budget cycles, and confusing the two units gets you either wasted urgency or missed opportunity. Underneath both books sits the same practical question, one theoretical and one operational: not just who currently holds power in a given room, but on whose clock the room is actually running, and whether you've bothered to find out before you try to move inside it.

Two ideas from the shorter book earn their keep beyond the provocation. The first is a distinction between the tragedian and the idealist: the idealist tries to change the world by improving themselves first and hoping the world notices, while the tragedian accepts an external, often unglamorous project, throws themselves at it, and lets whatever change happens to them along the way be the actual education, unplanned and unbragged-about. The second is what Rao calls the shadow self, the parts of a personality a person has disowned so completely they can only be seen projected onto other people, usually as the specific flaws that most irritate them. Predictability, in the book's account, is where the two ideas meet power directly: the sociopath who is consistent about the one or two things they'll never compromise on, and unpredictable about everything else, controls a room more thoroughly than the one who tries to be reliable about everything. As Rao puts it, in the book's own flattest line about the whole exercise: "If you don't know who the sucker is, it's you." A theory of hierarchy had become, within a year, a manual for operating inside one without becoming its raw material.

Chapter Four: The Consulting Gig That Changed the Subject

The second answer arrived by way of a job. In two thousand fourteen, Marc Andreessen and Chris Dixon at the venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz hired Rao for a year-long consulting engagement, working through what would become the firm's signature argument: that software, having already remade media and retail, was about to remake every remaining industry from the inside. Andreessen had made the core claim himself three years earlier, in a Wall Street Journal essay arguing that companies in every sector needed to assume a software revolution was already underway, even the ones that considered themselves software companies today. Andreessen's own essay had already supplied the case he wanted Rao to build out at length: in two thousand one, Borders handed its entire online bookselling operation over to Amazon, on the theory that selling books through a website was a sideline not worth the trouble of running well, a decision that looked prudent at the time and looks, from ten years further on, like a company teaching its own eventual replacement how to do its job. Borders filed for bankruptcy a decade later. Amazon, by then, wasn't just selling books. What Rao built out of the consulting year was an extended, essay-by-essay argument for that same claim, generous enough in its scope that Andreessen Horowitz gave him shared rights to publish it himself. He called the project Breaking Smart, launched it as a newsletter, and it ran from two thousand fifteen through the end of the decade.

Season One collects twenty of those essays, illustrated by Grace Witherell, with an introduction by Andreessen himself, running to roughly thirty thousand words on how networked, software-native organizations were about to out-compete every industrial-era incumbent still running on hierarchy and physical scale. One of the season's sharper essays carries the title "Entrepreneurs Are The New Labour," and its wager, consistent with everything else in the series, is that full-time employment inside a stable firm was becoming the exception rather than the default: more and more people, the essay argues, would spend their working lives running small, informal, software-enabled ventures of their own, gaining real autonomy in exchange for giving up the steady paycheck Rao's own Losers had traded ambition for five years earlier. Run that claim next to David Graeber's account, from an essay this show read as its own sixteenth episode, of jobs designed to look necessary without being necessary, and the two arguments turn out to be describing the same labor market from opposite ends: Graeber's bullshit job is stable and pointless, Rao's entrepreneur is purposeful and unstable, and neither writer is claiming the trade is a fair one.

Chapter Five: The Diagnostician Takes the Sponsor's Money

Here is where the timeline stops being merely biographical, because the same year Rao took the Andreessen Horowitz engagement, he had already spent five years applying his own Gervais Principle to exactly this kind of arrangement. A venture capital firm with billions riding on the belief that software eats every industry commissions an independently credentialed essayist to make that case at book length, in his own voice, under his own byline, and gives him a cut of the proceeds. Nothing in that sentence is secret; Rao has never hidden the sponsorship, and Andreessen's own name sits on the introduction page. But run the arrangement through the vocabulary Rao himself invented in two thousand nine, and the shape is unmistakable: a firm with capital at stake recruiting a credible outside voice to tell its story back to the market in language that reads as independent analysis rather than promotion. That is close to a textbook description of what his own essay called a Sociopath grooming a useful Clueless, except that Rao is neither, quite. He knows exactly what he's doing and says so whenever asked, which makes him a third thing his own two thousand nine framework never quite anticipated: someone who takes the sponsor's argument because he already believes a milder version of it, and gets paid the difference between the mild version and the strong one Andreessen actually needed written.

Burnham's managers, from this show's seventh episode, mistook a passing transfer of authority for a new ruling class and turned out, on examination, to have described an empty throne with nobody actually sitting on it. Breaking Smart is the opposite mistake pointed the other direction: an argument that whoever writes the story software tells about itself doesn't matter, when the entire point of hiring Rao was that it obviously did. The essays aren't wrong about the underlying trend. Software really has gone on eating one industry after another for the ten years since; that part of the record, as the next chapter shows, holds up better than most decade-old technology forecasts have any right to. What the essays can't do, from inside the arrangement that produced them, is mark their own interest in the outcome. That's not a job for the diagnostician who took the fee. It's a job for whoever writes about him nine years later.

Chapter Six: A Salad, Alone

The third answer to what a diagnostician does with a correct theory arrived almost by accident, in a single essay published on Ribbonfarm in August of two thousand seventeen, two years into the Breaking Smart run and clearly written on a different day of the week. Rao names his invented everywoman Maya Millennial, and gives her the exact demographic he calls Blue Bicoastal: young, urban, gentrifying, renting rather than owning, subscribing rather than buying, working a job title that would have sounded like nonsense to her parents' generation and paying rent that would sound like nonsense back. Maya isn't rich and knows it. She's also, and this is the essay's actual discovery rather than its premise, not fooled about what she's buying. When she orders the good bottle at an unremarkable chain restaurant, or picks the truffle-oil version of a dish that didn't need it, she isn't mistaking the gesture for real luxury the way an earlier generation's status anxiety might have. She has a clear-eyed sense of how mediocre the underlying thing still is. She's paying for the fifteen minutes of feeling otherwise anyway, and she knows that too, and Rao's name for the whole posture, premium mediocre, is the rare coinage that a writer gets exactly once in a career: five years on from the essay, people who have never read a word of Rao's other work still reach for the phrase unprompted, because it named something they'd been living inside without a name for it.

Rao sets two other invented millennials against Maya to sharpen what she is and isn't. Molly is the hipster, chasing an authenticity so studied it becomes its own kind of performance, precarious but proud of the precarity as a marker of taste. Max is the hustler, treating the whole economy as an arbitrage problem to be gamed his way out of, one geography and one tax structure at a time. Maya is neither purist nor operator. She's the essay's actual center of gravity because she isn't optimizing for anything in particular, just managing the gap between the income she has and the image she needs to keep believing her twenties are heading somewhere.

Chapter Seven: The Necessary Fiction

The essay's real argument, and it arrives late, the way this show's own charter insists a delayed thesis should, is that premium mediocre isn't a symptom of a broken economy so much as scaffolding for a half-built one. Rao's own image for the transition is a civilization caught between two states, industrial modernity dying badly on one side and a digitally enabled abundance still in its unglamorous early testing phase on the other, with premium mediocre as the improvisation people build to survive standing in the gap between them. Everyone who performs competence they don't fully have yet, everyone who buys the gesture of luxury rather than the substance, is doing something closer to keeping a set dressed and lit while the real production gets built behind it, in his own phrase, an extra on a set, playing a bit part, so that the people actually building the next economy still have an audience that believes in the story enough to keep showing up.

It's an unusually generous reading of what could just as easily be read as a coping mechanism with no upside attached, and the generosity deserves more skepticism than the essay's own confident say-so invites, because the evidence behind it is thin in a way this show's clinical instincts are built to catch. Rao ran an informal poll of his own Twitter followers, a little over a hundred people, self-selected into an audience that already reads him, and reported that a majority identified with the premium mediocre description. That's not evidence a phenomenon is real across an economy. It's evidence that a sharply observed sketch resonates with the exact readership most likely to see itself in that particular mirror, which is a different and much smaller claim than the essay spends most of its length making.

Chapter Eight: The Front Region, Rebuilt for Rent

What the thin evidence doesn't undercut is the mechanism, and the mechanism is one this show has already spent an entire episode building the vocabulary for. Erving Goffman's account of everyday performance, from the thirty-first episode in this feed, distinguishes what a performer deliberately conveys from what a scene simply gives off around them regardless of intent, a setting doing argumentative work the performer never had to say out loud. The hackerspace hoodie and the open code editor are pure Goffman: nobody claims out loud to be a programmer, the setting and the costume convey it on the performer's behalf, and the convincing part is that none of it ever needs stating out loud. Premium mediocre generalizes that same front-region logic from a single social encounter to an entire economic identity, performed continuously across years rather than assembled fresh for one dinner party or one job interview. Maya isn't running one convincing scene. She's running the scene as her whole adult life, and the cost of maintaining it, measured in rent and identity both, is the part the essay's generosity almost lets her look past.

Chapter Nine: Scored Nine Years Later

Rao's own worked example for premium mediocrity as an institution rather than an individual performance was the shared office company then still ascendant, a startup selling desks and community to people exactly like Maya, itself premium mediocre at the scale of a whole business: a real estate company performing, with enormous confidence, the vocabulary and valuation of a technology company. That company filed for bankruptcy in November of two thousand twenty-three, its stock down more than ninety-nine percent from a peak valuation near forty-seven billion dollars, undone by a mix of its founder's own excesses and a pandemic that emptied the very offices its whole model depended on filling. The failure doesn't refute Rao's theory so much as sharpen its edges: Maya's premium mediocrity was survival, a bet on her own future that cost her a little now for a story she could keep telling herself. The company's was something closer to Rao's own Sociopath tier of ambition dressed in Maya's aesthetic, and when a Sociopath's bet fails at that scale, it doesn't fail the small, private way an individual's overpriced bottle of wine does. It fails as a headline.

The larger claim underneath both Breaking Smart and premium mediocre, that software would keep eating industry after industry, has aged rather better than most decade-old technology forecasts, and by two thousand twenty-five industry analysts were mostly confirming it rather than disputing it: software really did go on to consume retail, video, music, and telecommunications more or less as Andreessen predicted in his original essay, and trade press marking the tenth anniversary of his essay treated the prediction as settled history rather than a live argument anyone still needed to make. What nobody in the original argument saw coming was software eating itself in turn, as a newer wave of artificial intelligence began automating the writing of software the way software had automated everything before it. Steven Sinofsky, still at Andreessen Horowitz a decade after Rao's own engagement there, addressed the wrinkle directly rather than dodging it, arguing that artificial intelligence changes what gets built and who builds it. It's a fair point, and also the kind of grading a firm reliably gives its own decade-old thesis when the thesis still has money riding on being right, the same structural problem this show has already met once this year, in Andy Grove scoring his own management theory against his own cancer diagnosis. Funded correctness is still correctness. It just never quite counts as an independent verdict.

Chapter Ten: What the Readers Pushed Back On

Premium mediocre is the rare Rao coinage that escaped Ribbonfarm's own comment section entirely: general-interest sites built around a much wider readership than his, Jason Kottke's long-running link blog among them, picked the essay up within days and pointed their own much larger audiences at it, which is a far better test of an idea's independent usefulness than any poll Rao could have run on his own following. Reception to Rao's later work otherwise splits cleanly into people who extended the argument and the smaller number who actually argued with it. The rationalist blogger who writes under the name Zvi Mowshowitz spent two long posts working through premium mediocrity in the weeks after it published, largely agreeing with the mechanism while landing one correction that actually lands: Rao, in an aside, had called his own blog premium mediocre by his own definition, and Zvi rejected the comparison outright, arguing that Ribbonfarm did the real intellectual work its readers came for rather than merely performing the appearance of doing it. As Zvi puts it, describing the pattern rather than the self-deprecation: "Premium mediocre is food that Instagrams better than it tastes." A cupcake, on his reading, can be honestly well made and still count as premium mediocre, because the flaw the term names is the markup relative to a plainer alternative, not the quality of the thing itself, a distinction Rao's own essay blurs more than it needed to.

A smaller strand of response pushed on something closer to the essay's politics than its logic. The writer Priscilla De Pace, responding a few weeks after publication, argued that Rao's frame leaves no room for premium mediocrity to be anything but a losing hand dressed up as a coping strategy, when the same behavior could just as easily be read as a quieter form of resistance: people occupying the trappings of a system they don't fully believe in without ever fully buying its promises. Rao's essay treats Maya's self-awareness as proof she's still, however knowingly, playing the game. De Pace's version treats that same clear-eyed quality as a sign the game has already lost some of its grip on her. Neither writer had the data to settle which reading is closer to true for the actual millions of Mayas the essay claims to describe, and Rao, to his credit, never pretended the small Twitter poll settled it either.

Chapter Eleven: The Book That Needs the Rest of the Body of Work

A third strand of reception is really a craft complaint, and it applies to Rao's writing generally rather than to any single essay. Reviewing Be Slightly Evil years after its newsletter run ended, the writer Taylor Pearson praised the book's refusal of easy moralizing about power while noting, with visible frustration, that the book is far too dense to stand on its own, rewarding only readers already fluent in the rest of Rao's output. It's a fair charge, and an oddly fitting one for a body of work this show has spent ten chapters explaining rather than merely summarizing: Rao writes for people willing to do the connecting work themselves, across newsletters and years, and the reward for readers who do it is real, but the essays rarely meet a first-time reader where they stand. A show built around never announcing its own conclusions has some sympathy for a writer who refuses to over-explain. It has less sympathy for one whose refusal to explain leaves the actual argument recoverable only by people who already agree with it.

Chapter Twelve: The Argument Rao Made Against His Own Earlier Essay

Two years after premium mediocre, in a shorter, stranger series called Mediocratopia, Rao effectively conceded De Pace's point without ever citing her by name. Where the two thousand seventeen essay treated mediocrity as a stopgap, an aesthetic people wear while waiting for a better economy to arrive, Mediocratopia argues that mediocrity, chosen rather than merely endured, is closer to an ethical stance: a deliberate withholding of full effort and full optimization, kept in reserve rather than spent, because a system built entirely from maximally optimized parts breaks in ways a system with slack in it does not. He writes, in the series' own compact phrase for the reversal, of finding "the courage to be ordinary." It's the same writer, two years on, arguing the opposite lesson from the same underlying observation: where Maya's mediocrity was something she was doing to herself under duress, Mediocratopia's mediocrity is something a person or a system does on purpose, and does well.

That argument has an obvious application to the kind of distance-run counselling programme this show has used before as its own working test case for management advice: an editorial choice to accept forms that are good enough rather than perfect, staff who meet a solid bar rather than an exceptional one, and reporting cycles pitched for sustainability over five years rather than for looking impressive in any single quarter, all trade a small, visible loss of polish for a much larger, harder-to-see gain in the programme's odds of still running next year. A programme optimized at every layer for its best possible showing looks better in this month's numbers and is more fragile against next month's unplanned problem. Rao's own two thousand nine framework would have called that fragility the cost the Sociopaths at the top never personally pay. Mediocratopia is closer to an argument that everyone else should stop trying to earn a promotion out of paying it.

Chapter Thirteen: The Museum Closes on His Own Birthday

The last chapter in this particular arc is biographical rather than argumentative, and it happened only two years ago. Rao kept Ribbonfarm running for seventeen years, folding the newsletter through several names along the way, Breaking Smart becoming Ribbonfarm Studio becoming, eventually, a publication called Contraptions. In October of two thousand twenty-four he announced the blog itself would close permanently on the thirteenth of November, his own fiftieth birthday, framing the site's whole seventeen-year run as one clean chapter of recent history, opening with the two thousand eight financial crisis and closing with that autumn's American presidential election, both serving, in his words, as bookends rather than coincidences. He didn't frame the closure as defeat. He framed it as a form he'd already spent two years arguing was underrated: knowing when a good-enough run has reached its natural length, and ending it on purpose rather than optimizing it past the point where ending well was still possible. The retirement post leaves a small, dry joke for whoever eventually mines the archive, offering, in his own phrase, "directions to the service entrance for AI scraper-bots," fully expecting that the next generation of readers arriving at his life's work would not be people at all.

Chapter Fourteen: The Chapters In Between

Run the whole arc together and it reads less like four separate ideas than one long argument a single mind kept having with itself, in public, for fifteen years. Two thousand nine named the trap well enough that this show has never stopped using his vocabulary. The following year gave that trap's residents a personal manual for surviving inside it without becoming its raw material. By two thousand fourteen he was taking money from people with a direct financial stake in one particular story about the future, and telling that story better than they could have told it themselves, in the exact position his own five-years-earlier framework would have flagged as compromised. Three years after that he stepped back far enough to name what the ordinary people underneath that story were actually doing to survive it, thinly evidenced but unmistakably recognized the moment it was named. Two years further on, he argued against his own earlier essay that the survival strategy might be worth choosing on purpose rather than merely enduring. And two years ago this past autumn, he closed the whole project on a date he picked himself, at a length he judged sufficient rather than maximal, which is either the least dramatic ending available to a fifteen-year argument about power, or, read against everything that came before it, the only ending that argument could have honestly earned.

This feed's own first numbered episode opened inside Jackall's corporate bureaucracies, using a vocabulary Rao had already supplied for it seventeen years earlier. It has spent the thirty-three episodes since testing that vocabulary against monopolies and dams and improv troupes and prison-camp essays and a nuclear reactor's stuck valve, largely without checking back on what its own original source had done with the idea in the meantime. He spent the same fifteen years doing something closer to what Andy Grove asked a whole industry to do with a bad diagnosis: naming it, profiting from naming it, arguing with his own naming, and eventually, deliberately, walking away from the naming altogether rather than let it calcify into a brand he'd have to keep performing.

Chapter Fifteen: What This Leaves You With

The question worth carrying out of this particular episode isn't whether premium mediocrity describes you, most listeners can already answer that one privately, but which of Rao's four postures you're actually standing in right now, in whatever version of the trap you're currently inside: still just naming it, already profiting from the naming, arguing with your own earlier read of it, or finally ready to end a good-enough run on a date you picked yourself rather than wait for someone else to pick it for you.

Next time, the shelf reaches back past every episode so far to Robert Merton's nineteen forty paper on bureaucratic personality, the same trained incapacity and goal displacement this feed has spent thirty-four episodes describing in fresher clothes, published with the citations attached, four years before C. Northcote Parkinson found the same pattern in the Admiralty's own growth curves and told it as a joke instead. Thanks for listening.
