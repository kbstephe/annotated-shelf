Bullshit Jobs (Graeber): five types, and the two surveys that disagree

David Graeber's 2018 book on paid work that even the worker cannot justify – the five types, managerial feudalism, the polls that launched it, and the two surveys that later disagreed about whether any of it is real.

26 August 2026

Chapter One: The Computer That Moved Four Hundred Kilometres

One of the testimonies in the book comes from a man who worked for a subcontractor to the German military, and I want to tell it at its own pace because the whole argument is folded inside it. A soldier in a barracks wants to move a computer from one office to the office next door, a distance of about five metres. He cannot simply carry it. The army has outsourced its logistics to a private firm; that firm has passed the IT portion to a second one, and the second has handed the actual moving of equipment to a third. So the soldier fills in a form. The form goes to the first firm, which forwards it to the second, which forwards it to the third, and the third dispatches the man telling the story, who drives several hundred kilometres to the barracks, signs a document, unplugs the computer, carries it into the next room, plugs it in, signs another document, and drives home. The book's narrator does not need to add a moral; the day's mileage is the moral. Somewhere in that chain a number of people were paid, a contract was honoured, a compliance requirement was met, and a computer travelled five metres by way of a motorway.

Now ask the clinician's question about that story, the question this episode is going to keep asking: what is the denominator? The moving of the computer is a case. How many cases like it are there, out of how many working days in the German economy, and is the man telling it typical of anything or is he a memorable outlier who wrote to an anthropologist because anthropologists were asking? The book you are about to hear described is built almost entirely out of stories like this one, hundreds of them, collected from people who volunteered them. It is the most read book this shelf has covered and the least measured, and the interesting part is that the measuring was done afterwards, twice, by people who disagreed with each other about what it showed.

The question the book is about predates it by a long way, and this series has been circling it since the Parkinson episode. An organization is supposed to be a machine for turning effort into some output somebody wants. Parkinson found the effort multiplying regardless of the output; Peter found it settling into positions where it could no longer produce; the Davies episodes found the output nobody wanted being produced with enormous care because a process demanded it. What none of those books asked, because their authors were all looking at a single institution at a time, is the aggregate version. Add it all up across a whole economy, every Colonial Office clerk and every final-placement supervisor and every duplicate reporting line, and how much of the paid work in a rich country is, on the honest account of the person doing it, for nothing? Graeber's answer is a number, and the number is the problem.

Chapter Two: An Essay, a Poll, and a Book

David Graeber was introduced two episodes ago, and the biography that matters here is the same: London School of Economics, Debt: The First Five Thousand Years, the planning meetings of Occupy Wall Street, and a mother's paperwork. What comes between The Utopia of Rules and this book is an essay, and the essay's reception is half the story.

In the summer of two thousand and thirteen a small radical magazine called Strike! asked Graeber for something provocative and he gave them three thousand words titled On the Phenomenon of Bullshit Jobs. The premise was a question he attributed to Keynes: in nineteen thirty, Keynes had predicted that by the end of the century technology would have advanced enough that countries like Britain or the United States would be working a fifteen-hour week. The technology arrived. The short week did not. Graeber's essay proposed that the missing hours had not been absorbed by more production of things people want, but by the invention of work, whole categories of employment that exist without producing anything, and that the people inside those categories know it. His phrase for the damage was that it is a scar across our collective soul. The essay's most quoted move was a thought experiment: imagine a class of workers vanishing overnight and ask whether anyone would miss them. Nurses, refuse collectors, mechanics, teachers, dock workers gone, and the world is in trouble within days. Private equity executives, lobbyists, public relations researchers, actuaries, telemarketers, bailiffs, legal consultants gone, and it is not obvious how anyone would suffer. From which he drew a general rule, that the more obviously a job benefits other people, the less it tends to pay.

The essay went around the world. The magazine's website fell over under the traffic; the piece was translated into more than a dozen languages within weeks. And then the poll. In two thousand and fifteen the polling firm YouGov asked British workers whether their job made a meaningful contribution to the world. Thirty-seven percent said no, and a further thirteen percent were not sure. A Dutch survey around the same time produced a figure near forty percent. Graeber had written a provocation with a made-up range in it, somewhere between twenty and fifty percent of all jobs, and now a real poll had landed inside the range, and the provocation had become a finding, or something that could be presented as one. He asked people who felt they had bullshit jobs to write to him, several hundred did, and the book that came out in two thousand and eighteen, Bullshit Jobs: A Theory, is built on those testimonies, the poll, and a theory of why a market economy would produce this at all.

The definition, in the book, is tighter than the essay's and it is worth getting exactly right because most of the later argument turns on what it includes. A bullshit job is paid employment so pointless, unnecessary, or pernicious that even the person doing it cannot justify its existence, though the terms of employment oblige them to pretend otherwise. Two clauses carry it. The first is even the employee: this is not an outsider's judgement that your job is worthless, it is your own. The second is the obligation to pretend, which is what distinguishes a bullshit job from a merely bad one. A bad job, in Graeber's vocabulary, is one that is useful and awful: cleaning, care work, the kind of thing that would be missed within a day and is paid as though it would not. A bullshit job is one that is comfortable and hollow, and the hollowness has to be concealed, from the boss, from the family, sometimes from the worker's own account of the week.

Chapter Three: Five Ways to Be Paid for Nothing

The book's taxonomy is the part people remember, and it earns the memory, because each of the five types names a mechanism rather than a job title. Graeber is careful about that: no occupation is bullshit as such; the types describe what a role is for within an organization, whatever it is called on the door.

Flunkies exist to make somebody else feel or look important. The receptionist in an office that gets three calls a day is not there for the calls. She is there because a firm of that kind has a receptionist, and a partner who walks past an empty desk in the morning is a partner at a lesser firm. Graeber's own examples run from doormen to the assistants of assistants, and the mechanism is feudal in a way he will make explicit later: retainers whose function is the standing of the person they are attached to.

Goons exist because the other side has goons. Lobbyists, corporate lawyers, telemarketers, the publicist whose job is to counter a competitor's publicist. Graeber's test is that the role only makes sense in an adversarial frame: if every firm's lobbyists vanished at once, no firm would be worse off relative to any other, which means the aggregate contribution of the category is zero by construction. Note that the goon's job is often not pointless from the employer's point of view, which is why this type sits awkwardly in a definition built on the worker's own judgement; a good deal of the book's later trouble with critics lives in this category.

Duct tapers exist to fix a problem that should not exist. The programmer who spends his life patching code that was written badly because the firm would not pay for it to be written well; the airline employee whose entire role is calming passengers whose luggage the airline has lost, when the money spent calming them would have paid for not losing the luggage. This one connects straight back to Davies, three episodes and a companion piece into the series: the duct taper is an accountability sink with a salary, a person whose presence lets an organization keep a fault permanent by making its consequences absorbable.

Box tickers exist so that an organization can claim to be doing something it is not doing. The officer whose reports on a hospital's patient-consultation process are produced, filed, and never read, so that the hospital can say consultation occurs. The whole audit society Davies described is staffed by box tickers, and the sharp thing in Graeber's account is the worker's experience of it: box tickers frequently know the box is the product, and their job is to keep producing it convincingly.

And taskmasters exist to assign work to people who would work without being told to, or, in the second and more corrosive variant, to invent work for others, the manager who generates the checklists and the forms and the strategic-alignment exercises that the box tickers below then tick. Taskmasters of the second kind are the ones who produce bullshit jobs downstream, and Graeber's diagram of an organization in this condition is one where taskmasters at the top manufacture tasks, box tickers in the middle complete them, and duct tapers at the bottom absorb the damage, with flunkies attached to everyone senior and goons on the perimeter.

Read that structure against the shelf and it maps onto the last five books with almost no adjustment. Parkinson's officials who make work for each other are taskmasters and box tickers in a closed loop. Peter's final-placement supervisor, the person promoted one rung past competence, is very often a taskmaster of the first kind, supervising people who need no supervision because that is the only supervision he is now capable of. And Whyte's organization man, the well-adjusted middle who identified with the firm, is what a box ticker looks like before he has admitted to himself what the box is. Graeber's contribution is not that he found these people. Everyone on this shelf found them. His contribution is the claim that they are now a large fraction of the workforce, and that the fraction is not a side effect of bureaucracy but the product of a specific economic arrangement, which he names.

Chapter Four: Managerial Feudalism

Here is the puzzle the theory has to solve, and Graeber states it fairly. Under the textbook account of a market economy, bullshit jobs should not exist. A firm that pays people to do nothing is a firm with higher costs than a competitor that does not, and competition should eliminate it. That is the argument every economist made against the essay within a week of its publication, and it is a good argument. Governments might pad payrolls for political reasons, the objection goes, but private firms cannot afford to, so if the phenomenon is real it must be a public-sector phenomenon, which would be an old and unsurprising finding.

Graeber's answer is that the textbook is describing an economy we no longer have. What we have, he argues, is managerial feudalism: a system in which a growing share of the economy's surplus is captured not by producing things but by controlling flows of money, rent, and administrative position, and in which the people who capture it distribute a portion of it downward in the form of retainers, because in a system of that kind having retainers is how standing is displayed and maintained. A feudal lord does not employ a hall full of attendants because they are productive. He employs them because a lord with a small retinue is a small lord. Graeber's claim is that the managerial class in finance, in the professional services that orbit finance, in the administrative layers of universities and hospitals, has recreated exactly that logic, and that the growth of the administrative layer in every large organization since the nineteen seventies is what feudal retinues look like when they are wearing lanyards.

The mechanism has three parts, and each connects to something the shelf has already documented. The first is the one Graeber shares with the flying-cars essay two episodes ago: the redirection of the economy's investment from making things toward administering, monitoring, and financialising the things that already exist. If the profitable activity is control rather than production, then the labour that control requires, compliance, reporting, reconciliation, the manufacturing and completion of forms, grows regardless of whether anything is being made. The second part is political, and it is where Graeber is most a moralist: a population kept busy is a population that does not have the time to ask what the arrangement is for, and he suggests, without quite claiming it as a design, that the ruling class of any period has understood that a leisured, unemployed, healthy population is a danger. The third part is cultural, and it is the one his critics find hardest to dismiss. Work has become, in the rich countries, the primary source of moral standing, so that a person who does not work is a person without a claim on the respect of others, regardless of what the work produces. The result is that everybody, including the worker in a bullshit job, has a stake in the job continuing, because the alternative is not leisure but disgrace.

That third part is Whyte's Social Ethic, seventy years on and stripped of its optimism. Whyte's organization man believed that belonging to the group was a moral good in itself. Graeber's box ticker believes, or has been made to believe, the same thing about having a job, and the two beliefs are one belief with the group replaced by the payroll. What Whyte saw as the birth of a new ethic Graeber sees at the end of its life, when the belief has outlived the conditions that made it plausible, the productive firm with a real product and a real career inside it, and continues to run on its own momentum. The organization man at least had an organization. The bullshit worker has a badge that opens a door to a desk at which nothing happens.

And I would push the shelf's own frame one step further than Graeber does, because the series has an instrument he lacked. He explains why an economy would pay for hollow work. He does not quite explain why nobody inside the organization stops it, given that many of them know. The Davies series answered that: nobody in a position to stop it is running the organization. Command left the owners and did not arrive at the managers; it dissolved into the procedures between them. A taskmaster who realised tomorrow that his department produced nothing could not abolish it, because the department is now an input to three other departments' reports, and a report that arrives without its input is a compliance failure with a name attached. Bullshit jobs persist for the same reason the sinks persist: the machine that would have to catch them is the machine they are part of.

Chapter Five: The Book at Its Strongest

Before the numbers, the steelman, because the book's best chapter is not the theory. It is the chapter on what the condition does to the people in it, and here the testimonies stop being anecdotes and start being clinical material.

Graeber's expectation, and the reader's, is that a job where nothing is required of you would be experienced as a kind of freedom. Eight paid hours, no output, an internet connection. The testimonies say otherwise, consistently and in detail. The people writing to him describe anxiety, depression, a corrosion of self-respect that they cannot explain to friends who work real jobs and cannot mention to the employer who has, after all, done nothing wrong. Several describe the specific torment of having to look busy, of maintaining a performance of work for a manager who may or may not know it is a performance. One of the book's most-quoted contributors, a man whose entire job was to be present in case a rarely used system failed, describes the experience as being paid to be a piece of furniture. Graeber reaches for a concept from developmental psychology to explain the damage: the pleasure of being the cause, the infant's discovery that its actions produce effects in the world, which he argues is the root of the human sense of self. A job in which nothing you do produces any effect is not rest. It is a slow attack on the faculty that makes you a person.

That is a real observation, and it is the part of the book that the later surveys confirmed without controversy. Whatever the prevalence of the condition, the people who report it are measurably worse off. I will come back to how much worse. But the steelman is that Graeber identified a form of harm that the discipline of work had not been looking for, because its instruments measured hours, pay, safety, and satisfaction, and this harm survives all four being fine. You can be well paid, safe, working reasonable hours, reporting satisfaction on the annual survey because the survey does not have a box for this, and still be coming apart in a way that only the plain question, would anyone miss your job if it vanished, can reach. The book made that question askable. That is not nothing, and it may be most of what the book will be remembered for.

Chapter Six: The Two Surveys

Now the measuring, which is where this shelf's home lens is needed, and the finding is that the answer depends entirely on which question was asked.

The first serious test came from a Cambridge and Birmingham group, Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood and Brendan Burchell, published in Work, Employment and Society in twenty twenty-two under the title Alienation Is Not Bullshit. They went to the European Working Conditions Survey, which asks a very large sample of workers across the European Union, every five years, a battery of questions about their jobs, one of which is how often they have the feeling of doing useful work. Take the people who answer rarely or never as the bullshit-job population and the numbers come out like this. In two thousand and five, seven point eight percent. In twenty ten, five point five. In twenty fifteen, four point eight. Not twenty to fifty percent; under five, and falling. The regional pattern was the reverse of Graeber's expectation too: the Nordic countries lowest at three percent, the British Isles and the Mediterranean highest at about five and a half. And the occupations at the top of the uselessness table were not the lobbyists and the compliance officers. They were labourers in mining, construction and transport, at fifteen percent; refuse workers, near ten; cleaners, at eight. The refuse collectors, the very people Graeber's thought experiment held up as the workers society could not do without, were among the most likely to say their own work was useless. Teachers, doctors and lawyers were at the bottom, around one percent. Graduates reported the condition at half the rate of non-graduates. The group's conclusion was that Graeber's account could not be sustained: the perception of useless work was real and was strongly associated with poor wellbeing, but it tracked bad management, lack of autonomy and disrespect rather than occupation, so that what it tracked was what Marx called alienation, a much older idea, and one that does not require a theory of managerial feudalism to explain.

The second test came a year later from Simon Walo at Zurich, in the same journal, and it reached the opposite headline. Walo used the American Working Conditions Survey from twenty fifteen, about eighteen hundred workers, and a different question: whether the respondent's job was, in their own view, socially useless, or gave them the feeling of making a positive impact on their community and society. Nineteen percent said their job was useless. And when he sorted by occupation, Graeber's categories showed up. Sales workers were two and a half times as likely as everyone else to consider their jobs useless; business and finance roughly twice; managers nearly twice; office and administrative support about one and three-quarter times. Legal work leaned the same way but did not reach significance. Those ratios survived adjustment for the very factors Soffia's group had said explained everything, the routine content of the work, the autonomy, the quality of management. Walo's verdict was that Graeber's theory had robust support, at least in what he called the heavily financialised Anglo-Saxon economies, while also finding that the alienation variables mattered too, so that useless work was multifactorial rather than any one thing.

So one study says five percent and falling and the wrong occupations, and the other says nineteen percent and the right ones. Both are competent. Both are in the same journal. How does a listener decide?

This is the moment the shelf's own instruments earn their keep, because the disagreement is not really about bullshit jobs. It is about a question this series has met twice already in other clothes: the rate you get depends on what you count, and the same statistic differs when the frame differs. Look at the two questions. The European survey asks how often you feel you are doing useful work. That is a question about the experience of the working day; a refuse collector on a bad crew under a contemptuous supervisor, doing objectively vital work, can answer never, and he is not wrong about his week. The American survey asks whether the job is socially useless or makes a positive impact on society. That is a question about the job's standing in the world, and a sales executive who is well managed and autonomous can answer useless, and he is not wrong about the job. Graeber's definition was the second kind of question: the worker's judgement of the job's contribution, not the worker's feeling of usefulness on a Tuesday. Soffia's group measured alienation and found alienation. Walo measured the thing Graeber described and found something like it, at a rate about half of the YouGov figure that started the whole affair, and concentrated where the book said it would be.

Which means the honest reading is narrower than either headline. The book's number, twenty to fifty percent, does not survive. Its map, that the condition concentrates in finance, sales, administration and management rather than in the useful-and-awful jobs, does survive, at least in the United States, at around one worker in five, and about one in nine in the occupations the book did not accuse. And the mechanism that best explains the remainder of the variation is not feudalism but the old and boring one, the quality of the relationship between a worker and the people above him. Graeber is right about where the hollow jobs are and wrong about how many there are, and the thing he most wanted to be right about, that the hollowness is a structural product of financialised capitalism rather than of bad bosses, is the part the evidence splits on, with the better-designed test of his own definition leaning his way.

Chapter Seven: The Case Against

The critics had assembled before either survey and their counts should be heard at full strength, because the strongest of them are about the book's method rather than its number.

Michael Robbins, reviewing it for The Nation, put the central objection in one sentence: the book's reliance on workers' subjective impressions of whether their work has value is its major weakness. A testimony is a self-selected story told to a sympathetic collector. The several hundred people who wrote to Graeber were people who had read an essay about bullshit jobs, recognised themselves in it, and wanted to be in the book. No one who read the essay and thought, that is not me, wrote in to say so. The book's evidence base is a case series with no controls. A series like that can establish that a condition exists and describe what it looks like, which Graeber did well, but it cannot say how common the condition is, and the book's central claim is about prevalence. Robbins also caught a factual problem the Bureau of Labor Statistics could have fixed for free: the fastest-growing job categories in the United States are not information and administrative work but care work and food service, useful-and-awful jobs, the opposite of what the theory predicts is expanding.

The second count is the feudalism. Robbins again, and he is not alone: an economy in which surplus is extracted from the labour of others by the owners of capital is a capitalist economy, whatever its retinues look like, and calling it feudal does not make the profit motive disappear from the firms that employ flunkies. The word does rhetorical work; it does not do analytical work. The taskmaster who hires a retinue is still, on the shelf's account, a component of a machine optimising for something, and the interesting question, which the book never quite reaches, is what that something is. Davies's answer, that the machine is optimising for the survival of its own procedures, explains the retinue without needing the lord.

The third count is about the taxonomy, and Andrew Anthony in The Observer made it briskly: the five types are arbitrary distinctions dressed as discoveries. A goon's job is not bullshit to the firm that pays for it; a duct taper is doing real work on a real problem; a flunky in a service economy is delivering a service, standing, that the customer demonstrably wants. The categories name the worker's discomfort and then treat the discomfort as evidence of pointlessness, when several of them describe work that is pointed, just not at anything the worker respects. Nathan Heller, in The New Yorker, added the gentlest and most damaging version: some of these jobs are apprenticeships, training that looks like nothing because it is being done by someone not yet able to do the real thing, and some are a form of workfare the society has decided it prefers to unemployment, and that is a policy, not a scandal.

And Emma Duncan, in The Times, supplied the number Graeber's Keynes framing needed and did not have. The British working week fell from about fifty-six hours in nineteen hundred to about thirty-one in twenty eighteen. Keynes's fifteen did not arrive, but half of the distance to it did, and a theory that opens by asking where the promised leisure went has to account for the leisure that actually came. She called the book enjoyably overstated, and the adverb is doing a lot of the reviewing.

I want to add a count of my own, the one the shelf's history of Graeber makes visible. Two episodes ago The Utopia of Rules argued that bureaucracy persists because it does not have to earn your understanding; it only needs the standing to make you do the interpretive labour. That was a structural claim about power, and it did not depend on anyone's feelings. Bullshit Jobs is the same author making a claim that depends entirely on feelings, the worker's own verdict, and then treating the verdict as a measurement of the economy. The earlier book's method was better. The later book's subject was more popular. It is not a coincidence that the popular one is the one with the number in it.

Chapter Eight: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

The verdict is that this is a good essay that became a famous book, and that the famous book contains one durable finding and one number that should never be quoted. The finding is the harm: paid work that the worker cannot justify produces a specific and measurable injury, invisible to every conventional measure of job quality, and both later surveys, whatever else they disagreed about, confirmed that the people who report it are substantially worse off. The number is the prevalence. Twenty to fifty percent was a provocation that a poll happened to land inside, and the two proper tests since put the figure at under five percent by one definition and around nineteen by the other, with the higher figure the better match to what Graeber actually meant. If you must carry a single figure, carry Walo's: about one in five American workers, concentrated in sales, finance, administration and management, and the rate in those occupations roughly double the rate elsewhere.

The dose is diagnostic, and the book is better taken in a specific order. Read the two thousand and thirteen essay first, in twenty minutes; it contains the whole idea and none of the padding. Then read the book's chapter on the psychological effects, the part the testimonies were made for. Skip the feudalism until you have read Davies, because Davies explains the same persistence without the costume. And read Soffia's paper and Walo's paper together, in that order, as an exercise in the pattern this series keeps finding: the same phenomenon, two competent groups, two questions, two answers, and the difference lives in the wording of a single survey item.

The question for Monday is Graeber's own, and it survives every objection to his book because it was never a claim. Take your own job, and the three jobs nearest to it in the building, and ask of each one, not whether it is hard, not whether it is paid, not whether it is respected, but whether anything would happen if it stopped. Be specific about the anything. A report that would not be filed is not a consequence unless somebody would act differently for lack of it; a meeting that would not occur is not a loss unless a decision would go unmade. If you can name the consequence, you have a job, whatever the survey says. If the only consequence you can find is that someone would remark on the absence of the activity, and nothing downstream of the activity would change, then you have found one of Graeber's, and the useful part of his book is the reminder that the person in it already knows, and has been told, by everyone including himself, not to say so.
