Geopolitics and the Green Revolution (Perkins): Borlaug's dwarf wheat and the missing control group

Norman Borlaug's method, read from John Perkins's history and Borlaug's own 1968 and 1970 lectures: shuttle breeding in Mexico, the Japanese dwarfing genes, the seed shipped to India and Pakistan, and why nobody can say what the seed did apart from the fertiliser, the water and the price.

13 November 2026

Chapter One: The Freighter

In August of 1965 a convoy of trucks came north out of Mexico carrying six hundred tons of wheat seed, bound for a freighter at Los Angeles. Three hundred and fifty tons were for Pakistan and the rest for India. The convoy drove into the Watts riots. It sat in the traffic around a burning district while the ship's sailing time came closer, and it reached the dockside a few hours before departure.

The freighter was still at sea, with both consignments in the same hold, when India and Pakistan went to war with each other. The seed arrived late in both countries. When it was tested, a good part of it would not germinate. It was sown anyway, thicker than planned, at the start of what proved to be two consecutive failures of the monsoon, the worst drought either country had seen in years.

The man who had bred that wheat and arranged that shipment was an American plant pathologist named Norman Borlaug. Telling the story to a room of geneticists in Australia three years later, he put the outcome down to God and luck riding along as co-pilots. By then he had something to report. India's wheat harvest in the spring of 1968 came in at about a third above the best the country had ever recorded. Pakistan's was up by half. Schools and sports stadiums were requisitioned to hold the grain. Two years after that the Norwegian Nobel Committee gave him the Peace Prize, and its chair said that he, beyond anyone else of his time, had helped provide "bread for a hungry world."

You will have met the stronger version, usually without a source: that Borlaug saved a billion lives. I have not found the study that sentence comes from, and I do not think there is one.

This series has been asking of each of its doers who was in a position to check them. Robert Moses was checked by nobody. Lee Kuan Yew checked himself. Hyman Rickover, last time, was checked by a reactor, a machine that reports within hours on anyone who has stopped paying attention. Borlaug's auditor looks like the best of the four. A harvest is weighed in public, every year, by people with no reason to flatter a foreign scientist. So this episode asks what a harvest can tell you about the person who claims it, and what it cannot.

Chapter Two: A Forester in Mexico

Who was he, and where does this account come from? The two biographies, by Noel Vietmeyer and by Leon Hesser, I could not open. What I have read instead is a scholar's book that treats Borlaug as one figure among many: Geopolitics and the Green Revolution, published in 1997 by John Perkins, a historian of science who interviewed Borlaug in 1989 and went through the Rockefeller Foundation's files. Beside it I have Borlaug's own two long accounts, a public lecture given in Canberra in 1968 and the Nobel lecture of 1970. Perkins's argument is in his title. Governments paid for high-yielding wheat, he says, for reasons of national security and foreign exchange, and the hungry came into it second. I will come back to whether that changes what the wheat did.

Borlaug was born in 1914 on a small farm near Cresco, in northeastern Iowa. He went to the University of Minnesota in 1933, somewhat on a lark by Perkins's account, and was told on arrival that he would need a spell of preparatory work before he could matriculate. Some of his advisers wondered aloud whether he was university material. He studied forestry. A plant pathologist there named Elvin Stakman, the leading student of the rust fungi that destroy wheat, talked him out of trees and into diseases, and he finished a doctorate in 1942. The war then sent him to the DuPont company in Delaware, where his contribution to victory was research on mildew in military rayon.

Stakman had meanwhile led a survey of Mexican agriculture for the Rockefeller Foundation. The Mexican government had asked the foundation for help, and its reasons were plain arithmetic. Mexico was importing more than half the wheat it ate, the bill was the largest single item among its food imports, and stem rust had ruined the crop in the northwestern state of Sonora three years running at the turn of the decade. National yields stood at seven hundred and fifty kilos a hectare, about eleven bushels an acre, and had not moved in living memory.

Borlaug arrived in October 1944. Perkins lists what he lacked. He had never worked on wheat or set foot outside his own country. He spoke no Spanish. The programme had no facilities to speak of, and within a year he and his wife had buried a child who died shortly after birth. In the spring of 1945 the programme's director, George Harrar, found the administrative load too heavy and handed the wheat to the new man.

The brief he received, as he recalled it to Perkins forty-four years later, was to get more wheat produced in Mexico as fast as possible. The minister of agriculture backed him and said openly that he did not expect miracles. That target is a national total, counted in tons and in dollars not spent abroad. Nobody asked him which Mexicans should grow the extra wheat, or which should eat it. The first place that silence shows is on a map.

Chapter Three: Two Thousand Kilometres

Mexico grew wheat in two places that had almost nothing in common. One was the Bajio, the central highlands near the capital, where the farms were small and the farmers poor, ploughing with a wooden plough behind oxen and cutting the crop with a sickle. The other was the newly irrigated desert of the northwest, the Yaqui Valley of Sonora, a country of big holdings with machinery and capital.

The breeder's orthodoxy, which Borlaug had been taught at Minnesota, said you bred a variety in the place where it would be grown. A plant had to fit its soil and its climate, and the only way to be sure of the fit was to make every selection on the spot. That meant one generation a year, and since a new variety took about ten generations to fix, ten years from first cross to release.

Some time between 1946 and 1948 Borlaug began breaking that rule. He sowed his crosses in Sonora in November, a few feet above sea level, and harvested in April. He took the seed two thousand kilometres south and more than eight thousand feet up to Toluca, near Mexico City, sowed it in May in cool rain where rust epidemics came every year, and harvested in October, in time to carry it back north for November. Two generations a year. The practice came to be called shuttle breeding.

The conditions in Sonora were bad. The experiment station there had been founded by a former governor and then left to fall apart. Borlaug slept in a hayloft, cooked for himself, and borrowed machinery from the neighbours or did the work by hand. He told Perkins that he had wondered in those seasons whether leaving DuPont had been a mistake.

His superiors thought the exercise expensive and unsound. In 1948 Harrar, with Stakman behind him, told Borlaug to stop going north. Borlaug resigned. Within a day the two of them declined to accept the resignation and let him carry on. Why they folded he never knew for certain. His guess was a letter that reached the office on the very day he quit, from a farmer named Aureliano Campoy whose land adjoined the Sonora station, praising what the wheat team was doing with no support from headquarters. So the method survived because a neighbour wrote in.

Borlaug wanted two things from the shuttle and got both. He wanted speed, and by 1950 the programme had released at least eight varieties that resisted rust. And he wanted the big growers of the northwest, because he had concluded that national production could only rise quickly on farms that could afford nitrogen. His colleague on the maize side, Edwin Wellhausen, held that the programme existed for the highland majority and stayed with them. Borlaug followed the tonnage. Mexico became self-sufficient in wheat in 1956, and the growers of Sonora, who had despised scientists after the rust years, were by 1968 funding their regional research station at five times the level government did.

The third result he had not planned. Most wheats will not flower until the days are lengthening. Borlaug's seed was sown in the north as the days shortened and in the south as they grew, generation after generation. Any plant that cared about the calendar died out of the programme. What remained was wheat indifferent to day length, and so wheat that could be moved across latitudes. He learned why only afterwards.

Look at it as a design. He ran his selection at two sites chosen to be as unlike as possible, and whatever survived both was, by construction, something that travels. It is the nearest thing in plant breeding to demanding that a result replicate before you believe it. But look at what the two sites shared. In each of them the plants were given water, by canal in the desert and by a metre of rain in the mountains, and in each the experimenter supplied the nitrogen. The wheat was selected to ignore latitude. Nothing in the procedure asked it to ignore poverty.

Chapter Four: Norin Ten

By the early fifties Borlaug had run into a ceiling, and it was made of straw. Feed a tall wheat enough nitrogen and the head grows too heavy for the stem. The crop falls over, which breeders call lodging, and the grain rots on the ground. In Mexico the best tall varieties stopped rewarding fertiliser at around eighty kilos of nitrogen a hectare and four and a half tons of grain. Harrar's judgement in 1950 was that the wheat programme had reached its yield potential and that what remained was better seed supply and better farming.

Borlaug and a colleague had been looking for short wheats since the mid forties. The ones they found were genetic accidents that did not breed true. In 1952 he searched the United States government's world collection of wheats for a usable dwarf and came away with nothing.

The answer reached him through the mail. A federal coordinator named Burton Bayles, who toured the western breeding stations, told him that year about a breeder in Washington State called Orville Vogel, who was working with a Japanese dwarf variety, Norin Ten. Borlaug wrote to Vogel. In 1953 Vogel sent him seed from a cross of Norin Ten with an American wheat. Borlaug's first attempt to cross it into his Mexican lines, in 1954, failed completely, because the dwarf had no resistance to Mexican rust and every mother plant died before setting seed. The second attempt, the next year, took.

What came out was a short, stiff wheat that could take a hundred and twenty kilos of nitrogen and stay standing. It also carried more grains in each head and threw up more stems from each plant, which nobody had asked for. On good farms in Sonora the new varieties gave five, six, seven and eight tons where four and a half had been the limit. Borlaug released the first two in the early sixties before he thought them ready. He had no choice. Farmers visiting the station on field days were pocketing seed from the breeding plots, and once the varieties were out a black market in them ran for two years.

Compare that to where he had started. In 1948 he held his first field day for farmers in Sonora, and twenty-five people came, five of them actual farmers. By the fifties he was getting thousands. Mexico's average yield, the stagnant seven hundred and fifty, reached almost twenty-eight hundred kilos by 1967.

Perkins is careful about whose achievement the dwarf was. The Japanese bred it. Vogel saw what it was for. The idea that a breeder's job was to build a plant that could use more nitrogen without collapsing had been standard among American wheat men for a decade before the seed crossed the Pacific. Perkins credits Borlaug and Harrar with no new concept except the shuttle. And his own figures show that Mexico reached self-sufficiency in 1956 on the tall rust-resistant varieties, some years before a dwarf was released; the dwarfs turned sufficiency into surplus.

So if the gene was Japan's and the insight was Vogel's, what did the man in the hayloft contribute that earned the prize? Perkins's phrase for his later role is that he became the chief courier of the dwarfing genes and of a gospel that went with them. To see what the courier added, follow the seed to India.

Chapter Five: The Long Pass

In February 1962 an Indian geneticist, M. S. Swaminathan, was walking the plots at the agricultural research institute in New Delhi and saw some wheat with short stiff stems and full-sized heads. He traced the records. The plants had come from Mexico in an international nursery for testing rust. He proposed that the institute invite their breeder.

Borlaug toured India for a month in March 1963, and that autumn he sent a hundred kilos each of four varieties, with small samples of six hundred more lines. The first season's results at four stations were plain enough. India's own improved wheats, on research plots, usually gave between two thousand and twenty-four hundred kilos a hectare. The Mexican ones gave twenty-nine hundred to thirty-seven hundred. The Indian farmer's average at the time was seven hundred and fifty-nine.

Here the scientists divided. The senior Indian breeders, B. P. Pal and S. P. Kohli, did not doubt the genes. They doubted the varieties. The Mexican grain came out shrivelled, and the Sonora varieties were susceptible to yellow rust in Punjab. The grain was also red, and when it reached the markets it sold at a discount of ten to twenty percent against white. They proposed the normal course: cross the dwarfs with Indian wheats, select for several years, and release something bred for the country. A Rockefeller man in Delhi agreed with them and pointed to American hybrid maize, which had been released in India too early and had caused trouble.

Borlaug's answer survives in a letter Perkins quotes. "Don't look for the perfect variety," he wrote; if a breeding programme was moving as fast as it should, anything being multiplied for release was already obsolete, and that was how things ought to be. Use the Mexican wheats as they stood, at once, and breed the better ones behind them.

He then made a second decision that the economists hated. Nitrogen was scarce in both countries and paid for in foreign currency. The planners wanted it spread: forty kilos a hectare over as much land as possible. Borlaug insisted on a hundred and twenty kilos on a third of the area. His reasoning, in the Canberra lecture, has nothing to do with agronomy. A peasant will not change his practice for a yield gain of ten or twenty percent, he said, but will for a doubling or tripling, so every demonstration plot must be made as spectacular as the plant allows. He wanted to shock the farmer out of his habits. He compared it to throwing the long pass on the first play of the game, and he had played and coached enough to mean it.

Hear that as someone who designs trials and it sounds like rigging: choose the dose for the size of the effect, at a level the country cannot afford to sustain. But his argument is better than that. A farmer watching his neighbour's field is running a comparison with one plot and no replication, across years whose weather alone can move a yield that far. A fifteen percent gain is invisible to him; he cannot tell it from the weather. A tripling can be seen from the road. Borlaug was sizing the effect to the statistical power of his audience. He added that the plots must be on farmers' own land, because nobody trusts a yield from a government station.

The fourth episode on the history of evidence was about a scarce drug. In 1948 the Medical Research Council had too little streptomycin for the patients who could have used it, and took the shortage as its justification for drawing lots. Borlaug in 1965 had the same shortage and the same opening. He could have given the scarce nitrogen to villages chosen by lot and learned what the seed did. He used it to persuade.

What turned persuasion into policy was politics, and Perkins is the source for it. Nehru died in May 1964. His successor, Lal Bahadur Shastri, moved the minister of steel, C. Subramaniam, to food and agriculture. War with Pakistan came in September 1965. That August the American government had put India's food aid on what amounted to a month-to-month footing and told Delhi that future shipments depended on what it did about fertiliser. The rains failed. In 1966 India imported ten million tons of grain. Shastri said on the radio that self-sufficiency in food mattered as much as defence.

Subramaniam, a few months into a subject he had never studied, did not wait. Before the 1966 harvest of the first imported seed was even in, he ordered eighteen thousand tons more. An Indian team went to Sonora, bought it field by field from Mexican growers, and loaded it on a Greek freighter called the Phaedra in July. Perkins calls it the largest seed purchase the developing world had seen. The president of the Rockefeller Foundation by then was Harrar, the same man who had tried to keep Borlaug out of Sonora, and he offered a hundred thousand dollars toward the cost on condition that the foundation carry no responsibility for whether the seed came up.

The government changed one more thing. It announced a floor price for wheat, breaking with a policy of holding grain cheap for the cities.

Then the rains came back. The 1968 harvest was the one that filled the schools. Borlaug's figures in Canberra that August were that the dwarf wheats covered eighteen percent of India's wheat land and had produced about forty percent of the crop. Villagers who had never bought fertiliser were queueing for it. "Black markets in fertilizers flourish and politicians tremble," he told the geneticists, with evident pleasure.

Chapter Six: One Factor in Seven

How much of that harvest was the seed? Start with the strongest form of the claim, which is Borlaug's own, and it is more careful than his admirers'. He compared 1968 with 1965, and said why: both were years of good rain. On that comparison India went from 12.3 million tons of wheat to somewhere between seventeen and nineteen, and Pakistan from 4.6 million to seven. He had curves from an Indian university showing the best native variety flattening at fifty kilos of nitrogen while the Mexican ones kept climbing to a hundred and sixty. And he never said the seed did it alone. The Canberra lecture lists seven biological factors, variety first, then fertility, cultivation, water, weeds, disease and insects, and after those the psychology of the farmer and the price he is paid. "There are no miracles in agricultural production," he said in Oslo. The dwarf was the catalyst, he said, and fertiliser the fuel.

There is one small crack even in his own telling. By the Nobel lecture, two years on, India's 1968 harvest had settled at 16.5 million tons. The seventeen to nineteen he gave in Canberra was the estimate of the man who most wanted it to be true. The gain was real and about a third. But the first count was his.

Now the prosecution. The anthropologist Glenn Davis Stone has argued, in a journal commentary I could not open and a short 2023 essay I could, that the standard story fails on its two main claims. India was importing wheat, he says, because of policy: cheap American grain suited a government that wanted low food prices for urban workers. And the wheat boom after 1967 came largely from the new floor price, which pulled land out of other crops, the lentils and beans called pulses among them. Once you look at all food grains together, in his reading, production after the drought went back to growing at the rate it had before. What did change was fertiliser. He gives eight kilos used per ton of food in 1965 and forty-four by 1980. India, on this account, traded free wheat for fertiliser bought with hard currency and called the result independence.

Parts of this Borlaug would have signed. He said in Canberra that easy American food aid had wrecked the beginnings of agricultural progress in more than one Latin American country, that finance ministries were addicted to it, and that India was spending two hundred and fifty million dollars a year on imported fertiliser with no prospect of making its own for a decade. The disagreement is about the counterfactual. Stone's is a before-and-after comparison of trends, and its weak point is the assumption that the old trend would have continued unaided. India's earlier growth in output, as I understand the agricultural economics and without a source in front of me, came largely from bringing more land under the plough, and by the sixties there was little left to bring. Borlaug said as much in Oslo. If that is right, the land had run out, so keeping the old growth rate took something new, and that something is the effect Stone says is missing.

Neither side has a control group. The intervention was a package of four things, seed, nitrogen, water and price, introduced together, on purpose, in the best-irrigated districts, in the year the drought broke. Perkins's own verdict on the seed is that it was possibly not entirely necessary and certainly not sufficient. More fertiliser on Indian wheats would have raised yields some way, up to the point where they fell over.

The best modern attempts to build the missing control are statistical. An economics working paper from 2018 by Douglas Gollin and two Danish colleagues uses the fact that high-yielding varieties arrived for different crops in different years, and that countries differ in which crops their climate suits, to compare eighty-four countries with themselves over time. It estimates that ten more percentage points of land under the new varieties raised income per head by about fifteen percent, lowered both births and deaths, and reduced the land under cultivation. The paper also discloses that Gollin chairs the impact panel of the research network that bred the varieties. A second study, in a health economics journal in 2020, matched the spread of modern varieties to survey records of some six hundred thousand births in thirty-seven countries and put the fall in infant mortality at 2.4 to 5.3 percentage points against a starting rate of eighteen percent. Its authors translate that into three to six million infant deaths averted a year by 2000, with the caveat that their sample is rural families who never moved.

These are still inferences from places that were not chosen at random. I think the effect was real and big. I think "a billion lives" is a slogan. And the uncertainty between those two statements is a thing Borlaug chose. What did the choice hide?

Chapter Seven: What the Harvest Cannot See

A harvest counts tons. Every objection to Borlaug concerns something a ton does not record, beginning with whose ton it was.

In Mexico the decision of 1948 settled it. The new wheat went to the irrigated estates of Sonora, and the other half of Mexican farming, which Perkins describes as communal rain-fed plots of maize and beans worked with little capital, sits outside the wheat story altogether. In India the dwarfs covered eighteen percent of the wheat land, the watered part, in Punjab and its neighbours. The political scientist Francine Frankel, whom Perkins leans on, made the early case, as I recall her argument, that the new farming widened the gap between those with wells and credit and those without. Vandana Shiva's book The Violence of the Green Revolution, which I know only from memory because the open copies are locked, carries that further and ties the technology to Punjab's ecological exhaustion and to its political bloodshed in the eighties. Her ecological charge has aged better than her political one.

Borlaug's reply, in Oslo, was a question: is it just to blame the new wheat for failing to cure every social ill accumulated since Adam and Eve? As rhetoric that works. As an answer it concedes the substance. He was hired to raise a national total, he raised it, and the distribution was somebody else's department. Perkins's title says whose. The governments that paid wanted grain they did not have to buy abroad, and they got it. That leaves the infants in the mortality study no less alive. But if the wheat went where the water already was, what happened where there was none?

The seeds did not travel to Africa. Gollin's paper reports that the Asian rice varieties proved poorly suited to African conditions, that maize which had done well in Mexico disappointed on African farms, and that for cassava, sorghum and millet, which much of the continent actually eats, there was no stock of research to draw on; for some of them international breeding did not begin until 1975. Wide adaptation had meant adaptation across day length, under irrigation, with nitrogen in the soil. In the scurvy episode that opened the nutrition series, the navy swapped lemons for limes because nobody knew which part of the cure was the cure. Here the world tried to export the seed, the portable part of a four-part package, to places without the canal, the fertiliser plant or the floor price.

Borlaug had written the warning himself. The Canberra lecture ends with the conditions a country must meet before the method will work, among them political stability, prices that reward the farmer, and fertiliser on hand. India and Pakistan in 1966 met them. Many countries could not. As I recall it, he spent a good part of his last decades in Africa on a privately funded programme, arguing that the missing ingredients were roads and affordable fertiliser. Stone's view is that the African countries the revolution missed "should consider themselves lucky." Given the infant mortality estimates, I cannot agree with him. But the continent is the nearest thing this story has to an untreated arm, and nobody designed it.

A comparison of that kind shows what the treatment added. It says nothing about what the treated will pay later, and two bills were already visible in 1968. In Canberra Borlaug urged India to develop what he called its vast subterranean water resources as a buffer against bad monsoons. India did. From memory, and no closer than that, satellite measurements published around 2009 showed the water table under northwestern India falling year on year, with the wheat and rice rotation he celebrated as the usual suspect. An aquifer reports to its auditor decades late. The other bill he presented himself. The Canberra lecture has a section headed negative effects, written in the year of the triumph, warning that high-yielding wheat and rice were about to drive cotton out of Pakistan because nobody had done the equivalent research on cotton. Stone's complaint about pulses is the same mechanism: a price and a seed for one crop are a tax on its neighbours in the rotation, and the diet that results has more calories in it and is narrower.

Chapter Eight: Time

So what did the courier add? Go back through the decisions and they are one decision made five times. Grow two generations a year where the textbook said one. Release a variety before it is finished. Ship the Mexican wheat as it stands and skip the years of crossing with local stock. Put the whole nitrogen ration on a third of the land so the result cannot be missed. Order eighteen thousand tons before the first crop is cut. Each time he gave up some understanding of the thing to have it sooner. He said so, and he never claimed the time was more than borrowed: in Oslo he told his audience that the new wheat had bought a breathing space of perhaps three decades. I am a man in a hurry, he quoted approvingly from the president of Pakistan, and he told the Canberra audience that slow academic methods would let the world disintegrate.

In a clinician's vocabulary he went from a promising phase two straight to mass treatment, on the argument that the control arm was famine. Whether famine was truly the alternative in 1966 is the thing Stone disputes and Borlaug assumed. Nobody could run that experiment, and I do not think he was wrong to refuse it. A man standing in Delhi in the second failed monsoon, holding seed that triples yields on irrigated land, who waits five years for a cleaner estimate, has made a choice too.

But speed has a specific price, and here it was attribution. Because seed, nitrogen, water and price moved together, the credit went to the component with a name and a breeder, and the lesson the world drew was a lesson about seeds. The lesson Borlaug drew was different. Ask him in those two lectures what made the difference between programmes that worked and programmes that did not, inside the same country with the same varieties, and he says leadership, price policy, and scientists willing to sweat in a field instead of piling paper in an office. That is a claim about organisation. The harvest could not test it either.

Now the series' question can be answered for him. The harvest audited the one thing he was asked to deliver, quickly and in public, and he passed. It was blind to distribution, to the water table, to the neighbouring crops and to the countries outside his entry criteria, and on each of those the report came in decades late or not at all. What is unusual about Borlaug among the doers on this shelf is that he wrote most of those blind spots down himself, in the year he won, in a section with a plain heading. Then he kept going, because he had never been asked to stop for them.

Chapter Nine: Dose

The Canberra lecture of 1968 is the thing to read. It is thirty-six pages, it circulates free, and it beats the Oslo one because it was written for wheat geneticists and he is trying to provoke them. The football coach is all there, along with the table of seed shipments and the list of conditions. From the Nobel lecture take the last third, on population. Perkins is dry and fair; his tenth chapter has the science, his eighth has Indian policy, and the British half of the book can be left alone. Stone's essay takes ten minutes. The biographies I could not judge.

A method that triples something at the places where it was first tried arrives at the next place under its own name, and the name is usually the part that fits in a crate. The managers who were ready, the supplies that came on time and the ministry that returned calls were all at the first sites, and none of them is in the title. Borlaug's wheat reached Africa as seed. In Punjab it had been seed, a canal, a fertiliser ration and a floor price, and only one of the four had a variety number.
