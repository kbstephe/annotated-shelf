Second Thoughts on James Burnham: Orwell Audits a Forecaster

Orwell's 1946 essay read as a method for telling a forecaster from a fan, with the twist that Orwell's own prediction at the end of it took forty-five years to come in.

26 August 2026

Chapter One: The Ledger

Suppose a colleague has been confidently wrong about the same thing four times in a row, and each time the wrong answer pointed in the same direction. You would not, at that point, be very interested in his fifth answer. You would be interested in the direction. Misses that scatter are bad luck or a hard problem; misses that all lean the same way are a finding about the person doing the forecasting, and the polite thing, the clinical thing, is to stop arguing with the forecasts and start looking for the lean.

That is the method in the essay this episode is about, and most of us never apply it, least of all to ourselves. This shelf has now met James Burnham three times. Episode seven found his managerial revolution, the claim that command was migrating from owners to administrators, and set it against Whyte. Episodes ten and eleven read The Machiavellians, the best of his books, and caught its school taking fascist professorships. Both times George Orwell's nineteen forty-six essay in Polemic, Second Thoughts on James Burnham, was cited the way you cite a verdict, in a subordinate clause. It has earned a reading of its own, and the verdict is the least interesting thing in it. What Orwell built is an instrument, and the instrument has a use far past Burnham.

Orwell, for the two people listening who need the biography: the Eton scholarship boy who went to Burma as an imperial policeman and came back sick of it, who washed dishes in Paris and went down the mines in Wigan and took a fascist bullet through the throat in Spain, and who by nineteen forty-six was thin, tubercular, widowed, and writing at a rate that suggests he knew how long he had. He had four years left. In them he produced most of the essays people still read and the novel that everybody reads, and the essay on Burnham sits at the hinge between the two, because it is where he worked out, in public, the psychology he then installed in Nineteen Eighty-Four. He had reviewed The Managerial Revolution in nineteen forty-four and taken its thesis seriously, doubts and all. The word in the title is second thoughts. He had changed his mind, and the essay is the record of why.

Here is the ledger he lays out, presented as flatly as he presents it. In nineteen forty, writing The Managerial Revolution, Burnham treated German victory as effectively settled and described the coming world on that basis. In the supplementary note of nineteen forty-two he wrote as if the Soviet Union were already beaten. By the winter of forty-four, in articles and the book that followed The Machiavellians, the Red Army was moving fast through eastern Europe, and Burnham's prophecy switched: now the future was Russian conquest of the whole of Eurasia. Orwell simply puts the dates next to the forecasts and lets them sit there. Every forecast was written at the high-water mark of the power it predicted, and every one mistook the mark for the tide.

So the lean is established before the theory is touched. That is the first thing to take from the essay: Orwell audits the track record before he argues the model. Most critics do it the other way around, and so, in my experience, do most journal clubs.

Chapter Two: The Diagnosis

What does a run of same-direction misses tell you about the forecaster? Orwell's answer comes in one sentence that this shelf has already quoted, about Burnham predicting a continuation of whatever is currently happening, and then in a second, cruder one that I think is the better instrument: whoever is winning at the moment will always seem to be invincible. He gives the examples with a straight face. If the Japanese have taken south Asia, they will hold it for ever. If the Germans have taken Tobruk, they will take Cairo. Then he names the mechanism, and the name has aged well: power worship. It blurs political judgement, he says, because it leads almost unavoidably to the belief that present trends will continue.

Now, a modern reader will hear that and reach for the vocabulary of the cognitive-bias literature, recency, extrapolation, the hot hand, and the reach is fair as far as it goes. But Orwell's claim is narrower and nastier than a bias. A bias is a lean everyone has. He is saying Burnham's lean has a direction, that it does not just extend trends but extends them on behalf of the strong, and that this tells you something about what Burnham wanted. The evidence he uses is not the forecasts at all. It is the prose. And the passage he chooses is the one I would have chosen, which is a little alarming.

In nineteen forty-five Burnham published an essay called Lenin's Heir, in which the man who had for years described Stalin as a jumped-up bureaucrat, the gray clerk who had betrayed the revolution, suddenly rediscovered him as a colossal figure, and Orwell quotes the paragraph where the rediscovery happens. It is a description of a Kremlin banquet. Burnham lingers on the toasts, the vast table, the silent secret police standing behind each guest, and, as the backdrop to all of it, the starving multitudes of the winter outside. Burnham presents this as analysis, a sober register of the scale of the thing. Orwell reads it as what it is: prose that has fallen in love. He calls the tone a sort of fascinated admiration. The starving multitudes are there for contrast, the way a painter puts shadow behind a face, and the face is Stalin's.

Then Orwell does the move that turns a review into an essay. He asks why Burnham needs the shadow at all, and answers that the cruelty is not incidental to the admiration but its source. Burnham finds Stalin admirable, in this passage, in proportion to the scale of the brutality, because to a mind of a certain kind, naked force looks like the realest thing there is. Everything softer, law, custom, the possibility that people might refuse, registers as sentiment, and sentiment is what a realist is proud of having outgrown. So the realist's forecasts lean toward the whip, not because he has weighed the evidence and the whip won, but because the whip is the only evidence his instrument is built to detect. Orwell's phrase for the whole condition, applied to the intellectual class and not only to Burnham, is that it is a major mental disease with roots partly in cowardice and partly in the worship of power, which, he adds, is not fully separable from cowardice.

That last clause is where the essay stops being about one American. Put it against the shelf. Jackall's managers in Moral Mazes had a rule, look up and look around, and the looking was worship of a very small local power; Whyte's organization man surrendered his interior to the group because the group was, locally, what was winning. The disease Orwell names at the scale of world history is the ordinary condition of the middle of any hierarchy, and his diagnosis is the one this series has kept reprinting in the form of a dosage warning. Diagnostic doses of the cynical frameworks are medicine. Identity doses are poison. Orwell caught the overdose in a distinguished patient, and what the banquet passage shows is what the overdose looks like on the page: the analyst's sentences start to glow when the subject is cruel.

Chapter Three: The Steelman, Which Orwell Supplies Himself

Is the diagnosis fair? The test of a critic is what he concedes, and Orwell concedes a great deal, more than the essay's reputation suggests. He grants that Burnham has probably been right about the present and the immediate past, that for fifty years the general drift has been toward oligarchy, toward the concentration of economic and political power in fewer hands and the decline of the representative institutions that were supposed to check it. He grants the managerial class is real. He grants, and this is the concession that matters, that the world Burnham describes, three great super-states, each too large to be conquered, each run by a self-selected elite, is a plausible destination for the drift if nothing interrupts it. He simply refuses to grant that nothing will interrupt it.

That is a lot to give away. A lesser critic would have used the four bad forecasts to throw out the structure. Orwell keeps the structure, discards the forecast, and treats the forecast's failure as information about the forecaster rather than about the world. That is the separation this shelf has tried to keep in its own hands, between what the author claims, what the evidence supports, and what the reader concludes, and Orwell keeps it under pressure, about a man whose politics he found repellent. His Burnham is not a fool. His Burnham is a mind of real gifts running a good structural model through a bad instrument.

And the essay contains one more concession that is really an accusation against the rest of us. Orwell notes, almost in passing, that Burnham never stops to ask why people want power. He treats power hunger as a natural instinct that needs no more explanation than hunger for food. Orwell thinks it is a pathology, historically conditioned, no more natural than the want of a title, and that a theory which cannot see that will always mistake the current shape of the disease for the permanent shape of the species. The shelf has a running version of this objection. Episode eleven's case against The Machiavellians was that the iron law of oligarchy is iron only if you stop counting, that organizations have held oligarchy at bay for generations by engineering rival power inside their walls. Orwell got there first, in nineteen forty-six, in one sentence: the realist has assumed the constant he most needed to test.

Chapter Four: The Prediction at the End

Here the essay turns on its author, and it is the part nobody quotes. Having demonstrated that a forecaster's lean can be read off his forecasts, Orwell ends by making a forecast of his own. Burnham's managerial society, he argues, is an unstable form, because slavery is no longer a stable basis for human society, and so the Russian regime will either democratize itself, or it will perish. He does not put a date on it. He suggests, in a hedge that is easy to miss, that it could take some time.

Now score it, the way he scored Burnham. In nineteen forty-six the sentence was wishful. Ten years later, after Hungary, it was wrong. In nineteen sixty-eight, after Prague, it was still wrong, and Burnham's three-bloc world was, at that point, the better description of the map. The prediction came in, in a form Orwell did not specify, in nineteen ninety-one, forty-five years after he made it, which is a longer horizon than any of the four Burnham forecasts he had lined up for execution. If you had audited Orwell in nineteen seventy, by his own method, you would have had a forecaster whose one big call had been open and losing for a quarter century, and whose lean was plain: he predicted the fall of the thing he hated.

I raise this not to even the score but because it is the actual lesson, and the essay's admirers, who tend to read it as Orwell winning, mostly miss it. The track-record audit is a real instrument. It found Burnham's lean, and Burnham's lean was real, and his later career confirmed it: the harder line always, the Cold War as a fight already decided, the medal from Reagan. But the instrument cannot, by itself, distinguish a lean from a long-horizon call. Both look identical for as long as the horizon stays open. The only thing that separated them, in the end, was that one of the forecasters had a mechanism and the other had a mood. Orwell's prediction had a reason attached: a state that runs on terror cannot get the effort out of its people that one running on some measure of consent can, and the gap will compound. Burnham's predictions had no mechanism except momentum. That is what the same-direction misses were detecting. Not that he was wrong, but that he had no way of ever being told he was.

Which changes what the audit is for. It grades nobody. It finds the forecasters who cannot lose, and those are the only ones you should never trust.

Chapter Five: The Case Against, and the Revival

The essay faces two counts, and the second is livelier now than it has been since nineteen forty-six.

Count one is about method, and it is the objection Orwell's own instrument invites. Reading a man's motive off his prose is a diagnosis, not a demonstration. The banquet passage is presented as a tell, and it is a tell, but a critic who reads tells has to accept that he is doing psychology on a text, that the text cannot answer back, and that the same reading applied to Orwell would find, in the lovingly detailed tortures of the Ministry of Love, a fascination he would not have enjoyed having named. Orwell's biographer Michael Shelden calls the essay a brilliant criticism of the whole concept of power worship, and he is right, but a demolition of a concept is not a fair trial of a man, and the essay knows it and does not care. Orwell chose that risk with his eyes open, and you should read him knowing he chose it.

Count two is that the world has spent the last decade deciding Burnham was right after all, and the deciding has come from a direction Orwell would have recognized. The managerial revolution has had a revival on the American right. Samuel Francis, whose posthumous Leviathan and Its Enemies is essentially Burnham applied to the postwar United States, made the managerial elite the villain of a whole political tendency, and the tendency now has its own writers. N. S. Lyons, in a long twenty twenty-three essay called The China Convergence, argues that the American and Chinese systems are converging on the same form, a hegemonic, self-reinforcing system of overlapping managerial elite interests, hard in Beijing, soft in Washington, and builds the argument from The Managerial Revolution outward. Read a certain way, this is Burnham vindicated: the structure Orwell conceded was true has become the thing everyone can see, and the forecast Orwell refused has come in under another name.

I do not think it holds, and the best reason it does not comes from the left, from Branko Milanovic, who marked the book's eightieth anniversary by re-reading it as an economist. Milanovic gives Burnham full credit for the structural move, grounding both capitalist and managerial society in who actually controls production, and then points out that on every predictive claim the book is simply wrong: capitalism did not collapse into managerialism, it won, globally, in nineteen eighty-nine; the three-bloc world never arrived; and even China, the managerial regime's supposed proof case, gets most of its value and employment from a private sector that the state constrains but did not build. His verdict on the American revival is blunt: the dominant class here still owns its property, defends it, and controls the government, which is what a capitalist class does, whatever its employees' job titles. The managers Burnham saw rising are, in Milanovic's reading, exactly where episode seven of this shelf left them: better paid than the workers, more comfortable, and not in command.

And the revival has Burnham's lean. It finds the managerial regime everywhere, describes it as total, treats its consolidation as the thing that is happening and therefore the thing that will continue. Lyons opens with an Orwell epigraph, the one about the constant struggle to see what is in front of one's nose, from the essay on language. He does not engage the Orwell essay about himself. It would have been the harder reading.

Chapter Six: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

What is Second Thoughts on James Burnham, then, and how much of it should you take? It is the best short instrument I know for reading a forecaster, and it is short: an hour's reading, free, in print everywhere. Read it whole, once, and then go back over the closing pages with a pencil, because that is where the author turns the instrument on himself without saying so, and where this episode found its conclusion. Do not read it as the man who was right about Burnham. He caught Burnham's lean and stayed wrong about Burnham's world for forty-five years, and the reason his wrong answer eventually came good is the reason the essay is worth your hour.

The dose is diagnostic and it is portable. This week, find one forecast you are living by, a projection in a grant, a trajectory a consultant showed the board, a colleague's confident read of where the department is going, and run the ledger on it. Not the argument, the ledger. When were the last three times this source called a direction, and which way did the misses lean? If they scatter, you have an ordinary forecaster working a hard problem, and you can argue the model. If they lean, stop arguing the model, because you have found something the model cannot tell you: what its author wants, or, more often, what he is afraid of. And then the harder half, which the essay demonstrates rather than states. Ask what mechanism the forecast has. A forecast with a mechanism can be wrong for a long time and still be a forecast. A forecast with only momentum is not a prediction at all; it is a description of the present with a date stamped on it, and the person offering it has arranged to never be told he was wrong.

The question to carry past Monday is the one Orwell did not quite ask, because he was too busy being right. Where, in your own work, are you predicting a continuation of the thing that is happening, and which power, exactly, does the continuation flatter? The ledger works on the person keeping it. That is the part of the instrument he left out of the instructions.
