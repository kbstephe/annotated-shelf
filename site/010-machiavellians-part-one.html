The Machiavellians (Burnham) 1 of 2: Machiavelli, Mosca, Pareto, Sorel

Part one of two on James Burnham's 1943 rehabilitation of the elite theorists – Dante's pamphlet in disguise, Machiavelli's scandalous honesty, Mosca's ruling class, Pareto's reasons that come second, and Sorel's myth.

24 August 2026

Chapter One: The Sacred Poet's Real Estate

Begin with the least likely opening any book on this shelf has attempted: a reading of Dante. Around thirteen twelve, in exile, the poet of the Divine Comedy wrote a Latin treatise called De Monarchia, on the government of the world. Its formal argument is as high-minded as argument gets: humanity's goal is the fullness of civilization; that goal requires universal peace; peace requires a single world government under a universal monarch, whose authority descends from God directly and not through the Pope. Serene, abstract, deduced from first principles – a treatise seemingly written from the moon. Now add the biography. Dante was a Florentine politician of the losing faction, banished under sentence of death, his property confiscated, his hopes pinned entirely on the one power capable of breaking his enemies and restoring his party: the German emperor, then preparing to descend into Italy against the Pope and the Florentine establishment. Set the treatise beside the situation and the celestial argument resolves, clause by clause, into a partisan pamphlet: the universal monarch has a name and an army; the argument against papal authority is the argument against the specific pope backing the specific government that exiled the author. The treatise about eternal peace is a weapon in a faction fight, and – this is the detail that earns the dwell time – it is a better weapon for not looking like one. This is where James Burnham opens The Machiavellians, published in nineteen forty-three, two years after The Managerial Revolution, and the choice of opening announces a method. Every piece of political writing, Burnham proposes, has a formal meaning – what it says, its surface argument about justice, rights, destiny, peace – and a real meaning: what it does, whom it arms, which concrete struggle for power it serves. The formal meaning is addressed to the audience; the real meaning is executed on them. And the mark of the tradition Burnham calls Machiavellian – the only tradition, he claims, that deserves the name of political science – is the refusal to read at the formal level. Episode four of this feed met the same razor wearing modern clothes: the purpose of a system is what it does, not what it says. Burnham's book is that principle applied to political speech itself, and his first exhibit was chosen for maximum sacrilege: even Dante, the most sublime formal writer who ever lived, is – read at the level of what the text was for – a lobbyist in terza rima.

Chapter Two: The Scandal of Saying It Plainly

The tradition's founder earned its name by committing the one sin power never forgives: he wrote down what everyone does. Niccolò Machiavelli was a working diplomat of the Florentine republic – sixteen years of missions to kings, popes, and usurpers, watching at close range how territory actually changes hands – until the republic fell, and torture and exile gave him the leisure to write up his field notes. Burnham's Machiavelli is not the cartoon poisoner of the adjective, and the rehabilitation matters for everything that follows. What Machiavelli did, on this reading, was transplant into politics the operation Galileo was performing on physics in the same culture a lifetime later: separate the study of what happens from the sermon about what ought to happen. His books describe how principalities are seized, how republics decay, how armed prophets succeed and unarmed ones are destroyed – as observed regularities, stated without moral flinching, checkable against the record. The armed-prophet finding is worth walking to its end, because Machiavelli had watched both arms of the experiment from inside the same city. Savonarola, the friar who ruled Florence by sermon, remade the city's morals in four years on the strength of preaching alone – and burned in the Piazza della Signoria the year the preaching stopped working, because a prophet without instruments of force governs exactly as long as the crowd stays persuaded, and crowds do not stay persuaded. The princes who lasted, Machiavelli observed across every case he could gather, were the ones who, when belief cooled, had something else. Stated as a regularity: new orders of things survive on conviction plus enforcement, never conviction alone. A moralist reports the fact with grief or not at all. Machiavelli reports it the way a physician reports a mortality rate, and the reporting is what the centuries could not forgive. The description was not cynicism; Machiavelli's own loyalties ran hot – he was a republican who wanted Italy free of foreign armies, and said so. The scandal was the separation itself. A science of politics, like a science of anything, has to report the mechanism whether or not the mechanism is edifying, and four centuries of respectable opinion have called that reporting wicked – have made his name a synonym for the behavior he merely documented – because the formal level of political talk depends for its life on never being described from outside. The men Burnham assembles in the rest of the book are the moderns who reopened the file: a Sicilian jurist, an Italian engineer-economist, a French engineer turned philosopher of violence, a German-Italian sociologist. Their findings differ; their operation is the same one, run with nineteenth-century tools – statistics, comparative history, the new sociology – on the claims modern states make about themselves. And the largest claim available to test, in their century and ours, was democracy's: government of the people, by the people. The first of them took that claim into the archive the way Machiavelli took princely virtue into the dispatches, and came back with the finding the whole school rests on.

Chapter Three: The Smallest Army That Always Wins

Gaetano Mosca spent a career in Italian law, parliament, and political science working out a single thesis, and Burnham states it as the school's foundation stone: in every society past the tribal – every one, without exception in the record – an organized minority rules the disorganized majority. Not may rule, tends to rule: rules. The claim's engine is not a sneer about the masses but a proposition about organization that this series has been circling since episode one. A minority that can coordinate – that knows its members, divides its functions, moves on a plan – operates, against a majority that cannot, with the advantage of an army against a crowd. A thousand men who act as one unit defeat a million who act as a million. And the majority, by the nature of the case, cannot coordinate: its numbers are exactly what makes it a crowd, and the moment some fraction of it organizes to contest the rulers, that fraction has become – run the definition – a new organized minority, bidding for the same position. The ruling class, Mosca called the standing winner of this asymmetry, and he traced its forms through history: warrior aristocracies, priesthoods, landowners, the wealthy, and – arriving in his own century – the class credentialed to operate the machinery of the modern state. His anatomy of the class has a refinement that matters for everything this shelf has read, because Mosca saw that the visible rulers are the smaller half of the structure. Below the commanding stratum sits a second, much larger one – the officials, officers, managers, and professionals who transmit the top's decisions and run the daily machinery – and Mosca judged this second stratum the true seat of a society's stability: dynasties and cabinets can fall and be replaced in a season, but let the second stratum rot, or defect, or stop believing, and the regime is finished no matter who holds the palace. A reader of this series has met the second stratum repeatedly without the name. It is Whyte's organization men, Jackall's mazed managers, the middle whose conversion the Social Ethic secured – and Mosca's point relocates the significance of all three books at a stroke: the contest that decides a society's character is not the crowd against the rulers, which the asymmetry settles in advance, but the recruitment, training, and loyalty of the stratum in between. Burnham's readers in nineteen forty-three could be trusted to hear the echo; his previous book had given that last cohort a name. But Mosca's subtler instrument is the one this shelf can use, and it explains Dante's pamphlet from a new side. No ruling class, he observed, ever says it rules by organizational advantage. Every one presents its position through what he called a political formula – a doctrine that translates brute position into rightful authority: the will of the gods, the divine right of kings, the mandate of heaven, the consent of the governed. The formula is not a cynical lie; that is the finding's teeth. The rulers believe it, the ruled believe it, and it functions – it stabilizes, legitimates, and recruits – precisely because it is believed. It is the formal meaning, institutionalized; the Social Ethic that episode six found converting Whyte's organization men, the we-do-not-make-mistakes identity that episode four found at the Post Office's System Five, are political formulas in Mosca's exact sense, working at corporate scale. A formula can rot, and when it does the class it clothed is usually near its end. But no class has ever governed naked. The majority is ruled by organization and consoled by doctrine, and the doctrine is sincere on both sides of the transaction.

Chapter Four: The Reasons That Come Second

If Mosca supplied the school's sociology, Vilfredo Pareto supplied its psychology, and it is the piece a clinician will fight hardest, so it deserves the careful version. Pareto was an engineer and a mathematical economist – the Pareto of the distribution and the optimum – who turned, late, to a vast treatise on how people actually behave, and its central finding can be put in one sentence: most human conduct is non-logical, and nearly all of it is rationalized. Non-logical, not illogical – the distinction carries the whole argument. Logical conduct, in Pareto's strict sense, selects means that objectively connect to ends, the way an engineer sizes a beam; it exists, and it is rare, mostly confined to technical work. The rest of conduct flows from stable underlying dispositions – sentiments, drives, cast of mind; he called their observable expressions residues – and then, because humans are the animal that cannot bear to act without reasons, the conduct gets dressed, after the fact, in justifications. Those he called derivations: the theories, principles, and arguments we produce to explain decisions already made in the sentiment. The derivations vary wildly – this century's natural law is that century's divine will is this morning's data-driven strategy – while the residues underneath barely move; and so, Pareto concluded, to study political argument as if the arguments caused the behavior is to study the paint to predict the ship. Before the objection forms, mark what the claim is not. It is not that people are stupid, or that reasoning never occurs; Pareto spent half his life doing mathematical economics and knew logical conduct when he saw it. The claim is about sequence – that in the domains that matter most to people, the conclusion typically arrives first and the reasoning is dispatched to fetch it – and the clinical literature of this listener's own field has spent the decades since accumulating embarrassing support: the confabulation studies, the motivated-reasoning experiments, the reliable discovery that expert judgment and expert justification can be varied independently. A physician who has watched a tumor board talk itself into the plan the chief proposed in the first minute has seen a derivation assembled live, by six well-trained minds, in perfect sincerity. Pareto's instrument does not say the reasons are worthless. It says the reasons are evidence about the speaker, not about the world – read them the way a diagnostician reads a symptom: not for what they assert, but for what is producing them. Burnham takes from this a corollary aimed straight at reformers: programs, ideologies, and manifestos are derivations almost entirely – you learn what a movement will do not from its stated theory but from the sentiments of the men it selects and the position they are fighting from. And Pareto's second export gives the school its dynamics. Elites, he found, are never permanent; history is a graveyard of aristocracies, and the mechanism of their death is the slow change in the mixture of types at the top – his shorthand, borrowed from Machiavelli, was foxes and lions: the men of combination, maneuver, and deal, versus the men of force, loyalty, and faith. Regimes of foxes rot toward cleverness without nerve; regimes of lions toward rigidity without adaptation; and the circulation of elites – the displacement of one governing mixture by another, sometimes by co-option, sometimes by catastrophe – is, on this account, what political history is made of, underneath whatever the derivations were saying while it happened. History, in the four words of his that outlived the four volumes, is a graveyard of aristocracies – and the sentence's tense is the argument: not battlefield, graveyard; the outcome is never in doubt, only the schedule. A ruling class dies, in his account, mostly by its own filters. It stops admitting the able and ambitious from below – the very types whose energy built it – and the excluded talent does not evaporate; it accumulates outside the walls, unemployed and increasingly organized, until the class within, grown clever and bloodless, meets the counter-elite it manufactured by refusing it. Circulation blocked is revolution scheduled.

Chapter Five: The Story That Moves the Crowd

The school's strangest member supplies its strangest instrument, and Burnham needs him for the piece Mosca and Pareto leave unexplained: if the majority can never rule, what are the majority's great movements? Georges Sorel – French, an engineer for twenty-five years before he began to write – studied the revolutionary syndicalism of his day and located its engine not in its economic program, which he thought mostly fantasy, but in what he called its myth: the general strike, the image of one apocalyptic day when all labor stops and the old world falls. The myth, Sorel insisted, is not a prediction and cannot be refuted by pointing out that the day never comes – that is like refuting a battle flag. It is a body of images that organizes the sentiments of a mass of men, binds them into a fighting unit, and makes coordinated heroism possible for people who will never attend a strategy meeting. Sorel drew the distinction that keeps the instrument honest: a myth is not a utopia. A utopia is an intellectual product – a designed future, detailed and comparable and therefore criticizable, the work of theorists addressed to other theorists. A myth is none of these things; it is closer to a battle hymn than a blueprint, and arguing with it is a category error the myth's carriers experience not as refutation but as confirmation that the arguer is an enemy. The distinction predicts something observable: utopias generate committees and amendments; myths generate discipline and sacrifice; and a movement can be located on the map by which of the two its rank and file would die for. Movements run on myths the way engines run on fuel, and the analytical payoff arrives when Sorel's instrument is bolted to Mosca's: a myth is how an aspiring counter-elite solves the crowd's coordination problem – the political formula of a ruling class that does not rule yet. Burnham, writing in nineteen forty-three, had before him the century's demonstration cases, and his readers did not need them named. What matters for this series is what the assembled machine now claims. Political speech is instrumental, top to bottom: the rulers hold a formula, the challengers hold a myth, the individual holds derivations, and at no level – this is the school's full bid – is the talk what it formally claims to be. Which sets the trap this episode has been walking toward, and part two exists to spring. If every doctrine is a formula, a myth, or a derivation, then what of the doctrine called democracy – and what, precisely, is Burnham's own book? The school's answer to the first question sounds, at first hearing, like the end of all political hope: government by the people has never existed and can never exist; the sovereign majority is the political formula of the modern West, sincere, functional, and false. Yet the book's subtitle is Defenders of Freedom, and Burnham meant it – the claim of part two is that this bleakest of schools, and only this school, explains how real liberty is actually produced and kept. Whether he proves it, what Orwell said about the man making the argument, what became of these ideas in our own century's stranger corners, and the verdict with the dose – next time, The Machiavellians, part two.
