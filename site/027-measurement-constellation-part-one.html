The measurement constellation 1 of 2: Ridgway, Goodhart, Campbell, one law found three times

Three short papers staged as a rediscovery – Ridgway's 1956 catalogue of what a single number does to an employment office and a Soviet factory, Goodhart's 1975 aside about the money supply that collapsed the moment it was targeted, and Campbell's 1976 law from a president's war on crime, Chicago's honest police chief, and the body count – with the argument that the law is not about measurement at all but about the moment a number acquires a consequence for the person who reports it.

Chapter One: The Employment Office

In the late nineteen forties a state employment agency in an American city had a problem that its managers thought they had solved. The interviewers who matched jobless applicants to employers' vacancies were being evaluated by a single figure, the number of interviews each conducted, and the figure had been chosen because it was the one thing the office could count without argument. The interviewers, being rational, conducted a great many interviews. They were short. Applicants were seen, noted, and sent away, and the count rose, and the number of people actually placed in jobs, which was the purpose of the office, did not. So the managers changed the measure. They began to evaluate interviewers on placements, and then on a set of eight figures, placements among them, and the sociologist Peter Blau, who was in the building watching, recorded what happened next. In one section the interviewers, now competing with each other for placements, began to hide the job openings that came across their desks, keeping the good vacancies in a drawer so that a colleague could not fill them first, and the section's placement rate fell. In another section, whose members had decided among themselves not to compete, the openings were shared and the placement rate held. The number had been improved. The office had been made worse. Blau published it in nineteen fifty-five in a book called The Dynamics of Bureaucracy, and the following year a researcher named V. F. Ridgway, whom almost nobody has heard of, read it and saw that it was an instance of something.

That is the first of three discoveries this episode is about, and I want to tell all three before saying what they discovered, because the interest of the constellation is not the law, which you will have heard stated in one form or another, but that it was found three times in three fields by three people who did not cite each other, and named after the last two. The shelf has been circling this mechanism since its first week. Davies's accountability sink, from episode two, was a process nobody could override; Grove's paired indicators, from three episodes ago, were a practitioner's defence against something he had seen in a fab and did not name. The thing they were both handling has a literature, and the literature is short, and this is it.

Chapter Two: Ridgway, Nineteen Fifty-Six

Ridgway's paper is seven pages long. It appeared in the second issue of a new journal, Administrative Science Quarterly, under the title Dysfunctional Consequences of Performance Measurements, and it is organised as a taxonomy of three ways to measure a person or a unit and what each one does. He begins by conceding, as everyone in this literature begins, that quantitative measures are useful and that the problem is not their existence but the confidence placed in them by people who have not looked at what they do.

The first way is the single criterion, and the employment office is his opening case. A single figure, he says, has the property of being the thing that is done: whatever the number counts, the organisation will produce, and whatever it does not count will be neglected, not from malice but because a person with one number to meet has been told, more clearly than any memo could tell them, what the job is. His second case for this is drawn from Joseph Berliner's interviews with Soviet managers who had left the country, published the same year, and from the general literature on the Soviet plan. A factory whose quota was set in tons produced heavy goods; set in units, it produced small ones; set by value, expensive ones, and in each case the quota was met and the economy was short of whatever the quota had not counted. The managers also, Berliner found, hoarded materials and labour against the next quota, rushed production into the last days of the plan period in what was called storming, and treated the customer as an irrelevance, since the customer was not the plan. The joke of the era, which Ridgway does not tell and which I include because it is the taxonomy in one image, was the cartoon of a nail factory whose plan was set in tonnage and which met it by producing one enormous nail.

Ridgway's second way is the multiple criterion, which is what the employment office turned to, and he argues that it does not solve the problem but moves it. A person given several numbers has to trade them off, and will do so according to which ones are noticed, which are easy, and which have consequences, and the trade-off is invisible to the people who set the criteria, since they see only the numbers and not the choices made between them. The Soviet manager with a dozen plan indices was not better controlled than the manager with one; he was a juggler, and he juggled for his own reasons.

The third way is the composite, in which several measures are weighted and summed into a single score, and Ridgway's objection to it is that the weights are a decision about what the organisation is for, made by whoever built the index, and that the people being scored will learn the weights and produce the score the same way they produced the single number, with the difference that now nobody can say what the score means. He ends without a remedy. His conclusion is that measurement changes what it measures, that this is a property of measurement and not a defect of the people measured, and that an organisation should know it before it builds its indicators, and the paper was read by almost nobody for thirty years.

Chapter Three: Goodhart, Nineteen Seventy-Five

The second discovery was made in a different country, in a different discipline, and in a footnote, more or less. Charles Goodhart was an economist at the Bank of England who was sent to a conference at the Reserve Bank of Australia in nineteen seventy-five to describe the British experience of trying to run monetary policy by targeting the money supply. The British had, from nineteen seventy-one, reformed their banking rules and begun to use the quantity of money in circulation as the thing they steered by, on the grounds that there was a stable, observable relationship between that quantity and the things they actually cared about, prices and output. The relationship had been real. It had been measured over years of data and it held. And then the Bank started using it, announcing targets for the money supply and acting to hit them, and it stopped holding, because the banks and their customers, who now knew what the Bank was watching, changed their behaviour to suit, and the quantity being measured no longer meant what it had meant when nobody was watching it.

Goodhart's paper stated the observation in a sentence that he has said he wrote half in jest and named after himself in the manner of Murphy's law: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. The paper is about monetary technicalities and the sentence is an aside in it. It was picked up by other economists, who found that the same thing happened to every monetary aggregate that any central bank adopted as a target, and the sentence acquired a name and a life. In nineteen ninety-seven an anthropologist, Marilyn Strathern, writing about the audit of British universities, rendered it in the form most people now know: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. That version is Strathern's, not Goodhart's, and it is broader than his, since he meant a statistical relationship and she meant any number at all.

I want to hold onto what is different about Goodhart's version, because the difference is the reason the constellation is worth three papers and not one. Ridgway's people were gaming. The interviewer who hid the vacancy knew what she was doing. Goodhart's banks were not, or not exactly. Nobody at a clearing bank sat down to break the Bank of England's model; they responded, each for their own reasons, to a policy that now made certain kinds of deposit cheaper or dearer, and the aggregate of their responses moved the number away from what it had meant. The regularity collapsed without anyone intending the collapse. This is the version of the law that applies when the people being measured are not employees at all, and it says that a number can stop meaning what it meant simply because it has been used, whether or not anyone is trying to fool you. An economist named Robert Lucas made a related argument the following year about why economic models fail when policy changes, and the two are usually mentioned together; Lucas's is a claim about models and Goodhart's about numbers, and Goodhart's is the one a manager needs.

Chapter Four: Campbell, Nineteen Seventy-Six

The third discovery is the one with the field cases, and the one that has been fought over most since, because its author was a psychologist working on how to evaluate government programmes, and the programmes he chose as examples were schools and police.

Donald Campbell was, by nineteen seventy-six, the most influential methodologist in American social science, the man who had taught a generation how to tell whether a reform had worked. In December of that year he gave a paper at Dartmouth, in an occasional-papers series that hardly anyone read, called Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change, and it was reprinted three years later in a journal called Evaluation and Program Planning, where it was read by everyone in that small field and by nobody outside it. Most of the paper is about how to evaluate programmes honestly. One section of it is about why the numbers a programme reports about itself cannot be trusted once the programme is judged by them, and that section contains the sentence now called Campbell's law: the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures, and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.

The cases are what make it stick. The first was the war on crime. Richard Nixon had taken office in nineteen sixty-nine promising to reduce crime and had made Washington, the one city the federal government policed directly, the demonstration. Two political scientists, David Seidman and Michael Couzens, went through the Washington figures afterwards and found how the reduction had been achieved: reported larcenies had been reclassified so that a theft worth fifty dollars, which counted as a serious crime under the federal definition, was recorded as forty-nine dollars, which did not, and the serious crime rate fell without any crime being prevented. Campbell's summary was that the main effect of a well-publicised, deliberate effort at social change had been the corruption of the indicator. His second case was the mirror of the first, and I think the more instructive. In nineteen sixty Chicago hired a reform police chief, Orlando Wilson, an honest man who insisted that crimes be recorded as reported rather than dropped, and the city's crime rate, on paper, rose sharply and stayed up, so that an improvement in policing appeared in the indicator as a crime wave. The number moved in the wrong direction because the reporting had got better, and the mayor who had hired the honest chief paid for it.

The third case was Vietnam. There the American command had begun by estimating enemy casualties and, when the estimates proved unbelievable, switched to counting bodies, on the reasoning that a body was a fact. The body count became the measure of a unit's success, and units produced bodies, and the bodies were counted more than once, and civilians were counted, and the figure that was meant to tell Washington whether the war was being won told it instead how badly the war wanted to be seen to be won. And the fourth case was the one that has since been quoted in every argument about schools: that an achievement test may be a good indicator of what a school teaches under conditions of ordinary teaching aimed at general competence, and that when the test score becomes the goal of teaching, it both stops indicating what it indicated and distorts the teaching, and Campbell was writing that fourteen years before anyone had heard of high-stakes testing and twenty-five before the law that made it national.

Campbell had a remedy, or the beginnings of one, and it is the place where his paper goes beyond the other two. He thought the corruption was a function of the stakes, and that the same indicator, collected by people who would not be judged by it, could be honest. He argued for multiple indicators, none of them decisive; for qualitative evaluation alongside the numbers; and, in an earlier paper on reforms as experiments, for a distinction between what he called the trapped administrator, who has staked his reputation on the programme's success and must therefore find it, and the experimental administrator, whose reputation rests on finding out whether the programme works and can afford the answer to be no. The whole difficulty, he said, was that the political system produced the first kind and needed the second.

Chapter Five: The Law in a White Coat

Before asking why the law had to be found three times, I want to show that medicine found it too, in its own material, and did not recognise it either, because the cases are the ones a clinician will carry longest.

The cleanest is a trial. Through the nineteen eighties cardiologists knew that patients who had survived a heart attack and who had frequent extra beats on their monitor, ventricular ectopics, died more often than those who did not. The extra beats were a good indicator; they had been measured for years and the association held. A new class of drugs suppressed them very effectively, and on the strength of that surrogate the drugs were being prescribed to hundreds of thousands of people. In nineteen eighty-seven the National Institutes of Health began a randomised trial, the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial, to confirm what everyone assumed, and two years later the monitoring board stopped it early, because the patients on the drugs, whose ectopics had been beautifully suppressed, were dying at more than twice the rate of the patients on placebo. The indicator had been a good indicator of risk under conditions in which nobody was acting on it. The moment a drug was chosen for its effect on the indicator, the indicator stopped meaning what it had meant, and the estimates of what the drugs had cost before the trial run into the tens of thousands of lives. That is Goodhart's version, the one with no gaming in it: no patient and no cardiologist cheated, and the regularity collapsed anyway, because the pressure applied for control purposes had been applied to the wrong point in the causal chain. The shelf said, two episodes ago, that Grove's biomarker proposal needed the paired-indicator rule applied to itself. This is what happens when it is not.

The second case is Campbell's version, in an English general practice. From two thousand and four the National Health Service paid family doctors a substantial share of their income according to a list of clinical indicators, the proportion of their diabetic patients with blood sugar below a threshold, of their hypertensives with pressure controlled, and so on, and the list was long and the money was real. Performance on the indicators rose fast, faster than the underlying care could plausibly have improved, and part of the reason was a provision called exception reporting, which allowed a practice to exclude from the denominator any patient it judged unsuitable for the target, and which some practices used, on the audits that followed, considerably more than others. The measured population got healthier partly because the hard patients had left it. It was a forty-nine-dollar larceny, done with a tick box, by people who mostly believed they were making a clinical judgement, and the honest practices, the Orlando Wilsons, who excluded nobody, scored worse.

The third case is closer to home for a researcher than either, and it is the one the field has been slowest to admit. A scientist's standing is judged, in most institutions, by numbers that were once indicators and are now targets: the count of papers, the citation count, the impact factor of the journals. Each of those was a reasonable proxy for a body of work under conditions in which nobody was managed by it. Each is now produced. One paper becomes three; the citation goes to a friend, or to oneself, rather than to the best source; the journal that once published what came in now manages what it publishes to protect a ratio, and there are journals whose impact factor has been withdrawn by the people who compute it for citing themselves too enthusiastically. None of this requires a villain. It requires a number with a consequence, a person who produces the number, and a distance between that person and whoever reads it, which is Campbell's law exactly, applied to the people who are supposed to know about Campbell's law. The taxonomy of research metrics gaming is now its own literature, and I think a listener can predict its findings from Ridgway's seven pages without reading a word of it.

Chapter Six: Why Three Times

So: an organisational sociologist in fifty-six, a central banker in seventy-five, an evaluation methodologist in seventy-six, each finding that a number used to judge people stops describing what it described, each in his own vocabulary, none citing the others. There was a fourth, in fact, in the year between the second and third: Steven Kerr, a management scholar, published a paper in nineteen seventy-five called On the Folly of Rewarding A While Hoping for B, the same law seen from the reward side, and which the management literature adopted as its own without noticing that the sociologists had been there twenty years earlier. And there was an ancestor, Robert Merton, whose nineteen forty essay on bureaucratic personality described the clerk who comes to treat the rule as the goal, and whom the shelf has in its queue.

The rediscovery is not an accident and it is not stupidity, and the reason is the one the shelf keeps finding. Each of these men was inside a field that did not read the others. Ridgway's paper is in a management journal, Goodhart's is in a central-bank proceedings volume, Campbell's is in an occasional-paper series on programme evaluation, and the citation networks of those three literatures did not touch. Each discovery got the name of the discoverer who was loudest in the field that later needed it: the economists needed a law when the monetary targets collapsed, so it is Goodhart's; the educators needed one when the tests took over the schools, so it is Campbell's; nobody needed one in nineteen fifty-six, so Ridgway has seven pages and a footnote in the literature review. It is a small, exact instance of a thing the shelf has been describing since Whyte: knowledge that exists in an organisation and cannot travel, because the channels run between people who already agree.

Chapter Seven: What Was Discovered

Now the law itself, and I want to argue that the common statement of it, the Strathern version, is not quite what any of the three found, and that the difference matters for what you do about it.

The common version is about measurement. It says that when you measure something and act on the measurement, the measurement goes bad. Stated that way it is a counsel of despair, and a manager who believes it has two options, to stop measuring or to measure and shrug. But look again at the three cases. The employment interviewer did not respond to being measured. Blau's office had counted interviews for years. She responded when the count became the thing she was judged by. The Bank of England's money supply had been measured, published, and modelled for a decade before it collapsed; it collapsed when the Bank started to act on it, and the banks knew it would. Washington's police had always recorded the value of stolen goods. The forty-nine-dollar larceny appeared when the crime rate acquired a president. In every case the number was fine until it acquired a consequence for the person who produced it, and the consequence, not the measurement, is the mechanism.

That reframing has three implications, and they are the reason the constellation is worth a listener's hour. The first is that the law is not about numbers. It applies with the same force to a qualitative judgement, an inspection, a supervisor's rating, the moment any of those carries a consequence for the person rated; it is easier to see in numbers because numbers make the drift visible. The second is that the corruption is proportional to the stakes and to the distance. Campbell's honest police chief could report a crime wave because he did not need the crime rate to fall; the interviewers in Blau's cooperative section could share vacancies because they had agreed not to be ranked by them. Where the number is collected far from where it is used, by people whose pay or standing depends on it, and where the people using it cannot see the process that made it, the drift is at its maximum, and the description I have just given is the description of every reporting chain in every distributed organisation on earth, including the ones that report on health.

The third implication is the delayed one, and it is where the constellation joins the rest of the shelf. Davies's accountability sink was a process that produced an outcome nobody was responsible for. Campbell's law describes the same object from the reporting side: a number that everyone in the chain has an interest in and nobody in the chain is accountable for the truth of, because the person who could check it is the person it is meant to inform. Jackall's managers, from episode one, kept every number vague for exactly this reason. And the connection to Grove is the useful one: his paired indicator is a Ridgway remedy, a second number chosen so that the two cannot be gamed in the same direction, and it works exactly as far as the pairing holds and no further, which is why the last episode found him, in his own Parkinson's, distrusting any score that the scorer could move by wanting to.

What none of the three papers says, and what the next episode has to, is what to do. Ridgway had no remedy. Goodhart's remedy, in monetary policy, was to stop targeting aggregates, which is not available to a programme that has to report something. Campbell's remedy was a change in the stakes, and the stakes are usually not the programme's to change. The shelf has, in the twenty years since these papers were rediscovered, a body of evidence about what happens when a health system is run on targets with consequences, and a smaller body about what has been tried against the drift, and the second half of the constellation is about that.

The question to carry into the week is Blau's, from the drawer. Somewhere in the work you run there is a number that people are judged by, and somewhere near it there is a vacancy in a drawer, a thing someone is doing to the number that you would not see from where you sit. Name the number. Then ask who produces it, what happens to them if it is bad, and whether you have ever once looked at how it is made rather than at what it says.
