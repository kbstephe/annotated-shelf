The Republic of Science (Michael Polanyi): the 1962 essay on research as a jigsaw puzzle solved by mutual adjustment, the Royal Society's abandoned 1945 survey of neglected subjects, and what a funding panel owes to a community that may not police itself

Polanyi's 1962 essay argues that scientists coordinate by watching one another's results and that central direction would paralyse them; tested against his own Manchester and Royal Society evidence, the Bernal debate, and the replication literature.

09 October 2026

Chapter One: A Puzzle With Too Many Hands

Michael Polanyi asks you to picture a jigsaw puzzle so large that somebody has decided it must be finished in the shortest possible time. The obvious plan is to hire helpers, and the question is how to use them. Share the pieces out equally, one heap per helper, and each helper works alone. Polanyi says this would suit a group of women shelling peas, where the total shelled does not change whether they sit together or in separate rooms, and would be hopeless for a puzzle, because few of the pieces in any one heap fit each other. Give every helper a full duplicate set and collect the results at the end, and the team still barely beats one good person working by himself.

The arrangement that works is the one with no arrangement. Put everyone at the same table, in sight of the same growing patch of picture. Whenever one helper fits a piece, every other helper sees which new openings that creates, and whoever is best placed to take the next one takes it. Each acts on his own initiative, each responds to what the others just did, and the puzzle gets done far faster than any plan for dividing it could manage. Put a single authority in charge of who touches which piece, Polanyi adds, and the speed of the whole table falls to the speed of that one authority.

That is the central picture of an essay he published in the journal Minerva in 1962, titled The Republic of Science: Its Political and Economic Theory. It runs to about twenty pages. He claims that the scientists of the world are such a table, that they are an organized body even though nobody organizes them, and that their organization is a form of coordination he calls mutual adjustment of independent initiatives. A scientist chooses a problem, pursues it by his own judgment, publishes, and every other scientist's choice of problem changes slightly in response. The coordination travels through the published results.

He supports the claim with a thought experiment about isolation. Seat the pea-shellers apart and nothing is lost. Isolate the scientists and, he says, each would go on for a while developing the problems left over from the shared stock of knowledge, then run out, and new problems of any worth would stop arising, because new problems come from other people's results. The field would stall. The evidence that science is a cooperative enterprise is how fast it stops without the cooperation.

This episode is about what happens to that picture when you ask three questions of it. What did Polanyi want it to do, and against whom? What does the one long historical case he offers actually show? And what does the picture assume about the table that the last fifteen years of work on replication have put in doubt? The third question matters most to anyone who sits on a panel that hands out money, because a panel is a small version of the table, and the picture says what it should and should not try to do.

The essay is part of the series on science as an institution. Episode fifty-seven took Vannevar Bush's report, which asked government to pay for basic research and then leave scientists alone. Episodes fifty-eight to sixty took Kuhn and his critics, who asked what the scientists who are left alone actually do with their time. Polanyi supplies the theory of why leaving them alone is rational, and he wrote it, as we are about to see, in the middle of an argument with people who thought the opposite.

Chapter Two: A Chemist Who Left the Laboratory to Win an Argument

Polanyi was a Hungarian-born physical chemist, a professor at Manchester, who by the evidence of this essay spent the years from the nineteen thirties onward increasingly worried about what governments and scientists might do to science, and he left laboratory work in the late nineteen forties, by the accounts I found, to write about it full time. The essay of 1962 is the summary of fifteen years of that writing, and it carries the scar tissue.

The argument it grew out of was British and specific. In 1939 the crystallographer J. D. Bernal published The Social Function of Science, a large book arguing that research should be organized and directed toward human welfare, and that the idea of research pursued for its own sake is something of a pose. Márta Fehér, a Hungarian philosopher who has written on the debate, reports that Polanyi quotes Bernal as calling that ideal a form of snobbery, a way for the scientist to disown the material base on which his work stands. Bernal sat on the Association of Scientific Workers, which published a manifesto listing among its aims that research should be directed chiefly to improving the conditions of life. The journalist J. G. Crowther and the biologist Lancelot Hogben wrote in the same vein and were, in Polanyi's words, best-sellers. Polanyi mentions Hogben's Science for the Citizen of 1938.

Polanyi's response had two parts. The first was an organization. In 1940 the Oxford zoologist John Baker and the botanist Arthur Tansley, with Polanyi among the founders, formed the Society for Freedom in Science, which published pamphlets against the planning of research. The second was Polanyi's own writing, which gave the Society its argument. In a pamphlet of 1946 called The Planning of Science he put the case that Bernal's programme was a mistake about how knowledge grows. The 1962 essay is the polished version, with a market metaphor and a political theory attached.

There was also a visit. Polanyi travelled to the Soviet Union in 1935 and came back horrified, according to Fehér, by the abolition of free markets and the attempt to plan scientific research. He wrote early about the Lysenko affair, the Soviet state's elevation of Trofim Lysenko's agrobiology over genetics. In a footnote to a 1942 address he later claimed his was the first paper to draw attention to the danger to science in general. So when he writes in the essay that the aim of steering science toward social ends is, in his words, "impossible and indeed nonsensical", he is answering people who he believed were offering Britain the Soviet arrangement with better manners.

That context shapes how to read the confidence. The essay is not an inquiry into whether science can be steered. It is a brief for the claim that it cannot, assembled by a man who watched the alternative being proposed in rooms full of distinguished colleagues. In January 1943, he tells us, the Association of Scientific Workers filled a large hall in London, and the meeting resolved, in the words of its official summary, that research would no longer be conducted for itself as an end in itself. That sentence is the villain of the essay. Everything Polanyi builds, he builds to show that sentence cannot be made true.

Chapter Three: How Nobody Knows What Everybody Knows

Mutual adjustment alone would produce chaos, and Polanyi sees this. If the helpers at the table watch each other, they still need some shared sense of which new fits count as progress and which are noise. In a puzzle the picture on the box settles it. Science has no box. So he turns to the standards by which scientists decide what a contribution is worth, and he lays them out under three headings.

The first is plausibility. A paper has to be believable in the light of what is already known, or it will not be published, and if it is published it may be ignored. He tells a story of his own as an example, and the story is worth the time because it is the best account in the essay of what the standard costs. In June 1947 Lord Rayleigh, a distinguished Fellow, published in the Proceedings of the Royal Society a set of simple experiments claiming that hydrogen atoms impressive a metal wire passed it energies up to a hundred electron volts. If true, Polanyi says, it would have been more revolutionary than Otto Hahn's discovery of fission. He asked physicists what they thought. They shrugged. None could find a fault in the experiments, none believed the result, and none thought a second run would pay. They ignored it. Polanyi adds that a possible explanation appears in his later book, Personal Knowledge, and that the physicists lost nothing by looking away.

The story is told with such relish that it is easy to miss what it asserts. The system of scientific opinion, by his own account, rejects an experiment that nobody can fault, on the grounds of a prior belief, and he treats this as a feature. A physicist's judgment that a result is implausible is accurate often enough to be worth the occasional miss. He concedes the miss, too. Unorthodox work of high originality may be discouraged or suppressed for a time, and he writes that these risks have to be taken.

The second standard is scientific value, which he splits into accuracy, systematic importance and intrinsic interest. A paper in physics beats a paper in biology on the first two, and biology wins on the third because living things are more interesting than inanimate ones. He says this with a straight face and a plain wit. The third standard is originality, judged the way a patent office judges an invention, by the degree of surprise among those who know the field.

Now he sets two of these against the third. Plausibility and value push toward conformity. Originality pushes toward dissent. He calls the resulting tension essential, because science must demand that serious work largely agree with what is believed while rewarding the person who departs from it, and it enforces its teachings in general for the sake of subverting them in particular. This is a close cousin of the essential tension Kuhn named in 1959, which episode fifty-eight followed, and Polanyi is making the same point from the other side of the Atlantic with fewer examples. He also offers a number to show how slow the process can be. Planck's quantum theory, discovered in 1900, took eleven years to win final acceptance, and thirty years later Planck stood close to Newton in standing.

The second half of the machinery is the part I find the most interesting and the most exposed. If no scientist understands more than a small fraction of science, how can anyone hold a joint opinion on the merit of a paper in a distant field? Polanyi's answer is a chain. Each scientist can judge his own specialty and also a neighbourhood around it, wide enough to include several other specialties. Those neighbourhoods overlap, and each scientist sits in several. Agreement about standards passes from one overlapping neighbourhood to the next, so uniform standards spread from astronomy to medicine without any person holding them all. Scientific opinion, he says, is held by no single mind. It exists in thousands of fragments, each endorsed at second hand by relying on the chains that link its holder to everyone else.

Hold on to that image of linked neighbourhoods. It is the second pillar of the essay, and it is the one that a grant panel puts its weight on every time it scores a proposal outside the reviewers' own work. The rest of the episode returns to it twice.

Chapter Four: The Committee That Looked for Neglected Subjects

Polanyi's strongest evidence is one episode, and he tells it in detail. In the summer of 1945 the University Grants Committee sent the Royal Society a memorandum signed by Sir Charles Darwin, titled The Balanced Development of Science in the United Kingdom. The worry was real. Some subjects needed expert study at only a few places, or at one, and appointments, the memorandum feared, were being made according to fashion, so that subjects of greater importance were pursued with less vigour than others of lesser. It proposed machinery for levelling out the gaps and redundancies. The Royal Society, through its sectional committees, would draw up lists of subjects deserving preference, and the exercise would be repeated, probably yearly, so the Grants Committee could keep the proportions right.

The Royal Society's secretaries circulated the proposal. Its own earlier discussion had agreed that coordination between universities was haphazard. The committees were asked for lists of neglected subjects.

Polanyi thought the request pointless and wrote to Sir Alfred Egerton of the Royal Society to say so. His argument was an alternative mechanism. Universities fill a vacant chair with the most eminent candidate they can attract, whatever the specialty, and this practice is the best safeguard of balanced development. His example was the physics chair at Manchester across the previous thirty years. The holders in sequence were Arthur Schuster, Ernest Rutherford, W. L. Bragg and Patrick Blackett, and each at the time stood for a rare section of physics: spectroscopy, radioactivity, X-ray crystallography, cosmic rays. Manchester had been right to choose each, he said, and would have been ill advised to heed the claims of subjects that had at that moment produced nobody of comparable ability. The test for giving a new subject more room was a growing number of distinguished people in it, and the falling off of creative work in others signalled where to withdraw.

Egerton replied sympathetically and passed the view along, yet the committees met anyway. Polanyi took part and compiled a list of neglected subjects in chemistry. The result looked to him vague and trivial, and he wrote to the chairman that he would not support it if it reached the Senate of his own university. He then learned his worry was needless. The view was spreading among the committee chairmen that a satisfactory condition in each science would come about naturally if each university always chose the most distinguished leaders for its posts, irrespective of specialty. Darwin himself called the reports rather disappointing. A year after it began, the action closed with a circular letter from Egerton to the Vice-Chancellors endorsing the reports in general and naming a small number of recommendations, seven proposals for new schools of research and nothing about how they would fit existing work. The effect on the universities, Polanyi says, seems to have been negligible. The recommendation for a strong school of analytical chemistry, which should have concerned him as professor of physical chemistry at Manchester, never reached him.

He also tells a shorter story that completes the pair. In July 1946 the Committee of Vice-Chancellors sent all universities a memorandum accepting that government had the right and the duty to satisfy itself that every field in the national interest was adequately cultivated, and asking for guidance and for surveys by the Grants Committee. Sir Ernest Simon, chairman of the council at Manchester, called it almost revolutionary. Polanyi reports that it made no impression on university life and is by now forgotten by the few who ever saw it.

From these two cases he concludes that planned coordination from a centre petered out, and that no body of scientists was better qualified than the Royal Society to show that a central authority cannot improve on the spontaneous appearance of growing points. The method that did the work in practice was the one he had described to Egerton: back the places where distinguished people are gathering, and take support from the places they have left.

This is a fair and vivid case, and it is told by a participant who is not a neutral one. Before judging it, set out what an admirer of Polanyi would say it proves, because the admirer's version is stronger than the one in the essay.

Chapter Five: The Strongest Version

A friendly reader would say the Manchester episode contains three separate claims, and that each has support outside it.

The first claim is about information. Anyone in a position to direct research from a centre must know where the gaps are, and the centre cannot know. The committees were asked to name subjects deserving preference and produced lists vague enough that Polanyi, a member, thought them trivial. The reason is that the knowledge needed to say a subject is neglected, as against unripe, is the knowledge of the people in the subject, and they are already acting on it. The invisible hand he invokes belongs to Adam Smith, and he proposes that the price system is only a special case of mutual adjustment, where prices broadcast current conditions to everyone and the scientist's broadcast is the published paper.

The second claim is about the unpredictability of use. Polanyi tells of a January 1945 broadcast on the BBC Brains Trust, where he and Bertrand Russell were asked about the practical uses of Einstein's theory of relativity, and neither could think of any, forty years after the theory appeared. Within months the first atomic bomb exploded, with its energy released at the expense of mass according to the relativistic equation. Einstein, he says, could not have taken those consequences into account when he began, if only because another dozen or so major discoveries had to be made before relativity could combine with them. Direct research toward a use, and you deflect it from the advance of knowledge that produces uses. His line is blunt enough to quote whole. "You can kill or mutilate the advance of science, you cannot shape it."

The third claim is that the arrangement is self-renewing. Scientific authority, in this account, is exercised over the novice for the purpose of equipping him to oppose it. A student is taught the current orthodoxy, and the training is meant to give him independent grounds for finding where it fails. Authority that cultivates dissent in particular points is no ordinary authority, and Polanyi spends the second half of the essay on the idea that this is the model of a free society. A society of explorers, he calls it, bent on a discovery it cannot name in advance. The freedom it protects is the freedom to speak what one takes to be true, which he sets against the freedom to do as one pleases. A dynamic orthodoxy grants the right to oppose itself in the name of truth. In a closing turn he says the emphasis should fall in the opposite direction from the market comparison: science is the higher form, and the market is what that principle becomes when it is applied to the production of goods.

The best version of Bernal's side gets a hearing in the same spirit, because the debate was not between a freedom lover and a tyrant. Bernal and his allies saw large areas of human need, from nutrition to housing, getting little attention from a research system that followed the tastes of its practitioners. They saw wartime mobilization organize physicists, chemists and statisticians for radar and the bomb and wanted peacetime to reuse the method. Polanyi sets the wartime case aside in a sentence. He says emergencies may arise in which scientists willingly apply their gifts to tasks of public interest, and that is not the same as shaping the ordinary course of science. That sentence leaves a large door open, and Bernal's side would walk through it.

Lysenko, too, supports a narrower claim than the one Polanyi draws from it. What the Soviet state did in genetics was dictate conclusions and persecute dissenters, a corruption of the verdict. What Bernal proposed was the direction of topics, which is a decision about where to spend. The two share a state and a plan, and they are different acts. A funding agency that says it wants work on tuberculosis has not told anyone what to find. Polanyi's best evidence addresses the first act, and his argument is written against the second.

Chapter Six: A Table That Does Not Police Itself

Now the harder question. Everything above assumed the table works. The helpers fit pieces, the pieces really fit, and a wrong fit is spotted and removed. Polanyi's claim for the efficiency of mutual adjustment depends on this. Scientific opinion filters out cranks, dabblers and frauds by the plausibility test and the refereeing system, and in parts of the world where it does not, he writes, research stagnates and unsound reputations grow.

The last fifteen years of work on reproducibility put pressure on exactly this assumption. In 2012 two cancer researchers, C. Glenn Begley, who had held a senior post at Amgen, and Lee Ellis of the MD Anderson Cancer Center, reported in Nature that Amgen's oncology scientists had tried to confirm the findings of fifty-three landmark papers in preclinical cancer biology, and had confirmed six. That is about eleven percent. The sample was chosen by the company and was not random, and the paper is a commentary rather than a controlled study, and a later large project on cancer biology used a different method and different papers. Take the figure for what it is, one industry laboratory's experience with studies it considered promising enough to build on.

Three years later the Open Science Collaboration, a group of some two hundred seventy authors, reported replications of one hundred published psychology studies. Ninety-seven of the originals had reported statistically significant results, and thirty-six percent of the replications did, with effect sizes about half as large. I have read the summary of this paper rather than the paper itself, so I hold the numbers loosely. The authors were careful to say that a failed replication does not prove the original false, and that is the correct caution. The result is also not what a well-policed table would produce.

Polanyi's mechanism offers three places to look for the cause, and each is awkward for him. The first is the filter. Plausibility, as he describes it, is judged by prior belief about the nature of things, and refereeing is the instrument. An implausible paper draws a question about the prior. A plausible one with a flimsy design draws much less, because the result agrees with what he expects. The test he celebrates for catching cranks is also a test that waves through the wrong kind of ordinary error. The Rayleigh story is a case of the filter working as designed, and one could tell the opposite story with equal confidence about a result that was believed because it was pleasant.

The second is the incentive. Polanyi wrote that scientists respond to professional standards rather than prices. That is true, and the standards rewarded novelty and significance. A field that rewards originality in the sense of surprise, as Polanyi's third standard does, will publish surprising results, and surprising results are enriched for mistakes. His own definition of originality is the degree of astonishment among experts. A system tuned to astonish will, by arithmetic, pass more flukes than one tuned to agree.

The third is the overlapping neighbourhoods. The chain of agreement is a fine mechanism for spreading a standard, and it spreads an error the same way. If every link endorses its neighbour at second hand, relying on the chain, then nobody in the chain has checked the original. This is what Polanyi says happens. He calls the authority of scientific opinion mutual, exercised between scientists and not above them, and admits that no single person is responsible at first hand for what science announces at any time. Rely on that and you have described a system with plenty of agreement and no particular person who has looked.

Episode fifty-nine met a case on the other side. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren's claim that a bacterium caused ulcers failed the plausibility test for years, because stomachs were known to be too acid for bacteria. The system treated an implausible result as noise. It was right in most cases of that kind and wrong in that one, and the cost of the error was years of ulcer treatment. Polanyi's own sentence, that the risks have to be taken, is the honest statement of what he accepts. The replication literature adds the other side of the ledger: the plausible result that stays in the books because nobody has an incentive to look again.

The correct conclusion is not that Polanyi was wrong. The community can police itself to some degree, and in fields with cheap, fast, decisive tests it does so well. Physics in his own examples, where an experiment can usually be repeated by a rival within months, is a table where pieces that do not fit stand out. Preclinical cancer biology and social psychology are tables where a piece that does not fit can sit in the picture for a decade. The essay treats science as one table. The evidence suggests many tables with very different speeds of correction, and the argument against central direction is strongest where the table corrects itself fastest.

Chapter Seven: What the Chairs at Manchester Would Not Have Told You

The Manchester evidence has a further weakness, and it is the one most relevant to money. Polanyi's rule for balanced development is to put new resources where distinguished people are appearing and take them from where creative work is falling off. Look at the chairs. Schuster, Rutherford, Bragg and Blackett were each appointed after the subject had produced work of distinction, and Polanyi explicitly says Manchester would have been wrong to heed the claims of subjects which had not yet produced men of comparable ability. The method follows winners. It rewards a field that has already shown what it can do, and it is silent about a field with no star yet because nobody has funded the people who would become one.

This is the structure that the sociologist Robert Merton later described as cumulative advantage, and it is the subject of the next episode. Credit and resources go to those already credited, and the signal used to allocate them was itself produced by earlier allocation. Polanyi's rule is stable and efficient while the indicator is reliable and the lag short. If it is noisy, or when a field takes twenty years to produce its first distinguished figure, the rule starves the ground that future chairs would have grown from. I would not call this a refutation, since the committees he was arguing against had no better method and said so. It does limit what the Manchester story proves. It shows that a central list of neglected subjects was no improvement on hiring the most eminent candidate. It leaves open whether that habit is the best a system can do.

The same worry applies to the overlapping neighbourhoods when they become a funding panel. Consider a constructed case, labelled as constructed. A review panel of twenty members, each expert in a narrow corner, meets to score sixty applications. For any application, perhaps three members sit in the neighbourhood of its field, and the rest sit two or three links away. Polanyi's chain says the far-off reviewers can still judge, because competence overlaps. In practice they rely on the near three, who rely on the applicant's record, and the record is a product of earlier decisions by earlier panels. The agreement that emerges looks like a consensus of the whole room, and it is a consensus of three people with a following of seventeen. That may be the best available arrangement, and it is a long way from twenty independent judgments.

The modern defence of Polanyi's side comes from economists who argue that research is better left alone. Terence Kealey, whom episode fifty-seven used, argued in The Economic Laws of Scientific Research that government support crowds out private effort. The modern attack comes from Philip Mirowski, whose chapter on why the economic metaphor did not work for Polanyi I know only from catalogue descriptions of the book, The Effortless Economy of Science. I report the existence of the argument and have not read it. As described, he holds that an invisible hand argument which began as a plausible account of science ended up as propaganda for a uniquely privileged claim on public support. I would put the objection less sharply. Polanyi's argument explains why central direction fails at the frontier. It does not explain why the public should pay for the table at the size it is, and the essay says so itself when it urges universities to appeal to the electorate on their own grounds. It argues for protecting the table from instruction, and it has little to say about how large a table society should buy.

Chapter Eight: Three Claims, Three Verdicts

The essay contains three claims that Polanyi treats as one, and they come apart under test.

The first is the claim about coordination in discovery. That scientists, working on problems of their own choosing and reading each other's results, move faster than any plan could move them, particularly at the frontier where nobody knows what the picture is. This claim holds up well. The Royal Society's year of work produced lists the author of the best argument against them found trivial, and the dispersed method of back-the-winners is hard to beat for choosing among subjects in progress. The Brains Trust story, told by a man who could not name a use for relativity five months before the bomb, is the best evidence in the essay that applications cannot be read off the frontier. I would add one limit. The evidence is about choosing among topics. It says little about whether a funder may announce a goal, such as a vaccine against a named disease, and pay many independent groups to work toward it. Polanyi's carve-out for emergencies concedes that point in a sentence, and the history of the twentieth century has a good deal more directed research than he allows.

The second is the claim about governance. That the community can be trusted to maintain standards, so that the authority of scientific opinion is a better judge than any outside body. This holds in some fields and fails in others, and the replication work gives the measure. Where tests are cheap and rivals eager, the table corrects itself. Where a check costs a year of someone's career, the authority of opinion is the authority of whoever spoke first. The claim needs a qualification the essay does not supply.

The third is the claim about allocation. That money should go where scientific opinion points, because opinion is the best available guide to promise. This one is the weakest in the form Polanyi gives it, because it needs the signal of distinction to be informative about future promise, and the signal is shaped by prior allocation. It remains hard to replace with anything better. The alternatives tried in the decades since include lotteries, programme managers with discretion, and funding people rather than projects, and the later episodes in this series take them up one at a time.

A reader who sits on panels can take away a test from this, and I label it the narrator's own. When a panel invokes scientific merit, ask which overlapping neighbourhood is vouching for it. If the answer is three members and a record, the panel is applying a standard that was itself produced by earlier panels, and a score of nine from such a room is better read as a report of the applicant's past than a forecast of the work. If the answer is a set of people who have repeated a similar result with a different sample, the standard has been checked by a table that corrects itself, and a high score means more.

On whether to read it, the essay is short, readable, and available free on a university course page, and the Manchester section alone, a page and a half, is worth the time of anyone who has served on a committee asked to list what is neglected. The parts on authority and tradition, where Polanyi sets Burke against Paine and reaches for T. S. Eliot, are the weakest and can be skipped without loss to the argument about funding.

I should say how this episode was grounded. I read the essay in full from a posted copy of the Minerva text. The debate with Bernal comes from Márta Fehér's published essay on Polanyi and the freedom of science, from which I take the quotation attributed to Bernal and the account of the Association of Scientific Workers. The founding of the Society for Freedom in Science, its founders, and the 1946 pamphlet come from search summaries of bibliographic records and Nature items, and I did not read McGucken's history. Begley and Ellis, the Open Science Collaboration, and Mirowski's argument come from summaries, not from the papers. Polanyi's account of the Royal Society episode is his own, and I have not seen the documents it describes. That is a limit on the case: the one story on which the essay rests is told by a participant and has not been checked against the other side's papers.

A panel member holds a stack of proposals in a field that has produced no distinguished name, and the rule that has governed her room for a generation tells her to give such fields nothing until someone distinguished appears. The question to carry into that room is who, in the building, is paid to find the next Schuster before he is one.
