Vannevar Bush, Pieces of the Action: the wartime memoir of the man who traded the uniform for the contract, tested against the funding fight it started and never finished

The 1970 memoir of the engineer who ran America's Second World War research effort, read as a manual on matching organization to problem, then tested against the postwar political fight over who gets to run science after the war that justified running it that way is over.

14 September 2026

Chapter One: The Rejected Detector

In the winter of nineteen seventeen, a young engineer named Vannevar Bush built a device that could hear a submarine's hull through open water by the disturbance it made in a magnetic field. He was working out of New London, Connecticut, for a private radio company, on his own initiative more than anyone's orders, in a war that was drowning American and British shipping faster than either navy could explain to the public. The device worked. Getting the Navy to use it did not. There was no officer whose job was to sit between the civilian who built the thing and the service that might deploy it, so the request for a test traveled through channels built for buying uniforms and boilers, not for judging an idea nobody in the chain of command had the training to evaluate. Bush was asked for modification after modification by people who could not have told him which modification mattered. Months went by. The war ended before the Navy's own process finished deciding what to do with a machine that already worked.

Bush never called this the defining event of his life, and by the measure of prizes and headlines it wasn't. But he kept coming back to it for the rest of his life as the clearest small case of a much larger problem: a democracy at war has no shortage of capable civilians willing to build what the military needs, and no reliable way to get their work in front of anyone who can say yes. Twenty-three years later, handed the chance to build that missing channel from the ground up, he built it in a form almost nobody in Washington expected, and ran it well enough that the failure of nineteen seventeen never got the chance to repeat itself at scale. The book he wrote about doing it, late in his life, is not really about submarines or radar or the bomb, though all three appear in it at length. It is about the plumbing: who gets to decide, how fast, and what happens to good ideas that arrive at the wrong desk.

By the time he wrote it, Bush had earned the standing to say so without much hedging. Tufts degrees in engineering, a doctorate from MIT in nineteen sixteen, a professorship and then the deanship of MIT's engineering school, and in nineteen thirty-one the differential analyzer, a room-sized mechanical computer of shafts and gears that solved differential equations no one had a faster way to solve, and that a generation of physicists and ballisticians leaned on before electronic computing existed. He had also co-founded the company that became Raytheon. By nineteen thirty-eight he was president of the Carnegie Institution of Washington, one of the country's few private bodies capable of funding basic research on its own authority, which put him inside the room with the people who mattered in American science just as Europe went back to war. The book he published in nineteen seventy, "Pieces of the Action," reissued decades later by Stripe Press in an edition slim enough to read in an evening, is his own account of what he did with that position: not a history of the war, which he leaves to the historians, but a manual, opinionated and specific, for anyone who will ever again have to move a room full of independent-minded experts from an idea to a working weapon faster than the enemy can move theirs.

Chapter Two: Ten Minutes with the President

The chance came on the twelfth of June, nineteen forty. Bush had spent weeks drafting and redrafting a plan for a civilian body that would organize American science for a war the United States had not yet entered, working the idea through an ally, Harry Hopkins, close enough to Roosevelt to get a meeting arranged within days. What Bush finally walked into the Oval Office holding was not the multi-page proposal he had started with. It was four paragraphs on one page. Roosevelt read it, and inside a few minutes wrote three words across the top in his own hand: OK, FDR. The National Defense Research Committee existed within days, reporting to the President directly, funded from his own emergency account, answerable to no cabinet department and beholden to no existing bureaucracy that might have spent a year arguing over which one owned it.

That single-page memo is the whole method compressed to its opening move. Bush had watched the nineteen seventeen submarine detector die inside reporting lines built for a different kind of decision, and when he got his own turn to design the lines of authority himself, he made the path from idea to yes exactly one signature long. But brevity at the top only solves half the problem. The other half is what happens underneath that signature, once money starts flowing to hundreds of laboratories working on problems the man at the top could not personally judge even if he tried. Bush's answer to that second half is the part of the book that reads, eighty years later, less like history and more like a live argument: a body built to execute a known plan and one built to find an unknown answer are different machines, and running either one like the other squanders whatever made it useful to stand up in the first place.

Chapter Three: Two Shapes of Organization

A military unit executing a known operation runs on a pyramid: orders at the top, obedience below, and speed that comes from nobody stopping to debate the plan mid-execution. Bush respected that shape and used it constantly, but he refused to run his own committee that way, because the National Defense Research Committee did not have a known operation to execute. It had a set of open questions, radar, submarine detection, explosives, none of which had a settled answer waiting to be handed down. He split the work into five divisions, each with a chairman chosen for competence in that division's domain and given real authority to run it, reporting to Bush directly, with Bush himself handling the traffic between the divisions and everyone outside them: the military services, Congress, industry. Nobody below Bush waited on a chain of approvals to move an idea forward inside their own division. The line Bush kept coming back to, describing the whole design, was that an organization has to know at every moment exactly where a given decision gets made, or the decision drifts until nobody makes it. He never pretended the pyramid was wrong for every purpose. He insisted it was wrong for this one, and that most of the damage he'd watched institutions do to good science came from applying it anyway, out of habit rather than any actual judgment about what the problem required.

The Navy supplied its own proof by contrast, on its own turf, without meaning to. German submarines spent the opening years of American involvement sinking tankers within sight of the Atlantic coastline, and the depth charges the Navy had on hand, a design called Ashcan, detonated too slowly to catch a submarine that had already dived past the depth where the charge went off. The fix was not a mystery; faster-sinking charges and better coordination between aircraft and surface ships were both within reach. What stood in the way for the first eighteen months of the war was an ordnance bureaucracy structured exactly like the pyramid Bush had refused to impose on his own committee: senior officers, trained for a different kind of war, sitting on top of a chain that had no fast path from a junior officer's field observation to a change in doctrine. The turn came only when a new command, the Tenth Fleet, was built under a younger admiral with the authority to work directly with Bush's laboratories rather than through the layers above him, borrowing the loose, direct structure Bush had already proven on the research side and applying it to the operational side. The lesson Bush drew, and stated as plainly as anything in the book, was that the form of an organization was never neutral background. It was itself a decision about how fast a truth could travel from the person who found it to the person who could act on it, and the Navy had simply made that decision badly for longer than it needed to.

Chapter Four: The Contract Instead of the Uniform

The administrative move that made the whole apparatus run, the one the rest of the book keeps circling back to, was smaller and stranger than it sounds: Bush chose not to draft scientists into the military and not to build a single sprawling government laboratory to house them either. He wrote contracts instead, cost-plus-a-fixed-fee, and signed them with universities and companies that kept their own scientists in their own labs, working under their own department heads, answerable to Bush's committee for results rather than for the terms of their employment.

The clearest single demonstration of what that choice bought is the Radiation Laboratory Bush's committee stood up at MIT in the autumn of nineteen forty, within weeks of a British scientific delegation crossing the Atlantic to hand the United States a piece of hardware called the cavity magnetron, which generated microwaves far shorter and more powerful than anything American radar had managed on its own. Britain was gambling, at real risk to itself, that American manufacturing capacity would turn that hardware into working radar faster than Britain's own overstretched industry could. Bush did not build a new government facility to house the resulting project, and did not commission the physicists involved into uniform. He signed a contract with MIT, which kept the people who became the Radiation Laboratory on MIT's own payroll, working in buildings MIT already had, while OSRD supplied the funding and the mission. By the war's end that single contract had produced more than a hundred distinct radar systems and something like a billion and a half dollars of hardware in wartime money, designing something close to half of all the radar the Allies deployed. None of it required the government to run a laboratory it had never operated before, or to persuade a research physicist that trading a professorship for a rank was the price of being useful.

A second contract, smaller and stranger still, ran the same trick on a completely different kind of problem. Bush's committee needed a fuze that could sense an approaching target and detonate itself at the right distance instead of relying on a timer or a direct hit, a problem that touched physics, electronics, and mechanical engineering all at once. The work started under a physicist named Merle Tuve at the Carnegie Institution's own Department of Terrestrial Magnetism, the same Carnegie Institution Bush had run before the war, which meant he knew exactly which building had the cyclotron space free. As the project outgrew what one department could hold, OSRD moved it under a fresh contract to Johns Hopkins, which stood up an entirely new laboratory, the Applied Physics Laboratory, purely to carry the work, folding in more than forty other industrial and academic contractors along the way. By August of nineteen forty-two, less than two years after the project started from nothing, the proximity fuze was in trials over Chesapeake Bay. It went on to change naval antiaircraft gunnery so completely that Allied commanders kept the fuze's very existence classified for the rest of the war, afraid that a single captured dud would hand the design to an enemy who had no equivalent.

Bush's own summary of what the contract bought, stated without much ornament, was that scientists worked best answering to other scientists inside the institutions that had trained them, not answering to a military hierarchy that had no fast way to judge whether a given idea had any future in it. Nobody had to leave a university to serve; the university itself became the unit that served, on contract, for as long as the war needed it to and not a day longer.

Chapter Five: The Same Trick, a Different Kind of Science

If the contract worked only for physics and engineering, the sample would still be interesting but narrow, the kind of coincidence a critic could wave away as one field's peculiar good luck. Bush's committee ran the identical mechanism through biology and pharmaceutical manufacturing at the same time, with a different set of contractors and a different committee chairman, and it produced the same shape of result on a schedule that mattered just as much.

The problem, in the autumn of nineteen forty-one, was that a British team led by Howard Florey had shown penicillin could cure infections no other drug touched, but had no way inside wartime Britain to grow it in anything beyond laboratory quantities. Florey crossed the Atlantic looking for American manufacturing muscle, and found it through OSRD's medical research committee, chaired by a pharmacologist named Alfred Newton Richards, who met with Florey that October and came away with an agreement that the committee would back a push to scale penicillin production using the same tool Bush had already proven on radar: contracts to the companies that already knew how to run large fermentation vats, rather than a government-built pharmaceutical plant starting from nothing. The specific breakthrough that made mass production possible, a technique called deep-tank fermentation, was worked out at Pfizer, which in late nineteen forty-three bought an old ice factory in Brooklyn and filled it with fourteen fermentation tanks holding seventy-five hundred gallons apiece. The plant opened in March of nineteen forty-four. By June, when Allied forces went ashore at Normandy, most of the penicillin that reached them had come out of that single converted ice house.

Put the two stories side by side and the actual claim of the book comes into view, not as a slogan but as something closer to a controlled comparison. Radar and the proximity fuze were problems in physics, run through university and industrial contracts under one committee. Penicillin was a problem in biology and industrial chemistry, run through a completely different set of contractors under a different committee chairman, on a compressed timeline set by a different branch of the same war. What repeated across both was not the science, which had almost nothing in common, but the shape of the arrangement: keep the expert inside the institution that already knew how to manage them, buy their time and their output on contract, and reserve the government's role for money, coordination, and the occasional decision only a national authority could make. The result Bush wanted readers to sit with was not that any one of these three technologies was inevitable. It was that the same administrative choice, applied three separate times to three unrelated fields under the same wartime clock, delivered a working answer inside two to four years each time, which is a stronger claim than any one success story could make on its own.

Chapter Six: The Tyro Problem

None of this, in Bush's telling, ran itself. A structure loose enough to let real experts move fast carries a cost: it is just as open to a confident amateur with no relevant expertise doing serious damage before anyone with the standing to stop him catches it, and Bush kept a name ready for that particular character: the tyro, someone who enters an unfamiliar field with total confidence and no humility, ignoring the channels built to catch the gaps in his own understanding. He was careful to separate the tyro from the amateur in the ordinary sense. An amateur who listens, learns, and earns the respect of people already working the problem is welcome in Bush's account, and several of his own best scientists started that way. What made a tyro dangerous was the combination of confidence and refusal to be taught.

His clearest case study was a British inventor named Geoffrey Pyke, who arrived with two schemes in quick succession, each backed by enough political enthusiasm to nearly bypass the ordinary channel of review entirely. The first, Project Habakkuk, proposed building an aircraft carrier out of a mixture of ice and wood pulp strong enough to resist torpedoes, an idea eccentric enough that most engineers dismissed it out of hand, and Winston Churchill himself approved it for serious study anyway. Pyke told Bush that OSRD had already agreed to build the thing, which was not true, and Bush's refusal to let an unauthorized claim stand became the whole episode's actual lesson: authority has to be visibly and specifically located, or anyone persuasive enough can claim it by simply asserting that it already belongs to them. The second scheme moved faster and did more damage before anyone caught it. Pyke wanted a fleet of small snow vehicles built to carry a raiding party against a heavy-water plant in occupied Norway, and the order to build a thousand of them went through fast enough to divert scarce aircraft-manufacturing capacity, without the service whose production line the order was eating ever being consulted. The Air Force found out, vetoed the diversion, and the vehicles that did get built found a second life hauling supplies rather than raiders, a use nobody had actually designed them for.

Bush's own summary of the discipline a loose structure still required was blunt to the point of being almost a slogan: when the general's hat is on sideways, the room is open for argument and every idea gets a hearing; when it's on straight, the argument is over and it's time for orders. Both postures were necessary, in his account, and the entire craft of running a body built to innovate rather than execute lay in knowing, at any given hour, which one the room was actually in. Stuck permanently in the argument posture, an organization produces Pyke's Norwegian weasels. Frozen the other way, permanently in the orders posture, it produces the Navy's slow depth charges. Bush's own committee spent the whole war trying to hold both postures at once, in different rooms, sometimes in the same afternoon.

Chapter Seven: Dime a Dozen

Bush was equally blunt about where he thought the actual credit for a working invention belonged, and it was not where inventors usually put it themselves. Having an idea, in his account, was the cheap part. Inventions are a dime a dozen, he wrote, and compared them to poems: satisfying to produce, worthless on their own until someone does the much harder, much less glamorous work of building, testing, manufacturing, and selling the thing the idea only gestured at. He'd seen enough hopeful amateurs walk in with an unworkable scheme dressed up as a breakthrough, and enough real breakthroughs sit unused for want of anyone willing to do the unglamorous finishing work, that he came to treat the finishing as the actual achievement and the initial spark as something closer to a ticket of entry.

That belief shaped how his own laboratories handled credit while the work was underway, and he described the prevailing mood inside them with something close to relief: nobody much cared whose name ended up on a given advance, because everyone involved understood that the war would not wait for the argument to be settled first. He contrasted that wartime ethic openly with the peacetime academic habit of guarding priority, publishing fast to establish a claim, treating a rival's parallel discovery as a threat rather than a confirmation. Inside OSRD's laboratories, for the years the war ran, that competitive instinct mostly went quiet on its own; everyone in the building could see, every day, what the actual stakes were, and a stake that large shrank credit-chasing down to its true size.

That same belief, extended past the individual scientist to the institution, is what put Bush on a collision course the moment the shooting stopped. If ideas were the cheap part and execution the part that actually earned a return, then the people and organizations that had built the working things had a claim to keep something from having built them: continued funding, continued autonomy, and, in the specific and contentious case that dominated Washington for the rest of the decade, continued ownership of the patents on what their wartime contracts had produced.

Chapter Eight: The Fight Nobody Remembers Winning

The peacetime version of the argument had a name and a face by nineteen forty-two: Senator Harley Kilgore of West Virginia, a populist New Dealer who watched the same wartime research machine Bush was running and drew almost the opposite lesson from it. Kilgore's own bill for a permanent postwar science agency wanted federal grants distributed with an eye to geography rather than concentrated wherever the strongest existing labs already sat, wanted political oversight of what got funded rather than scientists judging their own peers, wanted the social sciences included alongside physics and chemistry, and wanted patents on publicly funded discoveries to belong to the public that had paid for them rather than to the university or company that happened to hold the contract. Bush's own committee, for all its speed and its results, had in fact concentrated its wartime contracts overwhelmingly in a small number of elite institutions, MIT and Harvard and Johns Hopkins and a handful of others doing the bulk of the work, a pattern Kilgore's allies in Congress pointed to as evidence of the closed, self-selecting club they wanted the permanent postwar agency to avoid recreating.

Bush fought the geography argument and the patent argument with everything the war's credibility gave him, and mostly won the terms of the peacetime settlement that followed: a system built on competitive project grants judged by working scientists rather than political appointees, patents generally left with the institution doing the research rather than seized by the government outright, and funding that, decades later, still clusters around the same tier of research universities Kilgore warned about while the fight was live. The document that made Bush's case in its most polished form, a report called "Science, The Endless Frontier," written at Roosevelt's own request as the war closed, became the founding text for the National Science Foundation and shaped how NIH itself came to award money. That report is a different book from the one in front of us, scoped for its own future hour on this shelf, and gets only this pointer here: everything Bush argued in it about basic research paying for itself downstream had already been road-tested, in his own mind, by the three-year sprint from a British magnetron to a working radar network and from a pharmacology meeting to a Brooklyn ice factory full of penicillin tanks.

What "Pieces of the Action" adds that the more famous report leaves out is how contested Bush's win actually was while it was happening, and how much of Kilgore's critique simply got absorbed rather than defeated. Congress did eventually fold social science into the National Science Foundation's mandate, decades after Kilgore first proposed it. Concentration in a small tier of elite institutions remains, to this day, one of the standing complaints leveled against the exact funding architecture Bush built. The settlement that emerged was closer to a draw with better publicity for one side than to the clean victory the more famous report is usually credited with.

Chapter Nine: What the Same Mechanism Also Built

The book's own confidence, tested against the fuller historical record rather than against Bush's own account of it, holds up less cleanly than the radar and penicillin stories alone suggest, and three separate lines of criticism converge on roughly the same target.

The economist Terence Kealey's objection runs at the level of the whole method rather than any single project. Government funding for research, in his argument, tends over time to crowd out the private funding that would otherwise have appeared, because the pool of qualified scientists able to carry it out is finite and a subsidized public project simply pulls talent away from a private one that might have happened anyway. Kealey does not deny that OSRD's individual wartime results were real. His objection is to using an unrepeatable wartime emergency, with an unlimited budget, a single obvious enemy, and a national consensus that no peacetime funding fight will ever again enjoy, as the proof text for a permanent claim that the same government-directed model is the right one to run in ordinary times, against ordinary, contested scientific priorities, for decades on end.

The historian Larry Owens pushes from a different angle entirely, and lands closer to home for anyone who admires the wartime machine on its own terms. His argument, laid out in a nineteen ninety-four history of the OSRD's management, is that the exact strategy that let Bush move so fast, centralizing decision-making in a small Washington office while handing the actual work out to private universities and companies on contract, is also what produced, once the war's discipline was gone, an uncontrolled proliferation of public-private research arrangements that Bush himself came to find alarming and could no longer meaningfully oversee. The civilian control Bush had prized as OSRD's chairman, watching over hundreds of contracts himself, could not scale to the peacetime sprawl the same contract mechanism enabled once the number of arrangements multiplied past what any one office could actually supervise. The mechanism that won the war, on this reading, is the same mechanism that grew, unsupervised, into the vast web of defense and research contracting that later critics would find much harder to defend than radar ever was.

Kilgore's own objection, the one Bush spent the most breath defeating in real time, has aged into something closer to a standing feature of the system than a defeated alternative. The concentration of contracts in a small number of already-strong institutions was not a wartime accident that peacetime competition corrected. It became, largely through the settlement Bush himself won, the ordinary operating condition of how competitive federal research funding works, and the same complaint Kilgore made about fifteen institutions dominating OSRD's wartime contracts gets made today, with updated numbers, about which universities and medical centers pull down the largest share of federal grant dollars.

None of the three critiques argues that radar, the proximity fuze, or penicillin should not have been built the way they were. All three argue that the case for running peacetime science the same way rests on evidence the wartime record alone cannot actually supply, and that at least one cost of the wartime method, an unsupervised sprawl nobody designed on purpose, showed up because the method worked as well as it did.

Chapter Ten: The Study Section's Real Ancestor

What survives that reckoning, undiminished, is not a verdict about whether federal science funding should exist at all. It is the narrower, more portable claim Bush actually demonstrated three separate times under the same wartime clock: that matching an organization's structure to the shape of its actual problem, a tight pyramid for a known operation, a loose federation of autonomous experts for an open question, with the boundary between the two states made explicit rather than left to drift, produces results a mismatched structure cannot reach no matter how talented the people inside it are.

Every study section that sits down to score a grant application is, whether the members would put it this way or not, running Bush's own argument with Kilgore in miniature, on every single proposal that crosses the table. Fund the well-resourced center with the longer track record and the deeper bench, and you are voting Bush's side of nineteen forty-three: concentrate the money where the infrastructure to spend it well already exists, and accept that the resulting map of American science will keep looking the way it already looks. Fund the newer investigator with the thinner file and the more wide-open question, and you are voting Kilgore's: spread the bet, on the theory that talent shows up in more places than the last round of funding managed to reach, and that a system which only ever rewards its own past winners will eventually stop discovering anything its winners didn't already expect to find. Bush's side mostly won the architecture of the system in nineteen forty-five. Kilgore's objection never actually left the room; it simply moved from the floor of the Senate into the quieter, more private arithmetic of how any individual reviewer decides, application by application, which kind of bet the next open slot on the payline is actually for.

The distinction Bush drew between an executing organization and an innovating one applies past the funding question, to the narrower one of running any particular grant, trial, or program once the money has actually landed. A protocol with a known, validated procedure behind it wants the pyramid: clear steps, clear reporting lines, minimal improvisation, because the whole value of a known operation is that everyone executes the same known thing the same way every time. A question nobody has a validated procedure for yet wants something closer to what Bush built inside his own five divisions: real authority pushed down to whoever actually understands the specific problem, with the person above them managing the traffic between problems rather than second-guessing the technical calls inside any one of them. Getting that match backward, in either direction, treating an open question as though the procedure were already settled, or treating a settled procedure as an invitation to freelance, produces the same two failure modes this hour has already walked through: the Navy's depth charges arriving too slowly because nobody trusted a junior officer's read on the actual physics, and Pyke's weasels eating a bomber production line because nobody with the standing to say no had been asked.

Go back to whatever program or protocol occupies the largest share of the next working month, and ask it Bush's question honestly: is the level of top-down specification here matched to how well the underlying problem is actually understood, or is a known-operation structure being applied out of habit to a question that is still, in fact, wide open? The answer sorts nearly everything Bush spent this whole book arguing about into a single, testable line, and it is the same line a study section is drawing, usually without saying so, every time it decides how much freedom a given proposal actually needs in order to be worth the money.

Next time, the other half of that same argument, taken to its own logical extreme: David Braben's "Scientific Freedom," the story of a decade in which a British oil company funded scientists with no peer review, no milestones, and no requirement to explain themselves at all, and what that experiment can and cannot tell anyone still deciding how much freedom a good idea actually needs before it can prove itself.
