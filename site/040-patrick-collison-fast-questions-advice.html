Patrick Collison, Fast, Questions and Advice: three web pages that map the mind of a reader before the books he chose to publish

The first of eight episodes on the Stripe Press shelf opens with its curator's own pages: a list of ambitious things finished quickly, a list of questions nobody has answered, and advice to a younger self, read for what they say fast institutions need, tested against the survivorship the list conceals, and tied back to the question this whole series on organisations has been circling: how anything that cannot die gets replaced.

10 September 2026

Chapter One: The Day Before Yesterday

On a Thursday evening in July of nineteen forty-one, a brigadier general in the United States Army named Brehon Somervell decided that the War Department needed a single building to house its forty thousand scattered clerks and officers. By that Sunday, initial drawings existed. Ground was broken two months later, on the eleventh of September, with the country still at peace. The building was finished on the fifteenth of January, nineteen forty-three, four hundred and ninety-one days after the first shovel, and it was, on completion, the largest office building in the world. It still is. Somervell, when asked when something was needed, had a standard answer: the day before yesterday.

Now hold the Pentagon beside a bus lane. In two thousand and one, the city of San Francisco proposed a dedicated lane for buses along Van Ness Avenue, a wide, straight, existing street about two miles long. The lane opened in twenty twenty-two, around seven thousand six hundred days later, at a cost of three hundred and forty-six million dollars, which works out to about a hundred and ten thousand dollars per metre of road. A spokesman for the transit agency, asked about the delays, cited an increase in wet weather since the project began. The Alaska Highway, seventeen hundred miles of military road pushed across remote tundra in two hundred and thirty-four days in nineteen forty-two, cost, in the same year's dollars, seven hundred and ninety-three dollars per metre.

Both of those entries sit on a single web page, one above the other, under a one-sentence heading: some examples of people quickly accomplishing ambitious things together. The page is maintained by Patrick Collison, and it closes with a request for more entries, preferably with sources, and then with a question he does not answer. The physical projects on the list, he observes, happened before nineteen seventy to a disproportionate degree. Why?

Chapter Two: The Reader of the Two

Patrick Collison was born in Ireland in nineteen eighty-eight, sold his first company with his younger brother John before he was twenty, and in twenty ten the two of them founded Stripe, which processes payments for a large share of the internet's commerce and is, as I record this, one of the most valuable private companies in the world. That would earn him a business profile. It would not earn him an episode of this show, and it is not why he is here.

He is here because he reads, and because he has done something with his reading that almost no one in his position does. In twenty eighteen Stripe started a publishing imprint, Stripe Press, with a stated purpose of publishing ideas about progress, and over the years since it has issued about twenty titles, each one an editorial bet that a particular book should have found a wider audience the first time around. Several were out of print when he chose them. This series will spend its next seven episodes inside that shelf, and the discipline I am holding myself to is that the shelf is the source: what he chose to publish, and what he has written in his own name, rather than the sprawling list of seven hundred and some books on his personal site that he notes he has not maintained in about a decade. A curated press is a considered statement. A bookshelf page is an attic.

He has also written a little himself, mostly essays with co-authors and mostly about one theme. With the physicist and writer Michael Nielsen he argued, in the Atlantic in twenty eighteen, that science is producing less per dollar and per scientist than it used to, and backed it with a survey asking scientists to rank the importance of Nobel-winning discoveries by decade, which showed the rankings falling. With the economist Tyler Cowen he called, in the same magazine the following year, for a new field they named progress studies, devoted to understanding why some places and periods produce so much more than others. In April of twenty twenty, with Cowen again, he launched Fast Grants, which gave out more than fifty million dollars for COVID research with funding decisions made within forty-eight hours of application, an experiment I will come back to. And he co-founded the Arc Institute, a research organisation that funds scientists rather than projects, on a model this series will meet properly when it reaches Donald Braben. Every one of those is a practical attempt to answer a question on the second of his three pages. That is the order the pages should be read in: what got done fast, what he does not understand, and what he would tell a younger version of himself. Together they are the map of the mind that chose the books.

Chapter Three: The List

The Fast page is best heard as a sequence, because the entries share a rhythm. Dee Hock was given ninety days to launch the BankAmericard, the card that became Visa, starting from nothing. He did it, and signed up more than a hundred thousand customers in the same window. Kelly Johnson and his team at Lockheed designed and delivered the P-eighty Shooting Star, the first jet fighter the Air Force used, in a hundred and forty-three days. On the ninth of August, nineteen sixty-eight, NASA decided that Apollo Eight should go to the moon rather than stay in earth orbit; it launched on the twenty-first of December, a hundred and thirty-four days later. Ken Thompson wrote the first version of Unix in three weeks. Brendan Eich built the first prototype of JavaScript in ten days in May of nineteen ninety-five. The Xerox Alto, the first computer with a graphical interface, got built because Chuck Thacker said a futuristic computer could be done in three months and an executive bet him a case of wine that it could not.

Two of the entries need to be told more slowly. Marinship, the shipyard at Sausalito, began with a telegram from the Maritime Commission received by the Bechtel company on the second of March, nineteen forty-two. The site was chosen the next day. A proposal was presented in Washington on the ninth, and ten minutes into the presentation the commission told Bechtel to build it. Physical construction started on the twenty-eighth of March, a fortnight later than it might have, because forty-two families lived on the point that was to be demolished and were given two weeks to move. The first ship was finished on the fifteenth of September. That is a hundred and ninety-seven days from telegram to launched hull, and the two-week delay for the families is the only human cost the entry records.

The other is the one entry from this decade, and it is the one a clinical trialist will not be able to hear neutrally. The genome of the new coronavirus was published on the tenth of January, twenty twenty. Three days later Moderna had finalised the sequence of its vaccine candidate. The first batch was manufactured on the seventh of February, and on the twenty-fourth, forty-five days after the sequence appeared, it shipped to the National Institutes of Health for a phase one trial. Then, in Collison's phrasing, two hundred and sixty-six days of clinical trials and regulatory coordination followed, until on the sixteenth of November the company announced an efficacy of ninety-four and a half percent. Read the proportions. Designing the thing took three days. Making the first doses took a month. Establishing that it worked and was safe took nine months, and nine months was, by every historical standard, absurdly fast: the previous record for a vaccine from sequence to licence was measured in years. The entry does not editorialise about which of those phases was the bottleneck. It does not need to.

Chapter Four: What Fast Needed

Set the list's entries side by side and a set of conditions falls into view, none of which Collison states and all of which the entries share. Almost every one had a single person who could say yes and did not have to ask anyone else: Somervell, Hock, Johnson, the Maritime Commission administrator who interrupted a presentation at the ten-minute mark. Nearly all of them ran against a deadline that was not negotiable, and the deadline was usually a war. The Pentagon, Marinship, the Alaska Highway, the P-eighty and the Berlin Airlift, in which the Western allies flew two hundred and seventy-seven thousand flights in four hundred and sixty-three days to feed two million people, and built an entire airport at Tegel from first spade to first landing in ninety-two days along the way, are all products of a state at war or on the edge of it. Apollo Eight was a Cold War move made in a year when the Soviets seemed likely to send a man around the moon first. Moderna had a pandemic. Collison makes the wartime point himself on the Questions page with a number that is easy to forget: American involvement in the Second World War lasted three years, eight months and twenty-three days. The whole of it, from Pearl Harbor to the Japanese surrender, fits inside the time it took to complete the Second Avenue subway's environmental review. He sets that against a contemporary comparison from his own city: a sixteen-mile extension of the Bay Area's rail system, costing about two point three billion dollars and taking around seven years, delayed for more than a year because the wrong networking equipment had been installed. A country that built Marinship in six months could not, seventy-five years on, get the right router into a train station in less than twelve.

The third condition is the one that matters for anyone who runs anything, and it is the absence of veto points. The forty-two families at Marinship were given two weeks. Nobody sued. No environmental review was filed, because none existed. No neighbouring agency could demand a design change and decline to pay for it. Collison's own hypotheses section gathers the writers who have tried to date the change: a foreword to Herbert Kaufman's book on red tape claiming the nature of bureaucratic constraint in America shifted in the nineteen sixties; the political scientist James Q. Wilson's textbook contrast between an old system and a new system of American government, with the same decade as the seam; Mancur Olson's argument that stable societies accumulate interest groups the way a hull accumulates barnacles, each with a small claim and a veto; and Francis Fukuyama's word for the endpoint, vetocracy, a government in which so many parties can block a decision that none can make one. The transit writer Alon Levy adds, from the page's own citations, that it is not only construction that slowed but operation: American agencies run their existing systems worse, too. Ed Glaeser, reviewing a history of the fifteen-year fight over rebuilding Ground Zero, supplies the closing number for this whole line of thought. In nineteen thirty-one the Port Authority of New York built the George Washington Bridge, then the longest suspension span on earth, in forty-nine months.

Chapter Five: The List's Missing Column

Now the case against, and the list makes it easy to state because the list is honest about what it is: examples of success. A list of ambitious things finished quickly is, by construction, a list with no failures on it, and the fast failures are what the vetocracy was built to prevent.

Robert Moses built faster than anyone on that page. He put expressways through the Bronx in years rather than decades, and the neighbourhoods he cut in half did not recover for a generation; Robert Caro's account of him is the founding document of the new system Collison's sources describe, and the environmental review, the community meeting and the lawsuit are the direct descendants of what he did to East Tremont. Pruitt-Igoe in St. Louis, thirty-three eleven-storey towers, went up fast in the mid nineteen fifties and came down, on television, in nineteen seventy-two. Thalidomide moved from a German laboratory to the pharmacies of forty-six countries in under four years at the end of that same decade, and the two hundred and sixty-six days of clinical trials that Collison's Moderna entry records exist in their modern form because of what that speed cost. The forty-two families on Pine Point got two weeks. The thing that stands between a modern agency and a two-week eviction is not wet weather.

So the vetocracy is an antibody, and the honest form of Collison's question is the one an immunologist would ask: not how to get rid of the antibody but why it now attacks the host. The process that stops a Moses and the one that adds seven thousand days to a bus lane are the same mechanism at two doses, and the list shows the disease without measuring the dose. The Van Ness lane needed no one evicted, no river crossed, no neighbourhood destroyed. It needed a decision, and the system had distributed the power to say no so widely that a decision was the one thing it could not produce. Dan Davies would recognise the shape from this feed's second episode: an accountability sink, where a bad outcome, twenty-one years for a bus lane, arrives with nobody's name on it because every party that delayed it was exercising a legitimate check. And Scott Alexander's trap from three episodes back is here too. Any single agency that waived its review would carry the blame alone if anything went wrong, so none does, and the sum of everyone's prudence is a city that cannot paint a lane.

Both sides of this exist in miniature in the maternal mental health counselling programme this show's listener runs at a distance in Sierra Leone, whose monitoring I have had a hand in building. When the team needs to change a data form, a new question, a fixed skip pattern, a safety screen, it can be written, tested and on every counsellor's phone across ten districts in a few days, because one small group owns the forms and nobody else has a veto. That is the Fast list in miniature, and it is the reason the programme can respond to what its own data show. The same programme's protocol changes, the ones that touch what is done to a participant rather than what is recorded about her, go through ethics committees in two countries and take months, and nobody involved would have it otherwise, because the antibody exists for a reason. The skill is knowing which of the two you are looking at, and most institutions have stopped being able to tell.

Chapter Six: The Question Underneath the Series

The second page, Questions, is a list of things Collison says he finds interesting and cannot answer, seventeen or so of them, with a note that he has left out the ones about Stripe. The range is the portrait. Why is American economic growth, plotted on a log scale across a hundred and fifty years, so eerily close to a straight line through wars and depressions, and what does that say about whether anything a government does matters in the long run? Is the finding that one-to-one tutoring moves a student two standard deviations up the distribution real, and if so why has nobody built a system around it? What is the successor to the scientific paper, a format designed for the printing press and still, he notes, typeset in software from the nineteen eighties? Why are so many ordinary things, trains, food, public toilets, so much nicer in Switzerland and Japan? Why does a city of under a million people, Stockholm, keep producing globally successful companies? Each is asked in a paragraph, with the best sources he knows linked, and with named responses from other writers appended when they arrive, so the page is less a list than a slow correspondence. Two of the questions are the reason this series is starting where it is. The first is one this feed spent its last two episodes on: why certain things keep getting more expensive. His framing is a set of numbers, health spending up ninefold in real terms since nineteen sixty, school spending per pupil up two to three times, college more than doubled since the mid eighties, and a pointer, for more, to Slate Star Codex. So the bridge from the Alexander episodes to this shelf is one he built himself. The interesting part is the sub-question he adds that Alexander did not: how would you predict, in advance, whether a given sector will get cheaper or dearer over the coming decades? Nobody has a good answer. If someone did, it would be the most useful single tool a health system planner could own.

The second question is the one I think ties this entire feed together, and it is phrased with unusual care. How do you ensure an adequate replacement rate in systems that have no natural way to die? He credits Schumpeter with the observation that capitalism's real problem is not how capital gets allocated among existing structures but how structures get created and destroyed, and notes that systems and institutions inevitably get stale while working very hard to survive. Bankruptcy, he says, takes care of senescent businesses. What does the same job for everything else?

The short book he points to for that question is Herbert Kaufman's "Are Government Organizations Immortal?", published by the Brookings Institution in nineteen seventy-six, and it is the empirical floor under the whole of this show's first thirty episodes. Kaufman took the federal organisations that existed in nineteen twenty-three, a hundred and seventy-five of them, and checked which still existed fifty years later. About eighty-five percent did. Twenty-seven had gone, and in the same half-century two hundred and forty-six new ones had been created, so the population had more than doubled while barely anything left it. He had expected to find immortality was a myth. He found instead that it was very nearly the rule, and that the organisations which did die were mostly small, young, and killed by a specific political enemy rather than by any general process of selection. That is the finding behind Parkinson's growing staff, Peter's incompetent plateau, Jackall's managers who survive by managing blame, and Davies's sinks: none of those pathologies would matter much if the organisations that harboured them were subject to anything resembling a death rate. They are not. So the pathologies compound, and the vetocracy of the Fast page is what a population of immortal organisations looks like from the outside: everyone still here, everyone with a claim, no one with the power to finish anything.

Chapter Seven: The Question He Could Fund

A third question gives the series its later episodes. How, he asks, can we understand the dynamics of progress in science, when the National Institutes of Health spends about thirty-seven billion dollars a year and there are, in his words, essentially no books about the NIH and little investigation of whether the allocation works? Anyone who has spent a career writing grants to that institution will feel the observation land, because it is true. There is a large literature on the science the NIH funds and almost none on the NIH as an institution making choices, and the choices are consequential: which questions get asked, by whom, at what age, for how long, with how much reporting.

The reason he thinks the question is urgent is the argument he and Nielsen made in the Atlantic, and the evidence behind it is stronger than a magazine essay usually carries. They surveyed working physicists, chemists and biologists, asking each to compare pairs of Nobel-winning discoveries from different decades and say which mattered more, and the later decades lost: the discoveries of the nineteen eighties and nineties were rated, by the scientists best placed to judge, as less important than those of the nineteen twenties and thirties, even as the number of scientists and the money spent had grown many times over. A team of economists led by Nicholas Bloom published a more formal version of the same finding in twenty twenty under the title "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?", showing that across semiconductors, crop yields and medicine, the research effort required to sustain a constant rate of progress has been rising steeply for decades; in the case of the doubling of transistor density, by their estimate, it now takes something like eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early nineteen seventies. There are serious objections to how both studies measure output, and this series will take them up properly when it reaches the science-as-an-institution run. What matters here is what the finding did to its author. A man who believes that the returns to science are falling, and that nobody is studying why, is a man who will end up publishing Bush and Braben and Waldrop, and funding an institute, and asking whether the paper and the journal are the right containers for knowledge at all. The shelf is downstream of the survey.

Fast Grants was his attempt to run an experiment on that question rather than write about it. In April of twenty twenty, with the usual funding bodies taking months to review pandemic proposals, he and Cowen and a small group of donors set up a fund that promised decisions within forty-eight hours and money within a week, with a one-page application reviewed by working scientists. It disbursed more than fifty million dollars. What interests me is less the grants than the survey the organisers published afterwards. They asked the recipients, established scientists with ordinary federal funding, two questions. Roughly four in five said that if their existing grants could be spent however they judged best, they would change their research programme substantially. Roughly two in three said their work would move faster with fewer constraints on how funds were used. Those are self-reports from people who had just received money from the people asking, so discount them as you would any satisfied customer's testimonial. Even discounted by half, they describe a funding system in which a large share of the funded scientists believe the grant's conditions, not the grant's size, are what slows them. That is Kaufman's immortal organisation seen from inside the grant: a set of constraints that nobody chose, that everyone follows, and that no one has the standing to remove. The Arc Institute, which he co-founded in twenty twenty-one with the biologists Silvana Konermann and Patrick Hsu, is the same experiment run for the long term rather than the emergency: investigators receive multi-year funding for themselves rather than for a specified project, renewable, with no grant applications in between, on the theory that the best people should be chosen once and then left alone. Whether that produces more science per dollar than the NIH's project-by-project review is, at the moment, unknown, and to his credit he says so. The episodes on Bush, Braben and Licklider that follow are all earlier attempts to answer the question of what a funder should do instead, and each was published by the man who asked it.

Chapter Eight: Advice to a Boy

The third page is the shortest and the most revealing about its author. Every so often, Collison writes, someone emails to ask how to change the world, and with the caveat that he has lived only a fraction of one life, here is what he would tell his past self. The advice is addressed to someone between ten and twenty, and much of it is about depth: go deep on things, on several things, and by twenty know the shape of what you enjoy, because that will not change much. Make things, because operating under uncertainty is a different experience from learning. Make friends over the internet with people who are great at what you care about. Find vivid examples of success in your domain and spend time near them. Nobody, he warns the boy, is going to teach you to think for yourself, and a large fraction of what the people around you believe is mistaken, so the correlation between your worldview and theirs should not be too high unless you think you were unusually lucky in your starting conditions. He offers a heuristic for checking: do your friends at school think your path is a bit strange? If not, it may be too normal. Then two observations that carry more than the rest. Status lags by a generation or more, so if you think something is important and older people do not, there is a reasonable chance you are right and they are wrong. And people who did great things very often did them young; they were grey-haired when they became famous, not when they did the work. So, he tells the boy, hurry up.

Then the section for ages twenty to thirty, which reads in full: I don't know yet. I plan to think about this when I'm thirty-five to forty.

Read that page with a clinician's habits and the diagnosis is easy. It is the advice of someone for whom the advice worked, written to someone with the same starting conditions, and it does not know which of its recommendations were causes and which were luck. The Schelling-point paragraph, urging the reader to find a way to San Francisco because that is where the high-openness, energetic, optimistic people gather, is true of a particular fifteen years and a particular industry, and would have been terrible advice to a young Ostrom or a young Perrow. The Fast list's survivorship problem reappears on the Advice page, one level down: a list of what worked, assembled by someone it worked for.

And yet I have come to think the honesty of that blank section redeems the rest. Almost every advice page on the internet is written by someone confident about decades they have not yet understood. This one stops where its author's knowledge stops, and the two observations that survive the survivorship discount, that status lags a generation and that the work gets done young, are ones a fellowship director should carry around. The first says the thing your trainees are excited about and your senior colleagues dismiss is more likely to be the future than the past. The second says that the years in which they could do their best work are the years they are currently spending on the requirements you set them, and that a training system optimised for the safety of the institution is spending the scarcest resource its trainees have.

Chapter Nine: The Dose

The three pages, taken together, prescribe an attitude rather than a method, and the dosage question is how much of the attitude to swallow. In diagnostic doses it is a set of comparisons that anyone running anything should keep in a drawer. The Pentagon and the bus lane. The three days to design a vaccine against the nine months to prove it. Kaufman's eighty-five percent. The four in five funded scientists who would do something different if allowed. Each is a number that tells you where in your own system the days are going, and Collison's habit of asking why, without pretending to know, is the habit this show most wants to import from him. It is also, I should say, a habit that costs him nothing. A man whose company has never needed a grant, a permit or an ethics committee can afford to find the slowness of institutions merely interesting; the people who work inside them find it exhausting, and the difference in temperature is not a flaw in his questions so much as a limit on who gets to ask them that calmly. In identity doses the same attitude becomes the Moses posture, in which every check is red tape and every objector is a veto point and the forty-two families should be grateful for their two weeks. The Fast page does not endorse that reading. Its own hypotheses section is full of people explaining why the checks exist. But a reader who takes the list and skips the question at the bottom will end up there, and a great many readers of that page have.

Chapter Ten: The Thing That Could Be Done by Friday

There is something in your working life that a single person with authority and a deadline could finish in a tenth of the time it currently takes. This week, name it, and then do the harder part: list every party that can currently say no to it, and for each one ask whether its veto is an antibody, protecting someone from a real harm, or a barnacle, a claim that accreted because nothing ever scraped it off. If the list is all barnacles, you have found the bus lane. If any of them is an antibody, you have also found why speed alone is not the whole story, and Collison's question, asked properly, is what you do with the barnacles while keeping the antibody.

Next time, the first book from the shelf: Richard Hamming's "The Art of Doing Science and Engineering," with his nineteen eighty-six Bell Labs lecture "You and Your Research" as its spine, on why most scientists never work on important problems, what the open door costs and buys, and the uncomfortable half of his own argument that the people with the door closed get more done this year. Thanks for listening.
