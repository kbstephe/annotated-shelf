Scott Alexander, Beware the Man of One Study and The Control Group Is Out of Control: what parapsychology proves about everyone else's evidence

Two 2014 essays read as one argument: how a literature gets weaponised one paper at a time, why a field studying a phenomenon that almost certainly does not exist still clears the standard bar for proof, and what a trialist owes the next person who reads her paper, tested against the replication crisis that arrived on schedule.

Chapter One: Ninety Replications

In twenty eleven the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, the most cited journal in its field, published a paper by Daryl Bem, a Cornell professor emeritus with four decades of respectable work behind him, reporting nine experiments on more than a thousand undergraduates. The experiments were ordinary social psychology procedures, priming, memory rehearsal, habituation, with one alteration: Bem ran them backwards in time. Students were shown a list of words, tested on their recall, and only afterwards given a subset of the list to practise. Recall was better for the items the students would later rehearse than for those they never saw again. The cause came after the effect. Across the nine experiments the average effect was small, about a fifth of a standard deviation, and eight of the nine were statistically significant at the conventional threshold.

The paper had passed peer review at a top journal. The methods were the field's own, and so were the statistics. And the finding was that undergraduates can feel the future.

Three years later, Bem and colleagues published a meta-analysis of ninety experiments attempting to replicate the effect, from thirty-three laboratories in fourteen countries, involving tens of thousands of participants. The pooled result was significant at a p-value below one in ten billion, with a Bayes factor of several billion to one in favour of precognition, and a funnel plot that showed no obvious sign of publication bias. By every quantitative standard that a medical journal, a nutrition journal or a psychology journal applies to decide whether a finding is real, precognition had been established. Scott Alexander's essay of April twenty fourteen, "The Control Group Is Out of Control," takes that result as its starting point, and the question it asks is not whether Bem is right. It is what it means for everyone else that he can look right.

Chapter Two: The Placebo Science

The essay opens by crediting the frame to a writer named Allan Crossman, who called parapsychology the control group for science. The analogy is exact enough to spell out. In a drug trial, the fact that patients on the drug got better tells you nothing until you know how many patients on a sugar pill also got better, because that second number is the rate of recovery that happens whether or not the drug works. Move up a level. The fact that a research field studying some phenomenon produces positive findings tells you nothing until you know how many positive findings a field would produce studying a phenomenon that does not exist, because that second number is the rate at which the machinery of science, applied by sincere and competent people, generates "evidence" regardless of whether there is anything there.

You could never set that control up on purpose. You would need a phenomenon that certainly does not exist, a community of trained scientists who sincerely believe it does, and several decades of funding to let them study it with the field's standard tools. Nature ran the experiment anyway. Parapsychology is a small but real research community, with journals, conferences, university affiliations and, by the essay's account and by the account of several sceptics who have gone to look, methodological standards that are in some respects better than mainstream psychology's, in part because the sceptics have been arguing with them for decades. Ray Hyman, one of the most persistent critics of the field, co-authored a set of methodological recommendations with the parapsychologist Charles Honorton in nineteen eighty-six, and the field adopted them. So the control condition has been running for a long time, with the safeguards turned up, and the result is that parapsychologists produce significant findings for psychic phenomena at roughly the rate mainstream researchers manage for ordinary ones. Alexander quotes Eliezer Yudkowsky's summary: the standard statistical methods are, on this evidence, "so weak and flawed as to permit a field of study to sustain itself" without a subject.

Chapter Three: The Staring Experiment

The essay's centrepiece is a paper Alexander says sends him into nervous laughter every time he reads it, and once you have heard it you will understand why. There is a classic parapsychology protocol in which a subject sits alone in a room, wired to a skin-conductance monitor, while at random intervals an experimenter in another room stares at them through a video link. The hypothesis is that being stared at raises a person's physiological arousal even with no ordinary channel by which they could know it is happening. Marilyn Schlitz, a believer in psychic phenomena, had run the experiment repeatedly and found the effect. Richard Wiseman, a psychologist and sceptic, had run it just as often and got nothing.

They did what almost nobody in a scientific dispute ever does. They designed a single joint experiment, agreeing on every detail of the apparatus and procedure, and ran it in the same laboratory with the same equipment, alternating who conducted each session. Because everything else was fixed, "conducting" a session meant greeting the participant, reading the instructions, and doing the staring. Schlitz's sessions found the effect. Wiseman's found nothing. Same room, same wires, same script, same randomisation, and the only variable that predicted the result was which of the two authors had shaken the participant's hand. In the discussion section, where authors are supposed to propose explanations for their findings, each of them raised the possibility that the other had tampered with the data.

Alexander's reading is that this is the experiment that ought to be replicated ninety times, because it exposes a category of problem that none of the standard safeguards is built to catch. Randomisation defends against confounding between groups. Blinding keeps the experimenter from knowing which group a subject is in. Preregistration stops the analyst choosing the test after seeing the data. None of them defends against whatever it is that lets one experimenter's presence in the room produce a finding and another's abolish it, and the parapsychologists, who have been living with this for decades, have a name for it, the experimenter effect, and a body of theory about believers emitting the right kind of aura. Other fields have no such excuse and the same problem. Alexander cites the psychotherapy outcome literature, where the most reliable finding across dozens of meta-analyses is that whichever therapy the investigator running the trial happens to favour is the one that works best, with an effect size around half a standard deviation. That is an allegiance effect of the same size as the effect of many treatments themselves, and it appears in a mainstream field that does not believe in auras.

Chapter Four: What Bem Actually Did

Having granted the meta-analysis its numbers, Alexander does the thing this show has come to value him for, which is to go and read the ninety studies. What he found is instructive for anyone who has ever cited a pooled estimate. Most of the ninety were not peer-reviewed. Many were conference presentations, unpublished manuscripts, or student projects that Bem had solicited directly. That is a legitimate way to combat publication bias, since it captures results that no journal would print, but it also means the meta-analysis is only as good as the worst laboratory in it, and nobody outside those laboratories had checked the work. Many were not exact replications either, despite being presented as such; small changes in procedure that a believer would consider immaterial and a sceptic would consider decisive.

So Alexander filtered the list down to the studies that were both exact replications and peer-reviewed. He found seven. Three found sizeable positive effects, two found effects near zero, and two found sizeable negative effects. In the subset a sceptic would trust, then, the distribution looks like what you would expect if the true effect were nothing and the noise were large. Among the seven were three experiments by a team including Wiseman, whose careful attempt to replicate Bem had initially been rejected by the same journal that published the original, on the grounds that it did not publish replications. The journal later reversed the policy under public pressure, which tells you something about the machinery Bem's result had passed through on its way to print.

Alexander's conclusion about Bem is unambiguous: he thinks Bem is wrong. But he refuses the comfortable version of that conclusion. The scrutiny he had to apply to demonstrate that Bem's meta-analysis was not trustworthy, reading every included study, checking review status, checking whether replications were exact, is a standard that, in his estimate, almost no study in any field would survive. A handful of very large medical trials might. The ordinary paper, in the ordinary journal, that the ordinary clinician cites when she changes what she prescribes, would not. Bem is the control condition returning a positive result, and the appropriate response when the placebo arm improves is to distrust the assay, not to congratulate yourself on catching the one false positive you happened to look at closely.

Chapter Five: The Man of One Study

Eight months after the control group essay, in December of the same year, Alexander published a shorter and more practical companion under a title adapted from a warning attributed to Thomas Aquinas, who is supposed to have said he feared the man of one book. Alexander's version swaps the book for a study, and the essay explains why a literature, even an honest one, is a weapon waiting to be picked up.

Suppose a drug is weakly effective against some condition. Over a few years a dozen or two research groups study it, with different designs, different populations, and different degrees of care. Even with no fraud and no incompetence anywhere, the results will scatter. Most studies will find the true small effect. A few, by chance and design variation, will report that the drug works dramatically well. A few will show nothing. One or two will suggest harm. The scatter is not a flaw in the science. It is what sampling variation and heterogeneity of design produce when many people study the same thing, and the more rigorous the field, the narrower the scatter, but it never collapses to a point. Now add the ordinary fact that the studies were not all asking the same question: some tested the drug for acute illness and some for maintenance, some in a mild form of the disease and some in a severe one. Alexander uses valproate in bipolar disorder as his example, a drug that works well for mania, poorly for depression, and differently again for prevention. Pool those into one literature titled "does this drug work" and the scatter widens further, all of it defensible study by study.

Into that literature walks anyone with a case to make. The alternative medicine site does not deny the science. It cites the one study from the harmful tail, by respectable authors at a respectable institution, and says doctors are ignoring it. The pharmaceutical representative at the sponsored lunch brings the one from the dramatic tail. When the doctor says one study proves nothing, the representative produces a second and a third from the same tail, plus a replication, and the doctor, who has not read the literature and has patients to see, nods. Both parties have evidence. Both have several studies. Neither is lying about any individual paper. The only thing missing from each account is the shape of the whole distribution, and the shape is the finding.

Alexander adds a detail about how the stripping happens that anyone who has written a paper will wince at. A trial of that bipolar drug for six-month maintenance in ninety-one patients will be titled something like that, and will trust that the professionals reading it know that maintenance is a different question from acute mania. It will not say, in the title or the abstract, that it makes no claim about acute treatment, because no paper says what it is not about. So the study enters the literature as "Smith and colleagues found the drug ineffective," the scope gone without anyone noticing, and every subsequent citation inherits the amputation. Nobody misquoted anything. The paper's authors simply assumed a reader who would supply the context, and the reader who actually arrived was a partisan looking for a citation.

Chapter Six: The Minimum Wage, and Why It Never Ends

Alexander's worked case is the one literature almost every educated person believes they know: whether raising the wage floor costs jobs. He walks through what an honest reader encounters. The famous Card and Krueger study from nineteen ninety-two, comparing fast food employment across the New Jersey and Pennsylvania border after New Jersey raised its minimum, finding no job loss. The counterclaims that it was statistical malpractice. The authors' rebuttal. Then a generation of larger and more sophisticated studies, some finding no effect, others finding substantial ones. Then the meta-analyses, which ought to settle it, except that there is a meta-analysis of fourteen studies concluding the negative effects are publication bias, another of sixty-four reaching the same verdict, one of fifty-five countries finding effects in most, and a systematic review of about a hundred studies finding strong and consistent job losses. Then the expert surveys: five hundred economists signed a letter saying a rise would be harmful, and six hundred signed one saying it would be beneficial. One survey of leading economists found them agreeing nearly four to one that the benefits outweigh the costs; another, of labour economists, found seventy-three percent expecting job losses.

Alexander then writes the two summaries a partisan would produce from this material, one for each side, and the exercise is uncomfortable to read because both are accurate in every particular and each would convince a smart reader who saw only it. Each cites real studies, real meta-analyses, real letters signed by hundreds of real economists. Each is a list of true things arranged to conceal the existence of the other list. His warning follows from the demonstration. A smart person learns early not to accept an argument from authority. What most have not learned is that a list of dozens of studies, several meta-analyses, hundreds of experts and a survey of the field can also be assembled to prove either side of a contested question, and that this is the form an argument takes when someone wants to convince an educated audience of something.

He does not leave it at radical scepticism, and the tool he reaches for is one a trialist will recognise. Only one side of the minimum wage argument, he observes, had produced a funnel plot, the diagram that puts every study's effect estimate against its precision. The plot is the whole bell curve made visible. It shows the studies clustering around zero, with a longer tail on the job-loss side than the job-gain side, an asymmetry that in any other literature would be read as publication bias, small negative results getting printed while small positive ones went in the drawer. So there is a signal in the noise, and it points somewhat toward the side that says the effect is small. He also concedes, in the next breath, that the eleven studies in the far tail may each be right about the specific industry, wage level and economic moment they studied, the valproate problem again, and that the real answer is almost certainly "it depends" in ways no single number captures.

Chapter Seven: The Crisis Arrived on Schedule

Read in twenty fourteen, both essays sounded like a blogger's provocation. Read now, they look like a forecast that came in early. The year after Bem's paper, three psychologists, Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn, published "False-Positive Psychology," a demonstration that a researcher using only the ordinary, accepted flexibilities of analysis, choosing when to stop collecting data, which of several outcome measures to report, which covariates to include, could produce a significant result for essentially any hypothesis, and they illustrated it by showing, with real data and standard methods, that listening to a particular Beatles song made undergraduates measurably younger. Bem's paper was widely understood as the provocation that made that demonstration urgent. Two years later Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken named the underlying mechanism the garden of forking paths: a researcher does not need to consciously fish for significance, because every dataset presents dozens of reasonable analytic choices, and a researcher who makes those choices after seeing the data walks one path through the garden while the p-value pretends she walked the only one. Nobody has to cheat. The multiplicity is invisible from inside.

Then came the replications. In twenty fifteen the Open Science Collaboration reported an attempt to replicate a hundred published findings from three leading psychology journals; about a third to a half replicated, depending on the criterion, and the effect sizes of those that did were about half the originals. John Ioannidis had published his argument that most published research findings are false a decade earlier, in two thousand five, with a model showing that in fields with small samples, small effects, flexible designs and strong prior beliefs, the majority of significant results would be false positives, and the two thousand tens turned his model into a series of headlines. Medicine had its own version of the funnel plot lesson from the same author in the same year. Ioannidis took forty-nine of the most cited clinical research papers of the previous fifteen years, the ones that had changed practice, and followed each claim forward to see what later, larger studies found. Around one in six of the claims had been flatly contradicted. A similar number had survived, but with an effect much smaller than the famous paper reported. Fewer than half had been replicated at roughly the original size. Those were not obscure studies from the tail of anyone's distribution. They were the dots that got shown to every clinician, and a third of them were in the wrong place. Parapsychology's most useful contribution to science was to hit that wall a few years before everyone else did, on a question where the answer was obvious enough that people looked at the method rather than the finding.

Two episodes ago this shelf spent an hour on the multipolar trap, and the machinery behind the crisis is one. A researcher who preregisters her analysis, reports her null results, and declines to run the seventh subgroup test publishes less than a colleague who does none of those things, and in a field that counts publications, she loses ground to him every year she keeps her standards. A journal that prints replications gives up pages that would have carried novel findings and citations, so for decades almost none did, which is why Wiseman's careful failure to replicate Bem was turned away by the journal that had printed Bem. Nobody in that system needed to be dishonest. Every actor was doing the locally correct thing, and the field as a whole drifted toward a literature that a determined reader could dismantle and a partisan could quote. The reforms that have made a difference since, registered reports, journals committed in advance to publish whatever the preregistered study finds, funders requiring data sharing, are all the same move that took the antibiotics off the farms: a change to what the competition rewards, imposed on every competitor at once, so that keeping your standards stops being a way to lose.

Chapter Eight: The Steelman, and the Case Against

Let me give the essays their strongest form before I test them. The claim is not that science does not work. It is that the standard statistical apparatus, a p-value below five percent, a significant meta-analysis, a positive replication or two, is necessary for a finding but nowhere near sufficient, and that a field which treats it as sufficient will fill its journals with results that a determined reader could dismantle and almost nobody ever does. The evidence for that claim is a natural control condition that returned positive, plus a mainstream literature in which allegiance effects rival treatment effects, plus a contested policy question on which two complete and contradictory evidence bases coexist. An admirer of the essays would sign off on that summary, and so would most methodologists writing today.

Now the objections, and they are real. The first concerns Alexander's own reading of the parapsychology literature, which is more selective than the essay's confident tone suggests. He treats Bem's meta-analysis as the field's best shot and dismantles it, but the field's actual flagship is the ganzfeld literature, sensory-deprivation telepathy experiments with a much longer history, and there the story is messier. A meta-analysis by Julie Milton and Wiseman in nineteen ninety-nine found no effect in the post-communiqué studies. A later one by Lance Storm and colleagues in twenty ten, with more studies and different inclusion rules, found one. The sceptics and believers have been arguing about which inclusion rules are correct ever since, in the pattern Alexander's own minimum wage essay describes, and he does not turn that essay's lens on his own choice of which parapsychology to examine. He picked the meta-analysis that happened to be easiest to take apart. The ganzfeld work is harder. Bem himself, with Honorton, had published a meta-analysis of the automated ganzfeld studies in the Psychological Bulletin in nineteen ninety-four, under the post-communiqué protocol the sceptics had helped design, reporting a hit rate well above chance, and Hyman's response, printed alongside it, conceded that the studies were well conducted while withholding belief in the conclusion. That is a more interesting object than Bem's solicited replications, because it is the field's best evidence produced under its critics' own rules, and the essay walks past it. A believer would say that is the man of one study, one level up.

The second objection is about the target. Alexander presents the control group result as an indictment of frequentist statistics, and then in the same essay shows that the Bayesians on both sides of the Bem dispute behaved just as badly: Bem multiplying a ten-to-the-twenty prior by his Bayes factor as if that settled anything, and his critic Erik-Jan Wagenmakers setting a prior so extreme that no conceivable data could move it. The problem was never the choice of statistical framework. It was that every framework takes the data as given, and the data were produced by a process, solicited studies, experimenter effects, undocumented flexibility, that no calculation performed afterwards can see. The essay knows this. Its title says the control group is out of control, not that the t-test is broken. But readers took the wrong lesson away in large numbers, and a decade of methodological reform has spent a great deal of energy on the statistics and rather less on the room in which Schlitz and Wiseman shook hands.

The third is an objection to the tone of the second essay, and it comes from within the same circle. If a smart reader cannot trust a study, a set of studies, a meta-analysis, a systematic review, an expert letter or an expert survey, and is told to distrust any source that promises to summarise the evidence, what is left is a counsel of despair dressed as sophistication. Alexander half-admits this, with a joke about radical scepticism sounding easier than doing the work. His positive advice is thin: lower your confidence on contested questions, and before accepting an overwhelming case, spend five minutes checking whether the other side has one. That is good advice. It is also advice that leaves the reader exactly where she started on the minimum wage, and on most things.

Chapter Nine: What a Trialist Owes

This show is written for someone who runs randomised trials, and these two essays describe the two temptations of that trade from the outside with unusual accuracy. The first temptation is to become the man of one study about your own study. A trial you ran is, to you, the most vivid piece of evidence on its question, because you know its every weakness and every strength and you watched the data arrive. It is also, to the literature, one dot on a funnel plot, and where it sits on that plot is not up to you. The discipline the first essay asks for is to write the paper so that the next reader can place the dot: state which version of the question was asked, in which population, at which dose, for which outcome, so that the study cannot be stripped of its scope and quoted as "Smith found the drug ineffective." Most of what looks like conflict between trials is two trials answering different questions, and the authors, not the readers, are the only people positioned to prevent that confusion, in the title if possible and in the abstract at the least.

The second temptation is the one the control group exposes, and it is harder, because nothing in the design protects against it. Preregistration closes the garden of forking paths for the primary analysis, and clinical trials now treat it as mandatory, which is a reform parapsychology adopted before psychology did, as Alexander notes with some embarrassment on his own field's behalf. But Schlitz and Wiseman preregistered, in effect, a single joint protocol, and the result still depended on who was in the room. In a trial where the intervention is delivered by people, a counselling programme, a feeding programme, a training, the people delivering it believe in it, and their belief travels through every interaction with the participant in ways a protocol cannot fix. The defence is not statistical. It is to build the trial so that the measurement is made by someone who does not know what the participant received and, where possible, does not much care, and to treat any outcome that requires the deliverer's own judgment as a different and weaker kind of evidence. Read a trial of a behavioural intervention in which the therapists rated the outcome, and you should hear Schlitz greeting her participants.

The programme where this bites hardest is the one this show's listener runs, a maternal mental health counselling programme across several districts of Sierra Leone, where lay counsellors deliver a talking therapy and record, on a phone form, how each session went. I have spent a good deal of time inside that programme's data, and the forms are where the two essays stop being theory. Those forms are the programme's nervous system, and they are also, every one of them, an outcome rated by the person who delivered the intervention and believes in it. A counsellor who has sat with a woman for six sessions and watched her improve is Schlitz in that room. She is not lying. She is a sincere experimenter reporting what she saw, and what she saw was shaped by what she hoped to see. So the programme's monitoring is built to carry a second channel that does not run through her: a follow-up call from someone who did not deliver the sessions, a symptom scale administered by a different person weeks later, an occasional recorded session reviewed by someone with no stake in that counsellor's numbers. None of that is a statistical fix. It is the Wiseman half of the joint experiment, built into routine, on the principle the essays argue for without quite stating: the only defence against an experimenter effect is a second experimenter.

Chapter Ten: The Dose

Put both essays through the dosage test and they come out well, with one warning. The diagnostic dose is a habit: when a colleague, a reviewer, a guideline or a grant application presents a study as settling something, ask to see the plot, or ask where on the plot this one sits, or ask what the control condition for this kind of claim would be and whether anyone has run it. That habit costs a few minutes and catches a great deal. The identity dose is the radical scepticism Alexander jokes about and half-endorses, the posture in which no evidence is ever good enough and every literature is a weapon, which happens to be the posture that a person who wants to keep believing something in the face of good evidence finds most convenient. The essays' own author reached a conclusion about Bem. He did the work, read the seven studies, and decided. The point of learning how evidence gets weaponised was never to stop being persuaded by it. It was to be persuaded by the shape of the whole and not by the dot someone handed you.

Chapter Eleven: The Plot You Have Not Seen

Somewhere in your working life there is a claim you repeat with confidence, a drug that works, a programme that fails, an intervention the guideline endorses, and you have never seen the plot. Not the study, the plot: every estimate on the question set against its precision, with the tails visible. This week, pick the claim you lean on hardest and go and look for it. If nobody has drawn it, that is information. If someone has and the scatter is wider than you assumed, you have just learned how much of your confidence was borrowed from whoever chose which dot to show you.
