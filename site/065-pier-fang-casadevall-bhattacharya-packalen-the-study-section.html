The Study Section (Elizabeth Pier and colleagues, Ferric Fang and Arturo Casadevall, Jay Bhattacharya and Mikko Packalen): a mock review panel that could not agree on twenty-five good grants, the lottery proposed in reply, and the incentive and programme-manager alternatives

A PNAS experiment that rebuilt NIH peer review and found no agreement among reviewers, the modified lottery that Fang and Casadevall proposed, what New Zealand and Switzerland did with it, and the case that the ruler or the judge, not the dice, is what needs changing.

15 October 2026

Chapter One: Twenty-Five Grants and Forty-Three Judges

A study section is a small room. Eight to twelve people have each read a handful of applications in advance and written a score and a critique. The scores run on a nine-point scale where one is exceptional and nine is poor. At the meeting the primary reviewers speak, the room discusses, and the scores are revised. The process is confidential and the materials are destroyed when the meeting ends. The machinery that allocates most of the biomedical research money in the United States leaves almost no record that an outsider can examine.

A team at the University of Wisconsin, led by Elizabeth Pier, decided that the only way to study it was to build one. They worked with a retired scientific review officer, Jean Sipe, who had served the National Institutes of Health since 1997 and had chaired the committee that wrote its handbook for the job. She identified investigators who had received a National Cancer Institute R01 in the previous one to four years and invited them to serve for a five-hundred-dollar honorarium plus expenses. Forty-three oncology researchers agreed. Sipe chose among them the way she would have for a real panel, and they were split across four constructed study sections.

The applications were real. The team asked principal investigators to donate R01 applications they had sent to the two oncology study sections at the National Cancer Institute, and chose twenty-five. Sixteen had been funded the first time. The other nine had been turned down and funded only after revision, and for those nine the team used the original, unfunded version. Names were replaced with pseudonyms that kept gender and nationality. Every application in the pool had therefore been judged fundable by real reviewers at some point, and the one built-in difference is that sixteen cleared the line at the first attempt and nine did not.

Each reviewer served as primary reviewer on two applications, three of them on one, so the study gathered eighty-three critiques, and every application was read by between two and four people. The scores analysed were the preliminary ones, written before the meeting, because an earlier paper from the same group had found that discussion made the disagreement between different panels worse. Members of the team also coded each critique, counting the strengths and the weaknesses the reviewer listed.

Then they asked a plain question. If two reviewers read the same application, how alike are their scores? The statistic for this is the intraclass correlation, which splits the variation in scores between what the application accounts for and what everything else accounts for. For the scores it came out at zero. For the number of strengths listed it was zero. For the number of weaknesses it was 0.017. A second statistic, Krippendorff's alpha, where values above 0.7 are usually considered acceptable, was 0.024 for the scores and within a few hundredths of zero for strengths and weaknesses.

The authors state what zero means in their own data. Two ratings of one application were on average no more alike than ratings of two different applications. Twenty-five applications, each read by about three people, behaved as if they had been eighty-three separate applications with one reader apiece.

One more comparison. The original NIH reviewers had funded sixteen of these on the first pass and declined nine. The mock reviewers scored the funded and the unfunded about equally, and found about the same number of strengths and weaknesses in each. Whatever had separated the sixteen from the nine in the real round, forty-three new readers could not find it.

A very large caveat comes attached, and the authors state it themselves. The twenty-five were all good. Nobody tested whether reviewers can tell a fundable application from a hopeless one, and the paper says agreement might be higher in a pool of mixed quality. Its finding concerns the gap between good and excellent, and a payline of ten percent forces a panel to draw that gap every cycle.

Chapter Two: Consistent Alone, Incompatible Together

A zero is easy to produce for dull reasons, and the first suspicion is carelessness. The paper offers three defences. After each meeting the team asked reviewers to rate how much the constructed process resembled a real study section on a scale from one, completely different, to seven, identical. Eighty-eight percent responded, and eighty-one percent of those called the pre-meeting process very similar or identical. The team also compared the length of the critiques with a national sample of more than eighteen thousand real NIH summary statements, and reports that the mock reviewers wrote comparably. And the key effects were estimated at zero, not at something small, so a larger sample would be unlikely to rescue them. None of the three is fully convincing. The reviewers were volunteers deciding nothing, and the survey records what they said about themselves.

The more interesting finding separates two things that are easily run together. Maybe the reviewers saw the same problems and simply turned them into numbers differently. The team fit a model with three levels.

At the first level, one reviewer reading one application, more weaknesses listed went with a worse score, with a coefficient of 0.13. At the second, one reviewer across several applications, a reviewer who found more weaknesses in application A than in B also scored A worse, and the coefficient was 0.17. Reviewers have internal standards and apply them consistently. The third level is the question the whole design was built for. When reviewer one found more weaknesses in an application than reviewer two did, did reviewer one score it worse? The coefficient was 0.03, with a probability of 0.19. Across reviewers, no relationship could be detected.

Put those together. Each reviewer is a coherent instrument, and the instruments are calibrated differently. One person's four weaknesses is another's seven, and a two means whatever it means to the person who wrote it. Within one mind the ranking of the applications is sensible. Across minds the numbers do not share a unit, and the process works by pooling scores from different minds that each read different applications. A detail in the first model matters later: the count of strengths in a critique had no relation to the score at all. A good score was an absence of named weaknesses, not a heap of praise.

The finding has older neighbours. The mBio essay in the next chapter cites a Canadian analysis suggesting that two primary reviewers build considerable randomness into funding decisions, and an Australian analysis estimating that fifty-nine percent of funded grants could miss funding on the variability of scoring alone. A later paper from the Swiss National Science Foundation cites a Finnish comparison of two expert panels reviewing the same proposals, whose agreement statistic, kappa, was 0.23, and was the same when computed from the mean of the external reviewers' scores, so the meeting added nothing. Pier and colleagues are a recent entry in that literature, and the ones who rebuilt the room.

Everything so far is evidence about the instrument. The question a funder has to answer is whether the noise costs any science, and that needs a different kind of evidence.

Chapter Three: Does a Better Score Mean a Better Grant?

Two groups looked at the same data and drew opposite headlines, which makes the disagreement a tutorial on what a correlation can tell a funder.

The data are more than a hundred and thirty thousand R01 grants funded by the NIH between 1980 and 2008. Danielle Li and Leila Agha tracked what each produced in publications, citations and patents and asked whether the review score predicted it. It did. A one-standard-deviation worse score among awarded grants went with fifteen percent fewer citations, seven percent fewer publications, nineteen percent fewer high-impact publications and fourteen percent fewer follow-on patents, and the relationship survived controls for the investigator's record, institution, career stage and degree. That is the strongest evidence that panels see something real, and the 2016 reanalysis discussed next says it was read at the time as vindicating the system.

Ferric Fang, Anthony Bowen and Arturo Casadevall reanalysed the same data in eLife and restricted it. Li and Agha's sample included funded grants scoring worse than the fortieth percentile, which no institute funds today. Paylines hover around the tenth percentile at some institutes and seldom exceed the twentieth. So Fang and colleagues kept only grants in the top twenty percentiles, 102,740 of them, and asked how well the score predicted output inside that range.

Each grant supported a median of six publications. The slope was real and small: a step of one percentile was worth about 0.13 publications and about ten citations, and the score explained under one percent of the variance in citations. A random forest model, which does not assume a straight line, gave 0.98 percent. On the receiver operating characteristic curve, where 0.5 is a coin flip and one is perfect, the area was 0.54. If only half of applications could be funded, a grant in the top half by citations would have had a fifty-eight percent chance of funding under a ranking by percentile. Even at the very top, 334 of 1,987 grants that scored zero, seventeen percent, produced no citations. Only grants at the second percentile or better outperformed those below.

Both papers are right, and the difference shows why this debate was never going to end in a decision. Li and Agha asked whether review sorts the whole range of funded work, and it does. Fang and colleagues asked whether it sorts the part of the range that is contested, and hardly. A smoke alarm detects a house fire and is useless for ranking two smouldering kitchens.

Here is a constructed example, flagged as constructed, to make the payline's arithmetic visible. A funder has a hundred competent applications and can fund ten. Its panel's scores correlate with true output at about 0.1, which is what a one-percent variance share implies. Funding the top ten by score beats a draw from a hat by a small margin on average, and any single round is dominated by luck. The best that can be said for the ranking is that it is slightly better than the hat and far more expensive.

The reanalysis authors name what their check cannot see. Citations per paper roughly doubled between 1980 and 2004, and since paylines also fell, recent grants carry both better scores and more citations, a correlation unrelated to reviewers' skill. Grants funded despite scoring worse than the payline were thirteen percent of R01s in one government count for 2003 to 2007, are paid late in the fiscal year and are often cut, which can build a correlation by lowering their output. Earlier studies pointed the same way: about four hundred competing renewals at the National Institute of General Medical Sciences, and 1,492 grants at the Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, found no relationship between score and productivity.

All of it rests on citations as the measure of a grant's worth, and the previous episode gave reason for unease about that. If citations favour the incremental, a score that fails to predict them may be failing to predict something panels never tried to reward. For now the working conclusion is narrow. Across independent datasets, in the range where the money is decided, score is a weak predictor of the one output that is cheap to count.

Chapter Four: The Lottery in the Essay

Fang and Casadevall's proposal in mBio in 2016 has a two-stage shape that is simple to state. Stage one is peer review, used to sort applications into meritorious and not. Stage two is a computer-generated draw among the meritorious, with funding to as many as the budget allows. At a payline of ten percent, they suggest, the meritorious pool might be the top twenty to thirty percent. Critiques would be written only for applications judged not meritorious, which removes the face-to-face meeting that argues over rankings. Undrawn applications would be eligible again in four months, an investigator could enter one application per drawing, a separate lottery could protect new investigators, and programme officers could still use selective pay for people who repeatedly make the pool and never win.

Their case adds four pieces to the evidence of the last two chapters. The first is the scale of the machinery. They report that the NIH's Center for Scientific Review had an annual budget of about a hundred and ten million dollars, paying more than twenty-four thousand reviewers to handle about seventy-five thousand applications across about twenty-five hundred panel meetings. The second is bias. They cite a Bayesian analysis of 18,959 R01 proposals scored by 14,041 reviewers that estimated reviewer bias to affect about a quarter of decisions, a figure a published erratum attributes to Valen Johnson's 2008 PNAS paper. Bias bites hardest at low paylines, they argue, because when a tenth of applications are funded one reviewer's objection is enough to sink a proposal. The third is the success rate, which they report has fallen by more than two-thirds since the 1960s while the allocation machinery stayed the same. The fourth is a finance analogy. Burton Malkiel's argument that few fund managers beat an index suggests that experts forecasting the unforeseeable do no better than a rule that does not try, and they cite a simulation on four stock markets in which a random strategy matched four conventional ones with less volatility.

The data in the essay are the data of the earlier chapters, and the bias figure had to be corrected. The finance analogy runs in either direction, since it says random selection matches the average of skilled selection, an argument for a lottery as a cheap equal and not as an improvement. The essay describes the status quo as in essence a lottery without the benefits of being random. The choice, on that reading, is between luck nobody admits to and luck with a published rule.

The essay also predicted its own reception. Adrian Barnett, an Australian researcher who studies funding, answered in a letter a few months later that only one agency had been bold enough to use a lottery, the Health Research Council of New Zealand, and that when he spoke to Australian funders one staff member said it would make it look like they did not know what they were doing. Fang and Casadevall replied that the barrier is political, since agencies are reluctant to admit peer review cannot reliably stratify applications. Barnett added an observation that has aged better than the polemic. A lottery among equally worthy applicants is a randomised trial of funding, which the funder gets for free, since the winners and losers can be followed afterwards. He warned that the New Zealand sample was small and that it might take a decade for differences to show.

Chapter Five: What New Zealand and Switzerland Did

The Health Research Council of New Zealand began in 2013 with a scheme called the Explorer Grant for early-stage, potentially transformative health research. The grant is for 150,000 New Zealand dollars, about a hundred thousand US dollars by the survey authors' conversion, for up to two years, and the scheme is about two percent of what the council spends in a year. The draw is the smaller part of the design.

Applications are six pages and anonymised, and go to one of four panels of three members. Each member votes yes or no on two questions: is the idea potentially transformative, and is it viable. At least two yes votes make an application fundable. Fundable applications receive a random number from a spreadsheet function and are funded in ascending order until the money runs out. A panel member who recognises an applicant steps aside, which in a small country happens often.

The counts for 2013 to 2019: 394 applications, 308 judged ineligible, thirty fundable but not drawn, fifty-six funded. Do the division. Only eighty-six applications, a little over a fifth, reached the lottery, and about two in three of those were funded. The overall success rate was about fourteen percent. The panel does most of the selecting, using a threshold question about transformation and viability, and the dice decide only among those who passed. That is much closer to the Fang and Casadevall design than to the picture critics have when they say a lottery funds anyone.

The council also surveyed applicants from 2013 to 2019, and 126 of 325 responded, thirty-nine percent. Sixty-three percent called random allocation acceptable for this scheme and twenty-five percent did not, while for grants in general forty percent were in favour and thirty-seven percent opposed. Winners were warmer: seventy-eight percent of them backed randomisation here, against forty-four percent of declined applicants. Sixty-nine percent said the lottery had not altered how they wrote, and seventy-five percent said their preparation time was unaffected, with a median of ten days.

The survey's authors list its limits, and they are large. Funded applicants responded more often. The council ran the survey. Respondents recalled a year-old process. The group that was fundable and lost the draw was small. Support in the comments was often conditional: many said randomising is fair only among applications of equal merit or above a quality threshold, and several wanted the strongest applications funded outright, with the draw used only for the rest. That comment is a design proposal, and it is close to what Switzerland did.

The Swiss National Science Foundation first used random selection in a fellowship scheme for junior researchers moving abroad and later allowed it as an option across its programmes, according to search summaries I did not verify further. Rachel Heyard and colleagues, who work at or with the foundation, published the statistical method in 2022. Instead of ranking proposals by average score, which gives false precision at the margin, they fit a model that estimates each proposal's expected rank with a credible interval around it. A provisional funding line is set from the budget. Proposals clearly above it are funded, clearly below are rejected, and proposals whose intervals overlap the line enter the draw. Their motivation is the literature already in this episode, including an estimate that detecting small differences between average scores reliably would take a hundred reviewers or more per proposal. I read the introduction and the start of the method, not the results, so I have no Swiss outcomes to report.

The empirical record of lotteries is therefore two agencies, one applicant survey with a thirty-nine percent response, a statistical method and a promise that a follow-up will take a decade. Nobody has published a comparison of careers between those who won a draw within the fundable pool and those who lost it, and the argument for lotteries rests on how that comparison would come out.

Chapter Six: Cheaper to Run, Costlier to Play

There is a better reason for a lottery than the one the essay emphasises, and it comes from economics. Kevin Gross and Carl Bergstrom published a theoretical analysis in PLOS Biology in 2019 that treats a grant competition as a contest in which applicants invest in proposals to win a prize. A survey of top US universities that they cite found faculty giving eight percent of all their hours, and nineteen percent of the hours available for research, to preparing proposals.

Their model says the waste is built into the structure. When awards match the number of high-value projects, a good project can win at modest cost. As awards become scarcer, an investigator with a high-value project must either work harder for the same chance or work as hard for a smaller one, and the effort spent signalling value approaches the value signalled. In a lottery, applicants compete for admission to the draw. The value of admission scales with the number of awards and so does the cost of getting in, so the payline stops affecting efficiency. The price is that funded projects are of lower value on average. The authors say plainly they know of no empirical comparison. They give two anecdotes. The National Science Foundation moved two divisions in 2012 from a twice-yearly one-stage competition to a yearly two-stage one, and workload did not fall meaningfully. In 2014 Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council halved the length of its Project Grant application, and researchers spent more time preparing proposals, not less.

The New Zealand survey sits uneasily against the prediction. If a lottery lowers the stakes of each application, applicants should write less, and seventy-five percent said their preparation time was unchanged. I would not push that far, since the scheme is six pages, the answers are self-reported, and respondents skew toward winners. It does suggest that the benefit in effort may be smaller where a review screen still precedes the draw.

Gross and Bergstrom add a second benefit. If grants are partly a draw, administrators may stop treating grant success as a measure of a scientist's quality. In the Merton episode, funding just above a line compounded in one programme, and a lottery is a way to make that line honest, since everyone near it would know they landed there by chance.

Chapter Seven: The Case Against the Dice

Four objections to the lottery need a hard statement, and the third is the one I find hardest to answer.

First, the disagreement may be fixable. Pier and colleagues do not recommend a lottery. They recommend studying whether training can get reviewers to translate weaknesses into scores the same way, and whether reviewers can agree on what good science is before they start. A modified lottery is their fallback if agreement cannot be raised, and only after larger replications. A 2015 PLOS ONE study by David Sattler and colleagues gave a short training video to seventy-five public health professors who scored written descriptions of NIH criteria. Reliability between raters, and accuracy against the true value of the scale, rose for experienced reviewers as well as novices, and the authors note that experienced reviewers may overrate how well they know the scale. That test used descriptions of criteria, not full applications, so it says less than it might. It is the only direct test of the remedy I found, and the Wisconsin zero should not be treated as permanent.

Second, the lottery has losers who lose in a new way. In the essay's design only not-meritorious applications get critiques, so a meritorious applicant who loses a draw receives nothing to improve on. Some New Zealand respondents asked why the panel did not simply rank the applications. Whatever one thinks of review as a selection device, a critique is also a service to the applicant, and the Wisconsin data suggest that one reviewer's list of weaknesses is coherent even though it is not shared. A scheme that discards the list discards a free consultation.

Third, the lottery depends on a screen that the same evidence calls unreliable. A draw is fair only among applications of equal merit, and the screen that decides who is in the pool is peer review. Fang and Casadevall say experts can generally weed out proposals that are infeasible or badly conceived, and that may be so. The Wisconsin experiment never tested it, and the draw is only as good as the edge of the pool. New Zealand's panels excluded seventy-eight percent of what they saw. If the screen is noisy near its own threshold, the lottery has relocated the arbitrary line without removing it. The Swiss method answers this by sending to the draw everything whose uncertainty reaches the line, so that the boundary is as wide as the measurement error. That seems the most defensible design, and the one least like a hat.

Fourth, everything rests on a disputed ruler. The Fang and Casadevall case is made in citations and publications. If the aim is work that changes a field's direction, showing that scores do not predict citations does not show that a lottery serves science better, only that it would cost less. The authors themselves ask whether publication productivity is the right metric and doubt that study sections can recognise transformative research. That doubt is where the next paper begins.

Chapter Eight: The Ruler and the Judge

Jay Bhattacharya, at Stanford, and Mikko Packalen, at the University of Waterloo, wrote an NBER working paper in 2020 with a different diagnosis. Their subject is what is scored, not noise in the scoring. Citations have become the dominant way to evaluate scientists over five decades, they say, and a scientist responding to that measure drifts toward incremental work in crowded areas, where citations accumulate, and away from exploratory work that is likelier to fail and likelier to lay groundwork for later breakthroughs. On their account this, and not the exhaustion of easy discoveries, is why science has stagnated. They write against the Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb account from the previous episode by name.

Their model gives an idea three phases: exploration, with high effort and low impact; breakthrough; and incremental advance on a mature idea. Their two examples run long, and both are told here to the end. In 1967 biologists exploring a Yellowstone hot spring isolated a bacterium that survives temperatures lethal to most life, and named it Thermus aquaticus. The paper drew a few citations. In 1976 others isolated its heat-resistant DNA polymerase, with similarly limited effect. In the 1980s Kary Mullis conceived of copying a stretch of DNA over and over, but the first version needed fresh enzyme after every heating, which made it impractical. The breakthrough came when scientists, Mullis among them, learned of the hot-spring work, and automation followed. The second example is CRISPR, noticed in 1987, with two decades of work establishing where it occurs and then, by 2007, its function as an adaptive immune system, before the scientists now expected to win Nobel Prizes began. Even in 2012, they report, leading journals rejected contributions now considered key. Early exploration is hard to recognise and has little impact for a long time, so a measure that rewards impact penalises it.

Their fix is a second ruler. They would index the words and word sequences in each paper, treat each as an idea, date it by when it first appeared in the literature, and call a paper novel when it uses recent ideas. For a demonstration they took 191,354 biomedical papers published in 2001, marked those in the top twenty percent for the recency of their idea inputs, and compared that with citation percentiles in groups of five percent. Novelty and citations were positively correlated and distinct: many novel papers were little cited, and many well-cited papers were built on mature ideas. Rewarding both, they argue, would give credit for exploring. They discuss gaming, mentioning new ideas without using them, and synonyms, which they would handle with the National Library of Medicine's medical thesaurus.

I should be exact about what this paper is. It is a working paper, and the first page says it has not been peer reviewed. The causal claim, that the citation revolution produced the stagnation, is carried by the model and by studies the authors cite, including work finding biomedical research choices becoming more conservative. The paper's own empirical content is the figure showing novelty and citations to be different things. That is a precondition for the proposal and a long way from showing that rewarding novelty would produce breakthroughs. A commentator at the Roots of Progress blog, reached through a search summary, made the same point.

One claim in it bears on funding directly. The authors cite Azoulay and colleagues for the finding that a funding mechanism tolerating early failure yielded more novel work and a ninety-seven percent increase in breakthrough papers. I take that to be the comparison of Howard Hughes Medical Institute investigators with matched NIH-funded scientists, whose abstract I read, though I did not confirm the link or the number against that paper. The pattern it suggests, that how a grant is structured changes what gets tried, is a different lever from the dice or the second ruler. It changes the judge's time horizon.

Chapter Nine: A Person With a Budget

The third alternative drops the committee. In the ARPA model, named for the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, programme managers hold a budget and a technical area and decide whom to fund. Pierre Azoulay, Erica Fuchs, Anna Goldstein and Michael Kearney wrote the standard economists' description. They list four features: a flexible organisation, identification of a technological white space with a programme designed to fill it, discretion in project selection, and active management against technical milestones. At DARPA, they report, about a hundred programme managers oversee roughly three billion dollars a year of outside research.

The third feature bears on a study section. Programme managers at DARPA have great latitude to select performers without external peer review. ARPA-E, the younger energy agency, is under more political scrutiny and collects external reviews as inputs. Goldstein and Kearney found that average review scores were only slightly correlated with selection. In the example programme the paper shows, twenty-two proposals were funded and over half sat below the line a ranking by score would have drawn. Across the portfolio from 2009 to 2015, roughly half of all projects were promoted that way. Some managers say they select on the content of the review comments, not the scores.

Set that beside Chapter Two. Pier and colleagues found each reviewer internally consistent and the reviewers not comparable. A programme manager is a single reviewer who selects a whole portfolio, so the missing common unit is not a problem, because one person is deciding every project. That inference is mine, and the sources do not draw it. A committee averages incompatible rulers, and a manager applies one. What the single ruler gives up is the protection of averaging, which is part of what the bias argument in the Fang essay relies on. If the manager's taste is wrong, nothing dilutes it.

The paper is direct about costs. It calls one of them the dark side of discretion: quality depends on the talent of a few people. There is a tension between autonomy and accountability. Managers answer to a director, who answers to Congress, but they serve about three years, so each programme is an isolated episode, and its failure need not follow the manager to the next job. The model is also hard to evaluate. Transformative outcomes are rare and late, and one cannot measure the incidence of one-in-a-thousand ideas on a timescale a congressional committee cares about. The authors describe empirical evaluation as elusive. The history is part of the evidence: when Tony Tether became director in 2001, they report, DARPA moved to go or no-go milestones and the share of funding going to academic researchers fell by half.

Two limits apply. The model is built for mission-driven work on early-stage technology, and does not translate directly to investigator-initiated biology. And the paper is a description by economists of how agencies say they work, built on interviews and agency accounts. It does not test the model against a lottery, a committee or anything else.

Chapter Ten: Three Failures, Three Fixes

A study section can fail in three separate ways, and each proposal in this episode is aimed at one.

The first failure is noise. Near the payline, the Wisconsin experiment and the studies it joins say, scores carry little information about which grant will produce more. The remedy is the modified lottery, or better measurement carried into the decision as a width, as in the Swiss model. The second is the ruler. If citations are what panels and the later evaluation of panels are tuned toward, the system selects for work that gets cited, and the previous episode's three rulers each measured something different. The remedy is a second ruler. The third is that a committee averages incompatible judges and so selects for what they can all tolerate, a mild version of the Goliath's shadow in the funeral episode, where established figures shaped what their fields would take up. The remedy is the manager, with one coherent standard and an accountable name.

Side by side they interact. A lottery handles the scores as noise, which means a funder that adopts one says its panel's ranking within the pool carries no information. A manager treats the scores as advice, and the ARPA-E data show the advice overridden about half the time. That makes the manager a lottery of one, drawing on private information the committee lacks. The difference is where the arbitrary choice sits. In a lottery the choice is random and the rule is published. In a manager it is a judgment with a name attached.

None of the three has been tested against the others. That is the main thing to keep from this episode. Two agencies have run lotteries without publishing outcome comparisons. The novelty measure has been shown to differ from citations without being shown to change behaviour. The ARPA model has been described but, by its own students' account, not evaluated. The one experiment in the episode that did what it set out to do is Pier's, and it measured the instrument, not the outcome. The field has a strong critique of the existing system and weak evidence for every replacement, and the critique's best evidence applies to a narrow band of applications near the payline. Farther from the payline the system works better than the critics suggest, and Li and Agha's hundred and thirty thousand grants say so.

A funder that accepts all of that has a reasonable plan. Keep the expert screen, since it is the part with support. Stop asking panels to rank within the pool. Fill the pool by draw or by manager, tell applicants which, and keep the records, so that in ten years Barnett's trial can be read. Whether to prefer the draw or the manager depends on which risk a funder fears more, arbitrariness or unaccountable taste, and the evidence in this episode does not choose.

Chapter Eleven: For the Person Writing the Application

The use of this episode that comes closest to home is for an applicant and for a reviewer, and the two pull in somewhat different directions.

For an applicant, the Wisconsin model offers a specific and unglamorous finding. A good score did not follow from a long list of strengths; the count of strengths had no relationship to the score. The score tracked weaknesses, within each reviewer. In practice an application is graded by how many objections the reader can name. That suggests where marginal effort in a resubmission should go: toward removing every objection a reasonable reader could write down, and away from adding attractions. It rests on one small study of one pool, and I would hold it loosely, but it fits the Fang essay's complaint that grantsmanship is rewarded though never shown to track productivity.

A second implication is my own inference and concerns how many shots to take. If the top of the pool is close to a draw, then once an application is plainly in the pool, extra polish buys less than another application would. Gross and Bergstrom's model says the same about the whole community, where all that extra effort is what a lottery would remove. For one applicant the advice is uncomfortable, since each who follows it adds to the collective waste, which is why the problem is structural.

For a reviewer, the practical item comes from the Sattler study: read the scale as written. The NIH scale does not behave like classroom grades, where everything under sixty is a fail. A reviewer who grades like a teacher compresses the scale and loses information. The Pier result adds a reason to write the critique so that a second reader could reproduce the score from it, since without a stated mapping from weaknesses to numbers, the number cannot be compared with anyone else's.

The question to carry into the next stack of applications is this. Of the last ten proposals scored, could each one-point difference between neighbours be defended to a second reviewer who had read the same pages? It is a test of how much of a score is the application and how much is the reader.

For the record, here is how this episode was grounded. The Pier paper was read in full from a copy of the PNAS article, main text only, without the supporting appendix. The Fang and Casadevall essay was taken whole from the open-access text, along with Barnett's letter and the authors' reply. The eLife reanalysis was read through its results and the first part of its discussion, while Li and Agha are known only from their abstract, so every figure for that paper is the abstract's. The New Zealand applicant survey came through a tool that summarised it, and the numbers are as that summary reported them. Gross and Bergstrom were taken as abstract, introduction and discussion, without the mathematics. Heyard and colleagues were read for their introduction and the start of the method, with none of the Swiss results, and the account of the foundation's practice comes from search summaries.
