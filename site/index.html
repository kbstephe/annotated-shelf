The Annotated Shelf

Audio essays on organizations, power, and systems — from the Gervais Principle to the Unaccountability Machine.

  • Normal Accidents (Perrow): the light that lied at Three Mile Island

    Charles Perrow's 1984 book, built from the paper he wrote for the Kemeny Commission – the stuck valve and the indicator light that told operators it was shut, the matrix of interactive complexity and tight coupling that says which systems will eventually fail no matter who runs them, the marine collision that was never on a collision course, and the shuttle disaster Perrow himself refused to call a normal accident – against the high-reliability researchers who found a warship that doesn't crash, and Perrow's own later verdict on Fukushima.

  • The measurement constellation 2 of 2: targets and terror, the spike at eight minutes, and how to keep a number honest

    What happened when England ran its health service on targets with sanctions – Bevan and Hood's 2006 evidence: the ambulance response times that bunched just under eight minutes, the four-hour wait that fell from twenty-three percent to five, the nine hospitals that edited their waiting lists, the three kinds of gaming and the four kinds of people – then the design question the three papers never answered: how a programme that has to report numbers keeps them true, with paired indicators, random audit, denominators that count the people who never started, and a rule about what a number is allowed to cost the person who makes it.

  • The measurement constellation 1 of 2: Ridgway, Goodhart, Campbell, one law found three times

    Three short papers staged as a rediscovery – Ridgway's 1956 catalogue of what a single number does to an employment office and a Soviet factory, Goodhart's 1975 aside about the money supply that collapsed the moment it was targeted, and Campbell's 1976 law from a president's war on crime, Chicago's honest police chief, and the body count – with the argument that the law is not about measurement at all but about the moment a number acquires a consequence for the person who reports it.

  • Grove on medicine: the patient with a spreadsheet, the JAMA essay, and the physicians who wrote back

    Andy Grove's third appearance on the shelf, as patient, funder and critic – the 1996 Fortune cover on his prostate cancer, worked out from published outcome tables with a spreadsheet; the 2005 JAMA essay on why medicine's knowledge turns are so slow; the Parkinson's years, the Society for Neuroscience speech and the e-trial proposal; and the case against, from Derek Lowe's 'Andy Grove fallacy' to the confounding his own cancer analysis walked into, with a verdict on which of his complaints medicine has answered and which it has not.

  • Only the Paranoid Survive (Grove): inflection points, Cassandras, and the walk to the door

    Andy Grove's 1996 sequel to High Output Management, read as the manual under load – the six forces and the ten-X change, the memory exit as it actually happened below him, signal versus noise, letting chaos reign and then reining it in, the Pentium as the book's own counter-example, and the case against: survivorship, the company that stopped being paranoid, and what a doctrine of vigilance costs the people asked to live inside it.

  • How Will You Measure Your Life (Christensen): strategy, allocation, and the 98 percent rule

    Clayton Christensen's 2012 book as the fourth and final antidote – the ten minutes Andy Grove wouldn't give him, resource allocation as a life's real strategy, Herzberg's noble profession, the Sunday basketball game and the ninety-eight percent rule, jobs-to-be-done applied to a marriage, and the case against: Jill Lepore's assault on the parent theory and a book that never turns its own tools on itself.

  • High Output Management (Grove): leverage, one-on-ones, and what a manager produces

    Andy Grove's 1983 manual as the third antidote – the breakfast factory, the limiting step, leverage, the one-on-one, task-relevant maturity as the answer to the Peter Principle, paired indicators, and why the book reads like a factory floor because it came from one.

  • Governing the Commons (Ostrom): how communities run shared resources without the state

    Elinor Ostrom's 1990 book as the second antidote – Hardin's tragedy, the Swiss village that has grazed one alp since 1483, the Valencia water court, the fisheries that failed, the eight design principles, and what a hospital's shared things have in common with a pasture.

  • Exit, Voice, and Loyalty (Hirschman): what to do when your institution declines

    Albert Hirschman's 1970 book as the first of four antidotes to the shelf's cynical season – the Nigerian railway, the lazy monopoly, loyalty as a brake, the East German test that broke his own theory, and what a ward loses when its best nurses leave without a word.

  • Orwell, Shooting an Elephant: a man captured by his audience

    Orwell's 1936 Burma essay read as the cleanest specimen ever written of an actor captured by his own audience, paired with the earlier 'A Hanging' and set against the dispute over whether either one really happened.

  • Orwell, Politics and the English Language: the six rules and the linguists against them

    Orwell's 1946 essay read against the linguists who think it is nonsense – the six rules and their failures, the passive-voice count that embarrasses him, and the case that the mechanism is true even though the essay is bad.

  • Seeing Like a State (Scott) 2 of 2: collectivisation, ujamaa, metis, and the critics

    Part two of two on James C. Scott's book – Soviet collectivisation and Tanzanian villagisation as the four conditions met in full, mētis as the name for what they destroyed, the four rules Scott offers instead, and the critics who think he stacked the deck.

  • Seeing Like a State (Scott) 1 of 2: legibility, high modernism, planned cities

    Part one of two on James C. Scott's 1998 book – how a state makes the world readable to itself, why the reading is always a simplification, and what happened when the simplifications were built in timber, in tax rolls, and in concrete on a Brazilian plateau.

  • Bullshit Jobs (Graeber): five types, and the two surveys that disagree

    David Graeber's 2018 book on paid work that even the worker cannot justify – the five types, managerial feudalism, the polls that launched it, and the two surveys that later disagreed about whether any of it is real.

  • Orwell, Second Thoughts on Burnham: how to tell a forecaster from a fan

    Orwell's 1946 essay read as a method for telling a forecaster from a fan, with the twist that Orwell's own prediction at the end of it took forty-five years to come in.

  • The Utopia of Rules (Graeber): why deregulation makes more paperwork

    David Graeber's 2015 essay collection on bureaucracy, structural violence, and the death of the future – why deregulation always produces more paperwork, why rules feel like a relief to the people who don't have to interpret anyone, and what a Batman movie has to do with any of it.

  • The Peter Principle: promotion to incompetence, and the data that proved it

    Laurence Peter and Raymond Hull's 1969 satire on the last promotion everyone eventually gets – hierarchiology's mock science, the sales-floor data that later proved the joke true, and the one individual-level antidote the book actually offers.

  • The Tyranny of Structurelessness (Freeman): where power goes in leaderless groups

    Jo Freeman's nineteen seventy essay on where power goes when a group abolishes its offices – the friendship elite, the star system, the trashing that came for her afterward, and why the iron law arrives faster without a ballot.

  • The Machiavellians (Burnham) 2 of 2: Michels' iron law of oligarchy, and the verdict

    Part two of two on Burnham's elite theorists: Michels's iron law of oligarchy proven inside his own party, Burnham's case that liberty comes from fighting minorities, the school's compromised afterlife, and the verdict.

  • The Machiavellians (Burnham) 1 of 2: Machiavelli, Mosca, Pareto, Sorel

    Part one of two on James Burnham's 1943 rehabilitation of the elite theorists – Dante's pamphlet in disguise, Machiavelli's scandalous honesty, Mosca's ruling class, Pareto's reasons that come second, and Sorel's myth.

  • Parkinson's Law: why administration grows regardless of the work

    C. Northcote Parkinson's deadpan discovery that administration grows by its own laws – the Admiralty numbers, the two engines of multiplication, the bicycle shed, and why everyone laughed and no one acted.

  • The Organization Man (Whyte) 3 of 3: the corporate wife, the college, and Whyte's exit

    A third part on Whyte, for the material the first two compressed: the corporation's claim on the marriage, the retooling of the American college, what Friedan found at the same addresses, and how Whyte himself got out.

  • The Organization Man (Whyte) 2 of 3: Burnham's Managerial Revolution and Orwell's rebuttal

    Part two of three: James Burnham's wartime claim that managers were becoming the world's ruling class, Orwell's demolition of it, and the verdict on the pair of diagnoses the whole shelf descends from.

  • The Organization Man (Whyte) 1 of 3: the Social Ethic and personality tests

    Part one of three on William Whyte's postwar classic: the Social Ethic, the Park Forest fieldwork, the personality-test machinery, and the Fortune editor who taught America's middle managers to cheat.

  • The Unaccountability Machine (Davies) 3 of 3: repair, critics, and verdict

    The final part on Dan Davies's book: what repair would actually take, the critics given their full say, the synthesis with Rao and Jackall extended, and a verdict with a dose.

  • The Unaccountability Machine (Davies) 2 of 3: Stafford Beer's cybernetics and why economists get blamed

    Part two of three on Dan Davies's book: Stafford Beer's cybernetics rebuilt for the ear – variety, POSIWID, the five systems a viable organization needs, and why the economists get the blame.

  • The Unaccountability Machine (Davies) 1 of 3: the accountability sink

    The first of three parts on Dan Davies's book: the accountability sink walked slowly – squirrels at Schiphol, a conference desk, the Post Office, and the question of who is holding the override.

  • Dan Davies primer: Lying for Money, accountability sinks, and the Gervais Principle

    An analysis of Dan Davies's body of work — Lying for Money, The Unaccountability Machine, and the Back of Mind newsletter — synthesized with Rao's Gervais Principle: sinks as industrialized HIWTYL, the clueless middle as the sink's human interface, and Powertalk as the unattenuated channel.

  • Moral Mazes (Jackall): the rules managers actually live by

    Robert Jackall went into two American corporations the way an anthropologist goes up a river, and came back with the rules managers actually live by. The founding text of the cynical core.

  • Episode 0: pipeline test (skip)

    A sixty-second smoke test of the Annotated Shelf pipeline.
