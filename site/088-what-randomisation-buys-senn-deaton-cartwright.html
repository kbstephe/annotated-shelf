What Randomisation Buys (Senn v Deaton and Cartwright): dice, archers and the baseline table

A philosopher says the coin cannot balance indefinitely many unknown causes, two economists say the gold standard is a myth, and a trial statistician answers with two dice: what random allocation actually purchases, and why trial reports stopped testing whether the groups looked alike.

23 November 2026

Chapter One: A Column of P-Values

Open almost any trial report from the nineteen-nineties and turn to the first table. Two columns of numbers describe the people who were randomised, one column per arm: age, sex, blood pressure, how sick they were on the day they joined. And then a third column, at the right-hand edge, of p-values. Each one answers a question. Could the difference between the arms on this row have arisen by chance? Somewhere in a long table, one row usually comes back at 0.03, and a reviewer writes in the margin that the groups were not comparable.

The guidance on how to report a trial, CONSORT, says in its 2010 explanation that tests like these appeared in half of fifty trials published in leading general journals in 1997. Its verdict on them is mild in tone and devastating in content. Such tests, it says, are not necessarily wrong, just illogical. The reason is in the sentence before. The test asks whether a difference could have arisen by chance, and in a properly randomised trial we already know that every difference arose by chance. That is what randomised means.

So the column has been dropping out of the better journals. But its disappearance leaves a harder question standing. If the coin does not guarantee that the arms look alike, and everyone now concedes that it does not, then what did the coin buy? The medical profession has called the randomised trial its gold standard for half a century. What, exactly, is the gold?

This episode is a duel about that question, fought by people who agree on more than their titles suggest. On one side are a philosopher, John Worrall, and then an economist and a philosopher together, Angus Deaton and Nancy Cartwright, who say the trial has been sold on a property it does not have. On the other is Stephen Senn, a statistician who has spent his career designing and analysing clinical trials, and who thinks the critics are right about the property and wrong about almost everything they infer from it.

Chapter Two: The Claim Nobody Makes, in Print Everywhere

Start with the thing being attacked, because its defenders tend to deny it exists. Worrall's paper, What Evidence in Evidence-Based Medicine?, given at a philosophy of science symposium in 2002, lists the traditional reasons for randomising and goes after the one he thinks persuaded doctors. It is the claim that a random division controls for all variables, known and unknown, at once. He finds it said plainly. The philosopher Ron Giere wrote that randomised groups are automatically controlled for all other factors, even those no one suspects. Taken literally, Worrall says, this is trivially unsustainable. A fair coin can, by bad luck, give one arm most of the patients with some unmeasured trait that drives the outcome.

Sixteen years later Deaton and Cartwright collected the same claim from the places where it now lives. Deaton is the Princeton economist who won the Nobel prize in 2015 for work on consumption and poverty; Cartwright is a philosopher of science who has spent decades on how causal claims travel from one place to another. Their paper in Social Science and Medicine, Understanding and Misunderstanding Randomized Controlled Trials, is long and was published with a dozen or so commentaries. Its first section is a catalogue of overstatement. An impact-evaluation manual issued by the World Bank and the Inter-American Development Bank tells its readers that a randomised evaluation has eliminated all observed and unobserved factors that might otherwise explain the difference. A psychiatrist, Peter Kramer, writing for a general audience, says the randomising process will ensure, or make it highly probable, that a hidden genetic variant is spread equally between the arms, and that the result will be a fair test.

Their reply is a picture. In a later response to their critics they describe an archer whose arrows land two feet right of the target on half his shots and the same distance left on the rest. His arrows are unbiased. He never hits the target.

That is the distinction everyone in this argument eventually lands on. Randomisation makes the estimate right on average, where the average is over all the allocations the coin might have produced in this same group of patients. It says nothing, by itself, about how far this one allocation is from the truth. Fisher knew it. Deaton and Cartwright quote his remark, from 1926, that most experimenters carrying out a random assignment will be shocked to find how far from equally the plots distribute themselves.

The duel ought to begin at this point, and it does not. Senn agrees with every word of the archer. His 2013 paper, Seven Myths of Randomisation in Clinical Trials, begins by saying that critics of randomisation have betrayed an ignorance of how trials are run, and then concedes the property at once: balance is not what the coin provides. Deaton and Cartwright, for their part, cite Senn in the middle of their argument as a particularly clear statement of the point that imbalance does not compromise the inference. Each side treats the other as an ally on the central fact.

So the fight is somewhere else, and finding it takes the rest of the episode. It lies in three places: what hidden imbalance does to your standard error, what you should do about an imbalance you can see, and what randomisation buys if balance is not on the receipt.

Chapter Three: Two Dice

Senn's answer begins with a game. It has two fair dice, one red and one black, and two players, a statistician and a gambler. The red die is rolled first and the black second. Before the black die is thrown, the statistician must state the probability that the two dice will total ten, and the gambler may then bet either way at the odds implied. Letting the gambler choose the side keeps the statistician honest.

The game comes in three versions. In the first, both dice are rolled together, and the statistician sees neither in advance. There are thirty-six equally likely combinations and three of them make ten: four and six, five and five, six and four. The probability is three in thirty-six, one in twelve.

In the second version, the red die is rolled in full view, and only then does the statistician speak. A three or less makes ten impossible whatever happens next, so the correct forecast is zero. A four or more leaves exactly one face of the second die that completes the total, and the forecast becomes one in six. Before the throw, the statistician knows the two forecasts are equally likely, and their average is the same figure as before. But with a two showing on the table, quoting that average would be foolish; the gambler would take the bet against you every time.

In the third version, the red die is rolled under a cup. It has a score, but nobody knows it. Senn's claim is that this version is the first one again. The statistician should say one in twelve, and the gambler has no edge, even though everyone at the table knows that the true conditional probability, the one written under the cup, is either zero or one in six and certainly not the figure being quoted.

Now map it onto a trial. The red die is everything about a patient that predicts how she will do regardless of treatment: her severity, her genes, her diet, the things on the case-report form and the things nobody thought to record. The black die is the treatment. A measured covariate is the first die lying face up; an unmeasured one is still hidden.

Worrall's objection, in this language, is that the score under the cup is almost certainly not the average. Senn quotes him: there is no reason to think that any actual randomised trial reflects the limiting average. Senn agrees, and adds that this is no objection at all. There is every reason to believe the hidden red die is not showing three and a half, since no die ever does. It is sufficient, for stating the probability of a ten, to know that each of its six faces was equally likely.

That is what the analysis of a randomised trial does. It does not assume the hidden causes are balanced. It assumes they were allocated by a known random process, and it states its uncertainty accordingly. Senn goes further and turns the critics' premise around. The usual calculation is valid not only despite the fact that the unseen factors are unbalanced, but because we know they will be. If the groups were somehow known to be balanced on everything, the conventional analysis would be wrong, the way analysing a matched-pairs design as two independent groups is wrong.

Chapter Four: Indefinitely Many Causes

Worrall's strongest argument has not been answered yet, so take it in its best form. Concede that for any one unknown factor, a random split is unlikely to be badly skewed. But the unknown factors are not one. They are, as he puts it, indefinitely many. Even if each has only a small chance of being unbalanced, the chance that at least one of them is badly unbalanced might, for all we know, be high. Those who say randomisation tends to balance all factors, he writes, commit a simple quantificational fallacy. Deaton and Cartwright give the argument its modern illustration. The epidemiologist Jan Vandenbroucke pointed out that the human genome has about three billion base pairs. If only one of them matters for the outcome, and that one happens to be unbalanced, a single trial can be far from the truth.

Stated that way it sounds unanswerable, and the claim it refutes is a real one, since textbooks and manuals do say the coin tends to balance everything. But Senn says the argument aims at the wrong target. A trial's error estimate is not built by counting covariates. It is built by comparing two kinds of variation. Patients in the same arm, given the same treatment, finish in different places, and that spread within an arm is something you can see and measure. Every one of the three billion base pairs, and every unrecorded habit, contributes to it. The analysis asks how large a difference between arms that within-arm spread would produce by chance, if the treatments did nothing. So the hidden causes are already in the calculation, not as a list but through their footprint on the outcome. For Worrall's infinity to break the inference, Senn writes, it has to be shown to affect the difference between groups more than the variation within them, and a random allocation is what keeps the two in a known relation.

The indefinitely many covariates matter only through one quantity, the outcome, and the outcome is measured. Their relevance is bounded by it.

Senn's worked example is a real trial, small and old, described by Hills and Armitage: twenty-nine patients with bedwetting, each treated for two separate fortnights, once with a drug and once with a placebo, in random order, with the number of dry nights counted each time. Because each patient is compared with themselves, an enormous list of factors, from family habits to bladder size, is held fixed by design. On average the drug gave 2.172 more dry nights. Analysed as the paired design it was, the one-sided p-value is 0.00074 and the confidence interval runs from 0.91 to 3.43 nights. A permutation test, which shuffles which fortnight got the drug, gives essentially the same answer, and so does a Bayesian analysis with a vague prior.

Now analyse the same numbers wrongly, as if they came from two separate groups of children. The point estimate does not move; it is still 2.172. The p-value becomes 0.0141, nearly twenty times larger, and the interval widens to run from 0.24 to 4.10. Senn uses the wrong analysis to show what the right parallel-group trial would look like. Had these been two different sets of patients, everything the pairing held fixed could have differed between the arms. The analysis allows for that, because patients within each arm differ from one another by just as much, and the interval widens to match.

His analogy is engineering. An engineer's calculation is not useless because it cannot list every flaw in a steel beam; the tolerance factor is the allowance for the flaws nobody listed. A confidence interval from a randomised trial is wide for the same reason. Critics who read the interval's width as a failure of randomisation have mistaken the allowance for the damage.

Chapter Five: The Unhappy Draw

That disposes of the hidden die. The harder case is the one lying face up: an imbalance you can see, after the coin has been tossed. This is the problem the last episode left open, and it is where the two camps part in earnest.

Worrall's 2007 sequel, which Senn quotes, puts the standard view as he understood it. Everyone agrees, he writes, that if there is a clear baseline imbalance one should not draw any conclusion from the trial, and the classical statistician then insists, rather quixotically, on re-randomising until the division looks fair. Deaton and Cartwright recommend something close. If the allocation produces an obvious imbalance on known covariates, they say, try again, and keep trying until the arms are close enough on a measure of distance between their means, with the analysis then adjusted to respect the repeated draws, a method set out by Kari Morgan and Donald Rubin in 2012. They add that Fisher himself would have done well to abandon a random layout that happened to put the treated plots in a visible pattern across the field. Thomas Cook, one of their commentators, calls such draws unhappy randomisations.

Senn's reply begins with what he calls the first myth, that patients in a clinical trial are treated simultaneously. They are not. They present when they fall ill, often over years, and some patients finish the trial before others have joined it. There is no moment at which the whole list exists and can be shuffled again. A drug trial cannot be re-randomised; the first patient has already had her first dose by the time the hundredth falls ill. Nor, for the same reason, can a panel of doctors match the whole cohort in pairs before the coin is tossed, which is what both Worrall and an earlier Bayesian critic, Peter Urbach, had proposed.

And then he says the imbalance is not a problem anyway. His example is an asthma trial in which some patients are already on steroids and some are not, and the coin has left more steroid users in one arm. Split the trial in two, steroid users and the rest. Within each part the comparison is still randomised and still valid; it just has unequal numbers. Compare the arms within each stratum, combine the two comparisons with suitable weights, and the imbalance is gone from the estimate. The technique is called post-stratification, and analysis of covariance does the same job for a continuous measure. So Worrall is quite wrong, Senn writes, that everyone agrees a trial with a clear baseline imbalance cannot be interpreted. Valid inference does not need the arms to match at baseline.

In the same paragraph where Senn says patients are never treated together, he notes the exceptions. Phase one studies draw on banks of healthy volunteers. And in most cluster randomised trials, the clusters are identified before treatment begins.

That exception is where Deaton works. A development economist randomising villages, schools or clinics has the full list on the first day. Re-randomising until the arms look alike is perfectly possible there, it is cheap, and with twenty or forty units the chance of an unhappy draw is real. A medical statistician designing a drug trial has a trickle of patients and a sealed sequence of envelopes. Each side has generalised from its home trial. Deaton and Cartwright's advice is good for the trial they know, and Senn's objection is good for the trial he knows, and neither paper, as far as I can find, notices that the other is describing a different machine.

Chapter Six: Why the Column Died

Return now to the column of p-values, because both camps agree it should go, and the reason sharpens what each thinks the coin buys.

Deaton and Cartwright quote the statistician Colin Begg, writing in 1990, that a baseline significance test examines a null hypothesis already known to be true, so any significant result is a false positive by definition. Senn made the same argument at length in the mid-nineteen-nineties, in a paper I know from memory rather than from a copy. Every difference between properly randomised arms arose by chance, so asking whether it could have done tests nothing about the patients.

But Senn's real objection is subtler, and it comes from the version of the dice game in which the red die lies face up. Once you have seen a covariate, the question is not whether its imbalance is improbable. The question is how much the imbalance moves the outcome, and that depends on two things the p-value cannot see: the size of the imbalance and how strongly the covariate predicts the outcome. CONSORT's explanation says the same, that comparisons at baseline should rest on the prognostic strength of the variables and the size of any chance imbalances, not on a test.

Take an invented trial to see the gap, with round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic visible. A hundred patients per arm. Baseline severity is strongly related to the outcome; suppose the correlation is 0.7. The coin leaves the treated group 0.2 standard deviations less severe at the start. With a hundred per arm, that gap is a little under one and a half standard errors, so the baseline test returns a p-value of about 0.16, and the row passes unremarked. Yet with a correlation of 0.7, the gap alone shifts the expected outcome by about 0.14 of a standard deviation in the treated arm's favour. If the trial hoped to detect an effect of three tenths, that is half of it. Two rows down, eye colour comes back at 0.01 and draws the reviewer's ink. It predicts nothing and moves nothing.

The test is loudest on the rows that do not matter and silent on the one that does. The last of Senn's myths, that large trials are better balanced, adds a twist about size. In proportional terms, he agrees, the belief is true. But large trials also have smaller standard errors, so smaller baseline differences become important, and the gain in balance is entirely consumed by the gain in precision. A large trial is no safer from its unhappy draw than a small one; it simply measures its own exposure more finely. Deaton and Cartwright reach the same place from the other side. Sample size matters, they write in their reply, but saying so is close to useless without knowing how large is large enough, and that depends on the causal structure in front of you.

So what should a trialist do with a covariate seen to be prognostic? Senn's answer is that it belongs in the model, chosen in advance, whether or not it happened to be balanced. To ignore an observed prognostic covariate, he writes, is to treat the face-up die as if it were still hidden. Having measured something because you thought it predicted the outcome, you cannot then analyse as if you had never looked. He counts this, that an observed covariate may be ignored because the trial was randomised, among the myths some statisticians actually believe. He also admits the limit. Add enough covariates and the precision of a conventional analysis falls, so the pragmatic answer, a short list agreed before unblinding, is a compromise he thinks reasonable and cannot fully justify. How to choose and use that list is next episode's question, with Lord's dining hall and Freedman's warnings about regression.

There is one place where a baseline comparison of the old kind justifies its column, and Lanarkshire is it. In the last episode's milk experiment, the teachers rebalanced the lists by eye after the lottery, and when the children were first measured the controls were taller and heavier, by about three months' growth in weight and four in height. A test of baseline difference there is not a test of chance. It is a test of the allocator. Deaton and Cartwright say exactly this: baseline tests are appropriate if you suspect the random number generator failed or that someone subverted the allocation. The column measures the hand on the coin, and that is the only thing in the table it can measure.

Chapter Seven: What the Coin Buys

If not balance, then what? By now the answer has been half assembled, and it comes in two parts.

The first part is an honest error. Deaton and Cartwright write that Fisher's insight was never that randomisation balanced the arms, but that it provides the basis for calculating the size of the error. Senn would sign that sentence and did, in effect, with the dice. The hidden red die is allowed for because its distribution in probability is known, and it is known because a coin, not a person, put each patient in an arm. Fisher, in a 1938 letter to Harold Jeffreys that Senn quotes, put it in the language of field trials: what must be randomised is whatever contributes to the experiment's actual error, so that the error can be estimated, and anything removed from the error by design must be removed from its estimate too. Block on what you know, randomise within, and let the analysis match the design. That is the rule from the Lanarkshire episode, arriving from the opposite direction.

The second part is the one I think the duel finally turns up, and it is about people rather than plots. Senn's conclusion is that randomisation is valuable but not essential, and that what it does is reduce the extent to which we have to model the brains of others as part of our model of the world. Every other allocation scheme, he argues, makes the allocator part of the experiment. His illustration comes from Fisher again, in a letter about telepathy. Ask thousands of radio listeners to guess a card you have chosen, say the Queen of Diamonds, and you will find more than one in fifty-two guess right. Telepathy has nothing to do with it; people simply guess red cards more often than black. A haphazard choice is never really random; it carries the chooser's habits, and the analysis then has to model those habits or be fooled by them. Lindley, a Bayesian, had written that a haphazard arrangement would do as well as a random one, and Senn's reply is to ask what, exactly, such an arrangement consists of.

Worrall, to his credit, concedes most of this second part. Of the four arguments for randomising he examines, he calls one cast-iron: that a clinician who chooses which arm a patient joins will, out of concern for the patient, steer the frail away from the risky new drug, or toward it, and so bias the comparison. Lanarkshire is his argument with a teacher in place of a clinician. But he adds that randomisation is only a means here, and that blinding the allocator is what really protects the comparison. Senn's answer is another of his myths, that blinding can be had without randomisation: it cannot, because only a random sequence is as hard as possible to guess. A sequence anyone could predict, such as alternation, cannot stay concealed for long.

Deaton and Cartwright arrive at the same second purpose through economics, and this is the passage where the critics come nearest to the defender. They report a theorem by Abhijit Banerjee and colleagues that, for a single Bayesian investigator with trusted beliefs, the best design is never random: she should place treatments where they will tell her the most. The statistician Jimmie Savage had said much the same in 1962. But Savage also noted that different investigators hold different priors, and that individual priors are vulnerable to vagueness and the temptation to self-deception. Banerjee's group then model an investigator facing an adversarial audience, a regulator or a referee, who will reject any result that leans on the investigator's own beliefs. For that audience, they recommend randomising.

What the coin buys, then, on my reading of both camps, is not balance. It buys an allocation whose probability model is known to everyone, including people who distrust you, and from that one fact come both of its gifts: an error estimate that already allows for causes nobody measured, and a comparison in which nobody's judgement about who should get the treatment could have entered. The no-randomisation theorem is right for a scientist alone with her beliefs. Trials are almost never run for an audience of one.

Chapter Eight: Where the Critics Hit

None of that answers the bulk of Deaton and Cartwright's paper, and it would be unfair to leave it there. Their main targets are not balance. They are what happens after allocation, what happens inside the standard error, and what happens when the result is carried somewhere else.

Take the period after allocation first. Randomisation gives the arms a common origin, but unbiasedness needs more: that nothing after allocation became correlated with treatment. Patients who drop out, who refuse the arm they were given, who are seen by different staff at different times, or who learn their allocation from side effects, can all undo it. They mention the Women's Health Initiative hormone trial, where blinding broke for nearly half the women on hormones and only a small share of those on placebo. Their reply to the commentators presses the point: what unbiasedness needs is not randomisation but orthogonality, and that has to be argued case by case. A medical statistician will say that this is why trials are blinded, concealed and analysed by assigned arm. The economist's answer is that many trials in the social sciences cannot be blinded at all.

Inside the standard error is the more surprising charge, and it lands exactly where Senn said an attack would have to. He wrote that to defeat randomisation one must attack the validity of the probability statement, and that this is quite hard. Deaton and Cartwright attack it. The usual t-test assumes, roughly, that treatment effects are not wildly skewed. Suppose they are: a microcredit trial where most borrowers gain nothing and a rare one builds a business, or a health-insurance trial where one patient spends a fortune. Then the result depends on which arm the outlier lands in. In their simulation, with true average effect zero, standard tests rejected the null fourteen percent of the time with twenty-five per arm, where five percent was promised. When the outlier falls in the control arm, the spread looks small and the treatment looks significant, and the within-arm variation, Senn's measure of the hidden causes, is the very thing that has been fooled. They cite Alwyn Young's reanalysis of fifty-three randomised studies in the American Economic Association's journals: for thirty to forty percent of the effects reported as significant, randomisation inference on the authors' own data could not reject the hypothesis of no effect at all. That is a figure for economics, not medicine, but it is a real hole in the comfort the dice provide.

Carrying the result elsewhere is the second half of their paper and the part most medical readers skip. Their phrase for the usual move is that a result established for one population is simply asserted for another with the same words, as if the estimate carried its validity with it. A trial's population is selected, they note, by the willingness to be in a trial; it is an irony that only randomised trials are said to handle selection, when they are almost always run on selected people. The average effect in a trial is the average over that population's mix of reasons for responding, and a new population with a different mix will have a different average. Senn does not disagree. Near the end of the myths paper he concedes that trials alone will never answer every question we need answered.

Chapter Nine: Against Both Sides

Trouble for Deaton and Cartwright begins with their own reception. Senn's charge against Worrall, that critics of randomisation do not know how trials are run, applies at least in part to them: re-randomisation is advice for a trial with a list, offered as if it were advice for trials in general. And their prose seems to invite the very misreadings it complains of. In the reply, they note that one commentator, the economist Kaushik Basu, had summarised their first section as saying that, for average treatment effects, randomised trials cannot be bettered. They call this close to the exact opposite of what they wrote. When a careful reader can come away with the opposite of a paper's argument, some of the fault lies in the paper. A long section on the archer and the unbiased arrows, followed by a short concession that the coin makes an honest error possible, leaves many readers with the archer and not the concession.

They also underweight their own best citation. The adversarial-audience model is in their paper, and they treat it as a qualification. Read with Senn's line about the brains of others, it is closer to the main reason trials exist. Their preferred alternative, good control by people who know enough, is excellent science in a laboratory and a poor basis for a drug licence, because the regulator cannot see what the investigator knows and has every reason to suspect what she wants. Deaton and Cartwright write that if we know enough to control, we should; the regulator's question is who decides when that threshold has been reached.

And against Senn, the case is narrower but real. His dice assume the red die is fair and the black die is thrown as described. In a drug trial with concealed allocation and blinded assessment, that is close enough. In an unblinded social programme with attrition of a third, it is not, and his paper says little about trials of that kind. The skewed-effects problem is also not a myth he lists, and what it corrupts is his own comparison of the gap between arms with the spread within them. He would say, fairly, that these are problems for every design, and that a non-randomised study inherits all of them and adds its own. Deaton and Cartwright would say that is the hierarchy argument again, and that it needs showing, case by case, not assuming.

Chapter Ten: Verdict

For the clinical trialist, Senn wins the duel he chose. The coin does not balance the arms, and it was never supposed to. It makes the uncertainty statement honest for causes nobody measured, it takes the allocator's judgement out of the comparison, and it leaves you free, indeed obliged, to adjust for the causes you did measure. Worrall's indefinitely many factors are answered by the within-arm spread, and his re-randomisation is a remedy for trials that do not exist in medicine. Deaton and Cartwright win a different duel, about what follows: that unbiased is a thin compliment, that the standard error can be fooled by skewed effects, and that a trial's answer belongs to its own population until someone argues otherwise.

Read Senn's Seven Myths first, about twelve pages; the dice game and the bedwetting trial are the core, and the section headed Some Technical Matters is where he admits what he has not solved. Then read the first section of Deaton and Cartwright, about two-fifths of a long paper, for the archer, the manual quotations and the skewed-effects simulation, and their short reply to the commentators, which is livelier than the paper. Worrall's 2002 paper is short and clear; its section on why we randomise is the steelman to test yourself against.

Suppose a reviewer's report arrives on a trial of a hundred patients per arm. The groups, the reviewer writes, differed in baseline severity, p equals 0.04, and the conclusions are therefore unsafe. The answer the dice support runs like this. Baseline severity was named in the analysis plan as a covariate because it predicts the outcome, and the primary analysis adjusted for it; the adjusted and unadjusted estimates are both reported, and here is how far apart they are. The p-value on that row was never going to tell anyone whether the groups could be compared; the size of the imbalance and the covariate's correlation with outcome tell that, and both are in the table. And the one baseline question that would make the reviewer right, whether anyone could have known the next allocation before a patient was enrolled, is answered in the section on concealment. If that last answer is weak, the reviewer is right for a reason the reviewer did not give.
