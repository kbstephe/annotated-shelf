The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, Part One (Thomas Kuhn): normal science, the puzzle with a guaranteed answer, and what a study section does when it funds only the paradigm

Thomas Kuhn's 1962 essay read for what it says about ordinary research: how a field acquires its first shared achievement, the three kinds of mop-up work, why the problems are puzzles, why textbooks rewrite history, and Popper's charge that the normal scientist is simply badly taught.

06 October 2026

Chapter One: A Physics Student Reads Aristotle

A graduate student in theoretical physics, within sight of his dissertation, is drafted in nineteen forty-seven to help with an experimental course for non-scientists. James Conant, the president of Harvard, is running it, and the student's job is to prepare a case history: Galileo on falling bodies and the pendulum. To do that he reads the old physics that Galileo was arguing against. The story he told for the rest of his life is that he read Aristotle on motion, found it full of mistakes that no competent person could have made, and then, one summer day, stopped reading it as bad Newton. Read on its own terms, with its own categories, it made sense. I have that story from Kuhn's later recollections and from standard biographies, not from the book, so treat the date and the scene as his account of himself.

What the book itself says, in its preface, is plainer and sounds like a complaint. The exposure to out-of-date science radically undermined his conceptions about the nature of science and about the reasons for its special success. He had drawn those conceptions partly from his scientific training and partly from a long-standing hobby in the philosophy of science, and they did not fit what the historical record displayed. He left physics. He spent three years as a Junior Fellow at Harvard reading Alexandre Koyré, Piaget on how children's worlds change, the Gestalt psychologists, Whorf on language, and Quine. He gave eight public lectures in Boston in March of nineteen fifty-one. Then came a decade of teaching history of science to students, which he credits with teaching him how to say what he meant. The final push was a year in nineteen fifty-eight and fifty-nine at a centre for behavioural scientists in California, where he found himself among social scientists who argued openly about what counted as a legitimate problem and a legitimate method. Physicists and chemists, whom he knew well, did not seem to argue that way, and he wanted to know why. The preface is dated February nineteen sixty-two, from Berkeley, and the book came out as a small monograph in a series called the International Encyclopedia of Unified Science. It was written for an encyclopedia of the very philosophy it set out to damage.

This is the first of three episodes on that book. The second covers the part that gets quoted at parties, anomaly and crisis and revolution. The third covers the fight over what the book meant. This one takes the half almost nobody quotes: what a science is doing for the other ninety-odd percent of its time. The question it answers is an old one with a practical edge. If most research is mop-up work inside a framework nobody is questioning, what makes that work productive, what does it cost, and what happens to a funding system that mostly rewards it?

Here is the shape of the argument, so that the pieces have somewhere to land. A field becomes a science when one achievement attracts most of its practitioners and leaves them problems to solve. From then on the work is puzzle-solving, which means problems selected because a solution is guaranteed to exist, attacked under rules the community does not question. The training that produces puzzle-solvers is done through worked examples, not through rules. And the textbooks that carry the examples erase, as a matter of function, the history of how the field got to its framework. That last claim is the one with the longest reach, and we will arrive at it with a few of Kuhn's own examples already in hand.

Chapter Two: The Electricians

Start where Kuhn starts, with what a field looks like before it has this arrangement. His example is electricity in the first half of the eighteenth century. The people who worked on it called themselves electricians, a word he enjoys noting is their own, and the list of names is long: Hauksbee, Gray, Desaguliers, Du Fay, Nollet, Watson, Franklin. They read each other's books. They did real experiments, many of them excellent. Yet their theories had, in his phrase, no more than a family resemblance.

One group took attraction and friction as the basic phenomena and treated repulsion as a mechanical rebound, and they put off studying the newly discovered effect of conduction for as long as they could. A second group counted attraction and repulsion as equally basic. A third spoke of electricity as a fluid that ran through conductors, where the first group had imagined an effluvium given off by non-conductors. Each group could handle some effects well, and each had trouble reconciling its theory with the effects the others had chosen as their showpieces. Each also felt obliged to start from the foundations, because there was nobody whose foundations it could borrow. The books of the period, Kuhn says, were addressed as much to the other schools as to nature.

Now follow the fluid group, because their story is the one Kuhn runs to its end. Believing electricity a fluid, several of them had an idea that would not have occurred to a person exploring nature casually: bottle it. The result, independently in at least two places in the early seventeen-forties, was the Leyden jar. Benjamin Franklin took that strange new device as his central puzzle, and his success in explaining it did more than anything else to make his theory the one that attracted the next generation. Kuhn is careful about what that success did and did not amount to. Franklin's theory could not account for the mutual repulsion of two negatively charged bodies. It was accepted anyway. To be a paradigm, a theory must seem better than its competitors, he writes, but it need not explain, and in fact never does explain, all the facts it can be confronted with.

Hold that sentence beside what happened to the losers. Nollet, the most influential electrician on the Continent at mid-century, lived to see himself, in Franklin's report of it, the last of his sect except for one former pupil. The other schools did not lose a vote and then politely disband. Most of their members converted, a few clung on and were read out of the profession, and the work of the stragglers was simply no longer cited. When a debate did survive, over whether electricity was one fluid or two, the electricians concluded that no experiment could tell the versions apart, decided they were therefore equivalent, and went back to work.

What came after is where Kuhn's argument has teeth. Between about seventeen-forty and seventeen-eighty, he says, electricians were for the first time able to take the foundations of their field for granted. Few new kinds of phenomena were found in those decades. What changed was the kind of work. Charge detectors became far more sensitive. Techniques for measuring charge became reliable and widely shared. The concept of capacity was invented and tied to a newly refined notion of electric tension. Electrostatic force was quantified. And the reports changed their form. Franklin had written a book addressed to anyone who might be interested. Cavendish, Coulomb and Volta wrote in a mode that only other electricians could follow. Kuhn adds a remark about publication that every working scientist will recognize: today, he writes, the scientist who writes a book is more likely to damage than to enhance a professional reputation. The book as a form of research communication survives, he says, only where the field is still loosely drawn.

He also offers a criterion, and offers it with the sort of caution that is easy to skip. Except with the advantage of hindsight, he writes, it is hard to find a better mark of a science than a paradigm able to guide the whole group's research. By paradigm he means, at this stage of the book, a past achievement that has two properties. It is unprecedented enough to pull an enduring group of adherents away from competing modes of practice, and it is open-ended enough to leave all sorts of problems for them to resolve. The achievement has to be a model, something with law, theory, application and instrumentation together, from which a research tradition springs. Ptolemy's computations, Newton's Principia and Opticks, Lavoisier's use of the balance, and Franklin's Electricity are on his list.

The criterion leaves something out. It says nothing about truth. Franklin's picture of a single electrical fluid is not what a physics textbook teaches now, The criterion measures whether a community can stop arguing about foundations and start measuring things. Whether the foundations are right is a separate question, and the book's second half is about how a field finds out it was wrong.

Chapter Three: The Mop-Up

What does a field do once it has stopped arguing? Kuhn's third chapter answers with a classification he says exhausts the literature of normal science, and I will give it in his form, with his examples, because the examples do the persuading.

The first class is the determination of facts that the paradigm has shown to be revealing. Stellar positions and magnitudes, specific gravities, wavelengths, boiling points, combining weights. Big machines get built for this, and reputations get made. From Tycho Brahe to Ernest Lawrence, he notes, some scientists have become famous not for any novelty in what they found but for the precision, reliability and scope of the methods they devised for redetermining something already known.

The second class is the facts that can be compared directly with the paradigm's predictions, often facts of little interest in themselves. Here his examples are devices: special telescopes built to find the annual parallax that the Copernican picture predicted, a machine invented almost a century after the Principia to give the first clean demonstration of Newton's second law, an apparatus in eighteen-fifty that showed light moves faster in air than in water, and the huge scintillation counter that detected the neutrino. Each is an enormous feat of ingenuity whose output is a number nobody would have been surprised by. Without the Principia, he says of one of them, the measurements would have meant nothing at all.

The third class, which Kuhn ranks above the other two, is the articulation of the paradigm itself. It has three parts. There are physical constants: Newton's theory implied a universal gravitational constant but did not need its value, and nobody found a way to measure it for a century. Cavendish did, in the seventeen-nineties, and was not the last. Kuhn's footnote cites a review of some two dozen measurements between seventeen forty-one and nineteen-oh-one. There are quantitative laws, such as Boyle's, Coulomb's and Joule's. And there are experiments that choose among ways of applying the paradigm to a new area.

The second of those needs a run to its end, because it carries the book's first surprise. People assume that quantitative laws are found by taking measurements and looking for regularities. Kuhn says history gives no support to so Baconian a method. Boyle's experiments on air were not conceivable, he says, until air was seen as an elastic fluid to which the apparatus of hydrostatics could be applied. And Coulomb's inverse-square law is his best case. Coulomb built a special instrument to measure the force between small charges. People had measured electrical forces before, using ordinary pan balances, and had found no consistent or simple regularity. The difference was not that Coulomb had better technique. His design depended on a prior idea, that every particle of the electric fluid acts on every other at a distance, so that the force between particles was the one thing it was safe to assume would be a simple function of distance. With that idea, the apparatus could be built to isolate the force the idea predicted. The result, Kuhn adds, surprised no one, and several of Coulomb's contemporaries had predicted it in advance.

The old pan-balance measurements are easy to overlook. Their results were neither consistent nor simple, so they could not be used to articulate any paradigm, and in his words they remained mere facts, unrelated and unrelatable to the progress of electrical research. Only in retrospect, with a later paradigm in hand, can anyone see what they were measuring. Think about that as a statement about what counts as data. A set of careful measurements, honestly made and honestly reported, was not wrong. It was unusable, because nothing existed to tell the community what the numbers were numbers of.

Theory has its own mop-up, and Newton's successors show that a great theory does not finish the job. The Principia applied its laws to a few cases, without great precision, and was built for the heavens. Newton had treated a pendulum bob as a point, ignored air resistance, and derived Kepler's laws by neglecting the planets' pull on one another, which means his theory told astronomers to expect that the planets would not quite obey Kepler's laws, and they did not. Euler, Lagrange, Laplace and Gauss spent some of their best years on perturbed orbits, vibrating strings and fluids. Kuhn calls it probably the most brilliant and consuming science of the Enlightenment, and its aim was to improve the match between Newton's paradigm and the sky.

There is a specific episode he gives as a case of the rules holding. For decades, those trying to derive the observed motion of the moon from Newton's laws kept failing. Some suggested that the inverse-square law deviated from the truth at short distances. Kuhn says that to adopt that suggestion would have been to change the paradigm, to define a new puzzle and not to solve the old one. The community kept the rules until, in seventeen-fifty, one of its members found how they could be applied after all. He gives no name. As I recall the history from outside the book, the member was the French mathematician Clairaut, who found that carrying a series calculation to higher terms closed most of the gap, but that detail is mine and not Kuhn's. What matters for Kuhn's argument is the bet the community placed. It bet that the failure was a failure of the calculator and not of the law, and for the moon that bet paid.

Summing up the three classes, he says, the overwhelming majority of the problems undertaken by even the very best scientists fall into them. Work under the paradigm can be done in no other way. And to desert the paradigm is to stop practicing the science it defines.

Chapter Four: A Puzzle with an Answer

If most of a career is spent on predicted results, why would anyone bother? Kuhn's answer is the central metaphor of the essay, and it is more careful than the word suggests.

The aim of normal science, he says, is not novelty. A project whose outcome falls outside the narrow range the paradigm anticipates is usually just a research failure, one that reflects not on nature but on the scientist. That explains a feature of scientific culture that outsiders find odd: the people who compute ephemerides or take another measurement with an existing instrument are often doing work as valuable, in terms of information, as the work that scientists compete for, and yet they regard it with a certain contempt, because it repeats procedures already completed. What attracts them to the other kind is not that the outcome is unknown. The outcome is known, often in detail so great that what remains is uninteresting in itself. What is unknown is the route. Bringing a normal research problem to a conclusion, he says, is achieving the anticipated in a new way, which requires solving a series of instrumental, conceptual and mathematical puzzles.

Then he defines his term with unusual care, and the definition has two clauses that are easy to blur. A puzzle is a problem that can test ingenuity or skill in solution. Whether its outcome is intrinsically interesting is irrelevant to whether it is a good puzzle. The assured existence of a solution is relevant. His illustration is the jigsaw. Take two boxes, pull the pieces at random from each, and mix them. Whether that problem has a solution is doubtful, and for that reason it is not a puzzle in any usual sense, because it cannot test anyone's skill. The really pressing problems, he writes, such as a cure for cancer or the design of a lasting peace, are often not puzzles at all, largely because they may not have a solution.

I would ask you to hold on to that sentence in a medical context, and I will return to it at the end of the episode. For now follow the second clause. A puzzle must also have rules limiting the acceptable solutions and the steps by which they are reached. A jigsaw is not solved by making a picture; a child or a contemporary artist could scatter selected pieces on a ground and produce a better, more original picture. It would not be a solution, because all the pieces must be used, plain sides down, interlocked without forcing. Normal science has rules in the same sense. A man who builds an instrument to measure optical wavelengths cannot be content with a device that attaches numbers to spectral lines. He has to show, by analysing the apparatus in terms of established optical theory, that the numbers are the ones that enter theory as wavelengths. Kuhn's illustration is the pattern of electron scattering maxima, first observed and recorded with no apparent significance, and relevant to nothing until a theory predicting wave behaviour of matter in motion told the observers what they were looking at. Until that condition was satisfied, he says, no problem had been solved.

So he asks what the rules of the game are, and finds four layers. The first are explicit laws and concepts: Newton's laws made quantity of matter a fundamental category and forces between bits of matter a dominant topic of research. The second are commitments about instruments and how they may be used, which can persist for centuries, as with the long argument over whether fire was a legitimate tool of chemical analysis, or the resistance Helmholtz met from physiologists who doubted that physical experiment could illuminate their field. The third are quasi-metaphysical commitments. After about sixteen-thirty, most physical scientists took the universe to be corpuscles in motion, and that told them what entities existed, what an explanation must look like, and, importantly, what the research problems should be. A chemist who accepted it, like Boyle, paid special attention to reactions that looked like transmutations, because those showed corpuscular rearrangement most clearly. And the fourth are the high-level commitments without which nobody is a scientist at all: to understand the world, and to pursue pockets of apparent disorder until they yield.

Now the steelman, a fair statement of the essay before any criticism. Kuhn's claim is that the restriction is the productive thing. By confining attention to a small range of esoteric problems, the paradigm forces scientists to investigate nature in a detail and depth otherwise unimaginable. The community will have solved problems its members could scarcely have imagined, and would never have undertaken without the commitment. At least part of the achievement is permanent. I find this the strongest part of the book because it resolves something that otherwise looks like a paradox. A community that took nothing for granted could not afford the precision instrument, the decades-long constant-measuring program, or the mathematics of perturbation theory, because each of those is an investment that pays off only if the framework holds. The Leyden jar is a case: an object that would not have been invented by someone exploring at random.

And the price is in the same passage. A paradigm can insulate the community from socially important problems that cannot be stated in the form the paradigm's tools allow. Kuhn names the seventeenth-century Baconians as a case, and some of the contemporary social sciences. He also explains why normal science seems to progress so rapidly: its practitioners have chosen problems that, in his phrase, only their own lack of ingenuity should keep them from solving. The word chosen is doing real work there. A field that picks its problems because a solution is assured will, other things equal, report solutions at a satisfying rate. The rate tells you about the selection as much as the field.

Chapter Five: Learning by Example

Kuhn now makes the move that his critics found hardest and that I think holds up best. If normal science runs on rules, a historian ought to be able to list the rules. He tried, and could not. The exercise, he reports, is a source of continual and deep frustration. Whatever generalizations you write down about what the community shares, some members would reject them as stated, even while those same members agree completely about which past achievements are the models.

His explanation borrows from Wittgenstein, and he is candid that part of the argument goes beyond what Wittgenstein said. The philosopher asked what you need to know in order to use words like chair, leaf or game without argument, and concluded that there need not be a set of properties common to all games and only games. We apply the word to a new case because it resembles, closely enough, cases we learned to call by that name. Kuhn proposes that research problems within a tradition relate in the same way, by resemblance and by modeling on a corpus of accepted achievements. Scientists need no full set of rules because they have the models. He gives Polanyi credit for a similar idea about knowledge acquired through practice that cannot be fully written down.

Then the educational argument, which is where an academic will feel the book's weight. Scientists never learn concepts, laws and theories in the abstract. They meet them in a package that includes applications, and the applications are not embroidery. A student of dynamics finds out what force, mass, space and time mean less from the textbook's definitions than from watching and doing problem after problem. The process of learning by doing continues through the doctoral dissertation and beyond. The problems get more complex and less precedented, but remain modeled on earlier achievements. If scientists have abstracted rules from this, Kuhn says, there is little reason to believe it; asked to characterize the established bases of their field, they are little better than laypeople. They show what they know by doing successful research.

The postscript he added in nineteen sixty-nine gives the best illustration of what he means, and it is a chain of three problems. Galileo found that a ball rolling down one incline rises, on a second incline of any slope, to the same height from which it started, and he learned to see that situation as a pendulum with a point mass for a bob. Huygens then solved the problem of how a physical pendulum, with its extended body, swings, by imagining the body as a set of Galilean point-pendulums whose bonds could be released at any instant. When the bonds are released each point swings freely, but at the top of the swing their common balance point rises only to the height from which the whole had begun to fall. Then Daniel Bernoulli worked out the speed of water leaving an orifice in a tank by imagining that each particle of water, in a tiny interval of time, moves separately up to the height its speed would carry it. The particles' balance point must rise as far as the tank and jet's had fallen, and the long-sought speed followed at once.

Three problems, one law, and the law is a sentence nobody could use alone: actual descent equals potential ascent. Kuhn's point is that the sentence is nearly impotent until you have learned to recognize actual descents and potential ascents as things that occur in nature, and you learn that by working the problems, in the same breath as learning the words. What a student acquires, he says in the postscript, is the ability to see a variety of situations as like one another. After enough of them, the student sees new situations in the same gestalt as the rest of the specialty, and for the student they are no longer the situations met at the start of training. A group-licensed way of seeing has been assimilated.

The sixty-nine postscript also repairs something the book never did. The word paradigm, which the first edition used for everything from Newton's Principia to a particular experiment, drew a famous complaint from Margaret Masterman, who counted twenty-one senses in which Kuhn used it. Kuhn cites her paper, says a sympathetic reader found at least twenty-two, and agrees that most of the differences are stylistic inconsistency. He splits the term in two. The global sense becomes the disciplinary matrix, meaning everything a specialist community shares: symbolic generalizations such as force equals mass times acceleration, shared models and metaphysical commitments, shared values, and the exemplars. The narrow sense, the concrete problem solution used as a model, is what he says he now means by paradigm, and he calls it the deepest sense and the one the book most needs. The postscript adds a correction about scale that matters for the funding discussion below. The community that shares a matrix is not a whole discipline. Kuhn guesses that it is typically about a hundred people, occasionally far fewer, and says a revolution can be a change in the commitments of a group of fewer than twenty-five.

Chapter Six: Why the Textbook Lies

The eleventh chapter, on the invisibility of revolutions, is the one that I think carries the argument of this half of the book to its conclusion, and it sets up the next episode. The question is why revolutions, if they are as regular as Kuhn says, have been so hard for scientists and the public to see.

His answer is that the main source of our image of science is a document written for a different purpose. Textbooks, popularizations and the philosophy of science modeled on them all describe an already articulated body of problems, data and theory. To do their job, they need not report how that body was first recognized and adopted, and they have functional reasons to misreport it. Because a textbook exists to perpetuate normal science, it has to be rewritten whenever the language, the problem structure or the standards of normal science change. Once rewritten, it hides the role and even the existence of the revolution that required the rewriting. A scientist who has not lived through a revolution has a historical sense that reaches only as far as the most recent one.

The textbook then supplies a substitute for what it removed: a little history, in an introductory chapter or in scattered references to heroes. The effect, Kuhn says, is to make students feel they belong to a tradition that never existed. Earlier scientists are represented as having worked on identical problems under the canons that the latest revolution made look scientific. He quotes Whitehead on the spirit of the profession: a science that hesitates to forget its founders is lost. Kuhn corrects him. Sciences keep their heroes and forget or revise what the heroes wrote.

He gives three examples, and each is a small case of a figure being credited with answering a question that his own framework did not allow him to ask. Dalton wrote three incompatible accounts of how he came to chemical atomism, and all three make him interested from an early date in combining proportions, the problem he is famous for solving. Kuhn, citing the historian Leslie Nash, says the problems seem to have occurred to him together with their solutions, late in his work, after he had carried a set of questions over from physics and meteorology. Newton wrote that Galileo had found that a constant force of gravity produces a motion proportional to the square of the time. Galileo said nothing of the kind, and rarely mentioned forces at all. His theorem has that form only when embedded in Newton's dynamics, and by crediting Galileo with it, Newton's account hides the small revolution in what scientists asked about motion. And the concept of an element, attributed in chemistry texts to Boyle, whose own text gave a definition close to the modern one while arguing that no such thing as an element exists. The textbook version, Kuhn says, is mistaken as history, and the mistake gets built into the technical structure of the text.

The consequence he draws goes beyond history. Textbooks present experiments, concepts, laws and theories one at a time, as nearly in sequence as pedagogy allows. The impression created is of a building put up brick by brick. That is not how a science develops, he says, because many of the puzzles of contemporary normal science did not exist before the most recent revolution. The constancy of chemical composition may be one element in a new fabric of fact and theory that Dalton fitted to chemical experience as a whole, changing it in the process. Theories do not evolve to fit facts that were there all along. They emerge together with the facts they fit.

Here the narrator's own inference, and I will flag it as such, is that Kuhn's mechanism applies to institutions as well as to experiments. The previous episode in this series was about the nineteen forty-five report to Truman by Vannevar Bush, and its reputation as the founding text of a pipeline from basic research to products. Historians such as Benoit Godin, whose argument I summarized there from an abstract, have disputed whether that pipeline is in the report at all. Read next to Kuhn's chapter, that quarrel looks like a textbook phenomenon. A founding text becomes a hero, the later framework is read back into it, and the reading hardens into the history people teach. I cannot show that this is what happened to Bush, and Kuhn does not say so; the claim is that the mechanism he describes would produce this pattern, and that any founding document in a field invites the question of what its author could and could not have been asking.

Chapter Seven: The Case Against, from Popper and from the Evidence

The book met its most famous objection in person. In nineteen sixty-five, at a colloquium in London, Kuhn delivered a paper contrasting his view with Karl Popper's, and Popper replied. The exchange was published in nineteen seventy under the title Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge, with Kuhn answering his critics at the end. Popper's essay, called Normal Science and Its Dangers, is not what its reputation suggests.

It starts generously. Kuhn's criticism, Popper says, is the most interesting he has come across, and Kuhn understands him better than most of his critics do. He concedes that he had been at best dimly aware of the distinction between normal and extraordinary science, and that the distinction points to something of great importance. He quotes the preface to his own nineteen thirty-four book, which says that a scientist can attack a problem within an organized structure and leave it to others to fit his contribution into the framework of knowledge, and he points out that Kuhn overlooked it. Normal science in Kuhn's sense, he writes, exists.

Then the case. Popper says the normal scientist is a person one ought to be sorry for, and that he has been taught badly, in a dogmatic spirit, a victim of indoctrination. He has learned a technique to be applied without asking why, and has become what Popper calls an applied scientist, content to solve puzzles, a term Popper reads as a sign that the problems are not fundamental. The danger, he says, is a danger to science and to civilization, and it grows with specialization. His historical objection is that few scientists on record were normal in Kuhn's sense. He offers Darwin before the Origin, brimming with fundamental problems; descriptive botany, where genuine problems arise at every stage; and Boltzmann, a follower of Maxwell who was as far from a normal scientist as anybody. Between Kuhn's two types, he asserts, there must be many gradations. And Kuhn's schema of one dominant theory per field fits astronomy but not the theory of matter, where at least three theories had competed since antiquity, in what Popper calls constant and fruitful discussion.

The charge that matters most to Popper is logical. If rational criticism is possible only inside a shared framework, then frameworks cannot be criticized from outside, and that is relativism. He calls this the myth of the framework. His reply is that we are prisoners of our framework only in a Pickwickian sense, since if we try we can break out of it at any time, and he adds that discussion across frameworks, difficult as it is, has been among the most fruitful things in intellectual history.

Kuhn's reply is mostly about what follows from Popper's own premises. If frameworks are necessary for research, and breaking out of one means breaking into another, as Popper concedes, then the hold of a framework cannot be explained merely by bad teaching. Nor can it be explained, as Popper's colleague John Watkins had supposed, by the prevalence of third-rate minds suited to plodding work. Those exist, Kuhn says, and do damage. But a framework that is a prerequisite of research and also something one can leave at will, he argues, is close to a contradiction in terms. As for his critics, he says that the science Popper calls normal and the one Kuhn does are the same, and that Popper's dimly aware sense of the distinction follows from Popper's own position. I find that reply close to decisive on the question of teaching. A student who is not trained into a framework does not become a free critic. The student becomes a person with no research program at all.

Where Popper is on firmer ground is the question of how much of science fits the picture, and Kuhn conceded part of it himself. The postscript says a paradigm governs a group of practitioners and not a subject matter, and that theories of matter were for a long time tools used by many communities, not the property of one, so that disagreement about them was no counterexample to agreement inside any single specialty. That reply is partly a repair and partly a retreat to a position that is harder to test. If a community can be identified without reference to its paradigm, as Kuhn says it should be, then someone ought to be able to check whether such a community of about a hundred people does in fact share an exemplar set and a set of values. Citation networks and conference attendance, which the postscript suggests, are the means. The postscript also softens another claim. Crisis, he writes, need not be an absolute prerequisite of revolution, only the usual prelude.

The other attack comes from inside Kuhn's own vocabulary. Masterman's count of senses was not a quibble. If the word that names the book's main idea has two dozen meanings, an empirical test of the idea is hard to construct, since a given finding can be read as a paradigm in one sense surviving and in another sense failing. The postscript's split into matrix and exemplar is Kuhn's answer, and it is a good one for the exemplar sense, where the evidence from teaching is concrete. It is less convincing for the matrix, which now holds everything.

A last item belongs here, because it shows Kuhn is not simply the defender of normal science that Popper's essay makes him. In nineteen fifty-nine, three years before the book, Kuhn presented a paper at a conference in Utah on identifying scientific talent, later reprinted in a collection under the title The Essential Tension. I know its argument from secondary accounts and not from the paper, so I will give only its outline. Scientific education, he said, is a convergent training, and normal research is a convergent activity. Yet the cumulative effect of convergent work has repeatedly been to change the tradition, and that needs people able to think divergently at the right moment. The scientist has to be both traditionalist and iconoclast, and no rule tells a person which to be on a given day. That sets up the question Popper's essay never really answers: how a system that rewards convergence by design is supposed to produce the person who diverges.

Chapter Eight: The Study Section

So far the examples have come from physics, the field Kuhn knew best. The practical half of this episode concerns a place where his normal science is an institution with a budget, a score sheet and a deadline.

Begin with the puzzle criterion, because it translates almost without loss. A puzzle, Kuhn says, is a problem whose solution is assured and whose permissible methods are limited by rules. A grant reviewer, asked to judge feasibility, is asked for the same two things. Is there reason to believe this can be done? Are the methods ones the field accepts as able to do it? Preliminary data is the grant's proof that the pieces came from one box. The cure-for-cancer sentence has a version in every application, and its fate is predictable. An aim that is stated as a cure may not have an assured solution, so it is rewritten, by the experienced applicant, as a puzzle in the cure's neighborhood, with an assured result, an accepted method and a sentence of hope.

This is not an accusation. In January of twenty twenty-five, the National Institutes of Health began using a simplified review framework that reduces the older five criteria to three: the importance of the research, which merges significance and innovation, and is scored; rigor and feasibility, which covers approach, and is scored; and expertise and resources, which merges investigator and environment and is judged only as sufficient or not. The agency's stated aims include reducing the influence of reputation on scores. Look at the first two factors from Kuhn's side. Importance is the pole at which the field's exemplars are most clearly relevant, because importance to a field means importance as defined by its current problems. Rigor and feasibility is the puzzle test: can this be done with accepted methods? A proposal built on the paradigm does well on both. One whose importance lies in showing that the field's framing is wrong does badly on each, since its importance is invisible from inside the framing and its feasibility cannot be shown with the framing's methods. I am not saying the reviewers are conservative people. I am saying the form's questions are asked in the paradigm's language, and a form written in that language will favor work that is fluent in it.

What does the evidence say? Less than the claim needs, and I would not want to overstate either side. In December of twenty twelve, Joshua Nicholson and John Ioannidis published a comment in Nature arguing that too many authors of the most influential life-science papers were not funded by the NIH. They had taken the roughly seven hundred biomedical papers, from a Scopus search, that had been cited at least a thousand times, and found that only a small fraction of the NIH panel members were among the lead authors of such papers. The title was Conform and Be Funded. The computational biologist Steven Salzberg re-analysed a random sample of a hundred and twenty-five of the papers and found that sixty-three were review articles, which do not report original findings, and seventeen were not life-science papers. Of the forty-five that remained, thirty-four, or three quarters, had NIH support, and the others included work from the Army and the Centers for Disease Control, where NIH money would not be expected. He also criticized the requirement that both first and last authors be principal investigators. I have Salzberg's numbers through a secondary write-up, not from his own post, and I have not read the original article. On the evidence, I would say the conformity claim in its strong form lost its best test, and Kuhn's mechanism does not depend on it. Normal science does not require that outliers go unfunded; it requires only that the funded problems are mostly puzzles. That is a claim about composition, and nobody has measured the composition of the portfolio against a definition of puzzle.

A fair summary is that the evidence for or against Kuhn on the funding system is thin, that the best-known test of the conformity claim does not stand up, and that the prediction, which concerns what the system selects and not whom it excludes, remains untested. Ioannidis is the author of the hazelnut essay from episode fifty-six, and the habit he showed there of asking what the numbers can bear applies here too.

Here is a narrator's test, which Kuhn does not offer. Take any proposal, yours or another's, and write down the puzzle: the question, the accepted method, and the result the field would predict. Then ask what the specialty would learn if the result came out the other way. Suppose the answer is that colleagues would conclude the experiment had failed. By Kuhn's definition that proposal is a pure puzzle, since the community treats an off-prediction result as a flaw in the scientist and not in nature. Suppose instead the answer is that the specialty would have to revise what it believes. That proposal is a bet on an anomaly, and in Kuhn's account the community will be slow to credit it. Neither kind is a defect. A portfolio of puzzles alone would repeat what the electricians did after seventeen-forty, producing better instruments and no new phenomena, and one of anomaly-bets alone would accumulate nothing. What a funder's list reveals is the ratio between them, and the ratio is knowable only by reading proposals the way a historian reads the Leyden jar, asking what the authors thought they were looking for.

The question to carry into the coming week is for anyone who sits on a panel or writes to one. In the next proposal you read, look for the line giving what the investigators expect to see, and then for the one giving what they would do on seeing the opposite. A missing second line suggests a puzzle, and a good puzzle may be just what the field needs. A detailed second line raises another question: how would a reviewer in the same specialty score it, and does the form have anywhere to record that difference?
