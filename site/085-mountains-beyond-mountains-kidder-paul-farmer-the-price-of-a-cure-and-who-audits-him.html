Mountains Beyond Mountains (Kidder): Paul Farmer, the price of a cure, and who audits him

Tracy Kidder's portrait of Paul Farmer, read as a fight over arithmetic: drug-resistant tuberculosis in a Lima slum, a donor's six million dollars, a drug whose price fell ninety-seven percent, and whether the doctor who refused triage could be checked by anyone.

18 November 2026

Chapter One: Six Million Dollars

In April 1998 a room full of the world's tuberculosis experts met at the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in Boston to hear early results from a small treatment project in a slum on the northern edge of Lima. On the first afternoon a bearded microbiologist named Alex Goldfarb leaned into his microphone and did some arithmetic out loud. Goldfarb was running the Soros Foundation's tuberculosis work in Russia, where, he said, a hundred thousand prison inmates had active disease and most were being given a single drug, the worst regimen there is.

His sum went like this. He had six million dollars. Three million would pay for standard treatment for five thousand inmates. If a tenth of them carried strains resistant to the main drugs, then four and a half thousand would be cured and five hundred would not, and would die. With the other three million he could treat those five hundred with the expensive reserve drugs, or he could go to another region and treat five thousand more. His choice, as he put it, was not about the rights of five hundred people. It was five hundred against five thousand. And if he told Russians he had spent six thousand dollars a case in the prisons while tens of thousands died around them, they would say he was building a golden palace for a few.

The chair had to bang the gavel. The young people from the organisation that had run the Lima project glared at him. Tracy Kidder, reconstructing the meeting for his book about the project's leader, wrote that Goldfarb had made an argument they would have to answer, sooner or later.

The leader was Paul Farmer, an infectious disease doctor at the Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston who spent most of the year running a clinic in the central plateau of Haiti. The book is Mountains Beyond Mountains, published in 2003, and it is mostly remembered as a portrait of a good man. I want to read it as something narrower and, I think, more useful: a record of what happened to Goldfarb's arithmetic over the following four years, and of whether a doctor who refused that arithmetic on principle could be checked by anybody.

This series has been asking that question of each of its doers. Robert Moses answered to nobody. Lee Kuan Yew answered to himself. Rickover answered to a reactor, Borlaug to a harvest, Groves to a test in the desert, and Georges Doriot, last episode, to a record so skewed by one company that only his written process could judge him. Farmer is the hardest case, because the thing that would normally audit a doctor working among the poor, a budget and a ranking of what each dollar buys, was the thing he spent his career arguing against.

Chapter Two: The Writer Who Was Disarmed

Kidder met Farmer in December 1994 in Mirebalais, a market town in Haiti's central plateau, where Kidder was reporting on the American soldiers sent to restore the elected president. The occasion, Farmer reminded him years later, was a beheading. Kidder had already written The Soul of a New Machine and a string of books built on long immersion in other people's working lives, and he spent the next several years following Farmer from Haiti to Boston, Lima, Siberia, Paris and Cuba. The book is written in Kidder's first person, with Kidder visible as the slower walker, the man who drinks his filtered water too early and asks the awkward question about money.

The life behind it is short to state. Farmer grew up poor, for five years of it in a converted bus at a campground in Florida, went to Duke on a scholarship, discovered liberation theology and its phrase "a preferential option for the poor" while reading about the murder of Archbishop Romero, and met Haitian migrant labourers in the fields near campus. He went to Haiti before medical school and stayed in the habit, taking a joint degree in medicine and anthropology at Harvard while building a clinic in Cange, a squatter village of families displaced when an American-planned dam flooded their valley in the 1950s. The clinic became Zanmi Lasante, a Creole name meaning partners in health. In 1987 a Boston construction magnate named Tom White, who intended to die without a nickel, paid a lawyer to create a Boston charity under the English version of that name, PIH for short, to support it, run by Farmer, his friend Ophelia Dahl, and a medical student named Jim Kim.

By 1999, Kidder reports, Zanmi Lasante was treating roughly as many people a year as his local hospital in Massachusetts, which had a budget of sixty million dollars. Zanmi Lasante spent about one and a half million, half of it in donated drugs. It cured an uncomplicated case of tuberculosis in a patient's hut for between a hundred and fifty and two hundred dollars, against fifteen to twenty thousand in an American hospital. Nobody in its catchment area had died of tuberculosis since 1988.

Those numbers are Kidder's, and the obvious worry about a book like this is that its author fell in love. Kidder raises the worry himself. In the chapter on Farmer's near-fatal hepatitis, which Farmer diagnosed on the last day of a month he had spent working through it, Kidder wonders at the recklessness of a man responsible for so many lives, and then admits that he had come to hold Farmer to a higher standard than he held anyone else, himself included, and that "to see him in action was to excuse him."

That sentence is the book's own answer to the question of who audits Farmer, and it is a confession that the biographer cannot. So the episode has to look for the audit elsewhere, and the first place is a study Farmer ran on himself.

Chapter Three: The Study in Cange

In 1988 a woman from the clinic's catchment area died of tuberculosis while Farmer was in Boston recovering from a broken leg, hit by a car. The staff told him, kindly, that she would have lived if he had been there. He heard it as an indictment. He wanted, Kidder writes, a medical system that worked in his absence.

So he called meetings, and the meetings split. The community health workers, most of them recently peasant farmers themselves, said the poorest patients did worst because they were hungry and could not afford the trip to the clinic; one of them quoted a proverb about washing your hands and drying them in the dirt. The Haitian professionals, the doctor and nurses, said what the journals said: patients stopped taking their pills once they felt better, partly because they believed the disease was sent by an enemy through sorcery.

Farmer, still a student, designed a study to settle it. Two groups of patients got free treatment. One group also got regular visits from health workers and a small monthly cash stipend for food, child care and transport. He hiked to every patient's hut to interview them, and asked each whether tuberculosis came from sorcery. Nearly all said it did, in both groups. Then the results. Among those given only free medicine, forty-eight percent were cured. Among those who also had the stipend and the visits, everyone recovered.

The sorcery question had predicted nothing. When Farmer went back to one of his favourite patients, an elderly woman who a year earlier had told him with some offence that she knew perfectly well the disease came from coughing, she now said of course she believed in sorcery, and she knew who had sent hers, and she meant to get her back. When he asked why, in that case, she had taken all her pills, she smiled at him the way an elder smiles at a child and asked, in Kidder's free translation of the Creole, whether he was incapable of complexity.

There are questions Kidder does not ask. How were the two groups chosen? He does not say, and nothing in the passage suggests the allocation was random. How many patients were in each? The book gives Farmer's line about a hundred chatty Haitians and no breakdown. A cure rate of everyone is a number that small, unrandomised comparisons produce more often than large ones do. None of that makes the finding wrong, and its direction has held up well in the decades of work on social support for tuberculosis treatment since. But be clear about what kind of evidence it was: a strong signal from a careful doctor's own programme, which he then acted on without waiting for anyone else to replicate it. From then on every tuberculosis patient in the catchment area got the stipend, about five American dollars a month, and the clinic did not lose a patient to the disease for twelve years.

That is the first pattern in Farmer's way of being checked. He ran the check himself, read it generously, and changed the system at once. The second pattern arrived in Peru, where the check was run by somebody else.

Chapter Four: Ten Patients on a Bench

Jim Kim wanted a project of his own, and in the early 1990s a priest Farmer had boarded with in medical school, Father Jack Roussin, had moved from a Boston parish to Carabayllo, a shantytown of sand and rock on Lima's northern edge. Kim started a branch there, Socios en Salud. Farmer, after a health census, told Kim that the one thing they would not need to do in Lima was tuberculosis, because Peru had just built a national programme that the World Health Organization called the best in the developing world.

In May 1995 Father Jack flew to Boston, was diagnosed with tuberculosis, started the standard four drugs, and died about a month later. The culture came back a day or two after his death. His strain was resistant to all four drugs he had been given and to the fifth first-line drug as well. He had never been treated before, so he had caught it from someone, most likely in his parish.

The director of Socios, a quiet Peruvian public health graduate named Jaime Bayona, went looking. The official records showed nothing. The clinic staff, asked about patients with highly resistant disease, said no, after a pause. So he changed the question: had anyone been treated and not cured? and a nurse said oh, sure. Some nurses would open a file on the desk and say they could not show it to him, and he taught himself to read records upside down.

Farmer flew in and sat on a wooden bench in a government clinic while ten patients came in one at a time, some carried. He carried their sputum back to the Massachusetts state laboratory in his suitcase. Most turned out to be resistant to all five first-line drugs. And their records told a story he had not seen in Haiti. In Cange, resistance came from too little medicine: a strike, a flood, a clinic that closed. These patients had done everything right. They had been treated under direct observation, the strategy the World Health Organization called DOTS, short for directly observed treatment, short course, which Farmer considered the greatest advance in tuberculosis control since antibiotics. When the first course failed, the guidelines called for the same drugs again, plus one, a rule drawn from African trials two decades old in which most failures had come from patients skipping pills. In Lima the failures came from resistant bacteria. Each round of the official regimen killed off the susceptible bacilli and left the resistant ones to grow, so the patients emerged two years later sicker and resistant to more drugs. Farmer called the process amplification. They had got worse because they followed doctors' orders.

After amplifying their resistance, the programme had nothing more to offer them. The reserve drugs, called second-line, were sold privately, and families sold what they owned to buy enough to make the resistance worse. The official manual Kidder quotes said that where resources were constrained, treatment categories should be prioritised by cost-effectiveness. Another official statement said, more plainly, that in developing countries people with this kind of tuberculosis usually die, because effective treatment is often impossible there.

Hold that against Goldfarb's six million. The manual's sentence is his arithmetic written as policy, and it had a consequence the arithmetic did not show. The patients it set aside were not a fixed category of hopeless cases. Some of them had been made hopeless by the regimen the same manual prescribed.

Chapter Five: The Case for the Normas

The Peruvian authorities were not villains, and Kidder, to his credit, lets Bayona explain why.

Peru's programme existed because residents of places like Carabayllo, with their nuns and priests, had marched for it, and some of the programme's leaders had marched with them. In a time of austerity they had got the government to pay for DOTS and had used the money well, ending decades of chaotic treatment. The rules, las normas, said a patient had to fail both the first course and the retreatment before anyone could try anything else. Socios asked to take patients earlier, after the first failure, and offered to pay for everything. The answer was no, and the reason was the precedent. If a foreign charity set a higher standard in one slum, the national programme would have to meet it across the country, and the only money for that was the DOTS money, and spending it would recreate the conditions that had bred the resistance in the first place.

That argument is right on its own terms, and it cost a young man named David Carbajal his life. A Harvard medical student working with Socios, Sonya Shin, found him with probable resistant disease and could not get permission to treat him. She helped his sister shave and dress him for the funeral. His parents, consoling her, said it was a problem in the system: the system could not try another regimen because that would mean admitting a bigger problem. Farmer wrote an angry letter. The reply called it inappropriate behaviour in a foreign doctor.

This show already met the structure those parents described. Dan Davies calls it an accountability sink, a decision moved into a rule so that no person has to answer for it. The nurse who opened the file and would not show it, the doctor who knew and could not act, the official who could not set a precedent, each was following a rule written in Geneva and applied in Lima, and the rule's authors never met David Carbajal.

The strongest version of the rule came from its chief defender. Arata Kochi, who ran the World Health Organization's tuberculosis programme and had spent years selling DOTS to the world, told Kidder later that resistant tuberculosis was mostly the product of human error, that if a programme could not treat it right it should not try, and that in a review in South Africa they had found a third of the whole tuberculosis budget going to a few resistant cases while the basic programme was poor. Use your money for DOTS, they had said. Then Farmer came, with what Kochi saw as the activists' approach: good clinicians without public health experience, for whom the patient in front of them was everything. Kochi called it an unsolvable conflict.

I think Kochi's South African case is a stronger argument against Farmer than Goldfarb's, because it names a mechanism. Treating the worst cases well is expensive and visible. Running the basic programme well is cheap and invisible. A ministry under pressure will spend on the visible thing, and a weak basic programme will keep manufacturing resistant patients faster than any amount of reserve drugs can cure them. Archie Cochrane, earlier on this shelf, made the general version of that complaint about the British health service: that a system which does not measure what its money buys will spend it on what looks like care. Goldfarb and Kochi were Cochrane's heirs, asking Farmer to count.

Chapter Six: The Price Column

Farmer's first reply was rhetorical, and the room did not move. At a meeting in Chicago in 1997 he read out a list of what he called myths about resistant tuberculosis. Too expensive to treat? A family in Texas in which one person had infected nine others had cost more than a million dollars. DOTS alone would stop it? Only if resistance were less contagious than ordinary tuberculosis, which he called wishful thinking. When the moderator thanked him for a provocative talk, Farmer turned back from the stairs to ask why it was provocative to say that sick people should be treated if the technology existed. A rumour reached Lima that somebody had phoned Peru's programme director to say Farmer was accusing him of killing patients.

Then the evidence came in. By the Boston meeting Socios had treated its first fifty-three patients for most of two years, and more than eighty-five percent, Kidder reports, appeared to be cured. The best treatment centre in the world, National Jewish in Denver, had reported cure rates of about sixty percent five years earlier, with one especially hard case costing a quarter of a million dollars. Kochi, who had been a student of the meeting's chairman at the Harvard School of Public Health, opened by using a new term his staff had coined, DOTS-plus, a strategy that would include treating resistant cases where resistance was common. He explained to Kidder later, smiling, that if you cannot beat them you join them, and then to some extent you can control them.

None of that answered Goldfarb, and Jim Kim, speaking the next morning, did not pretend it did. Many people had asked why a small charity had taken on something so costly, he said, and they were right to wonder. To pay for Peru, PIH had chosen not to feed perhaps four thousand more children in Haiti.

So the triage was real, and Farmer's camp had made it. What happened next concerns a column of the spreadsheet everyone had treated as a constant.

The main expense in treating resistant tuberculosis was the drugs, and the drugs were expensive for a reason that had nothing to do with making them. Kim, whose doctoral research had been on the Korean pharmaceutical industry, found that the patents on all but one class of second-line drugs had expired years earlier. The market was small because the patients were poor, so few companies made the drugs, and they could name their price. Take one of them, capreomycin, an injected antibiotic made by Eli Lilly. The Brigham's pharmacy paid twenty-nine dollars ninety a vial. Lilly, after long badgering, sold it to the project in Peru at twenty-one dollars. In Paris it cost eight dollars eighty. When Farmer tried to buy it in Paris, the agent told him there was a global shortage. Farmer asked why. There seemed to be an emergency. Where? In Peru.

Kidder calls the next problem a logician's conundrum. To lower the prices, Kim had to show that many programmes would buy the drugs. For many programmes to buy them, the prices had to fall. For the prices to fall, generic makers had to come in, and they would be likelier to come in if the World Health Organization put the drugs on its essential medicines list. But a drug almost nobody uses is, by definition, not essential. The verdict that treatment was not cost-effective at current prices kept demand low, and low demand kept the prices where they were. The cost-effectiveness analysis was auditing a price its own conclusion helped hold up.

The circle broke from several sides at once. A young Dutchman named Guido Bakker, from the International Dispensary Association, a nonprofit that buys essential drugs for poor countries, saw through a slide of inflated demand Kim showed to the drug companies, was angrier at the companies than at Kim, and announced from his seat that his organisation would go to generic manufacturers. It did, with Doctors Without Borders putting up the cash for the first shipments. Kim lobbied Geneva for the essential list. Eminent experts wrote in against it, and their worry was a serious one: cheap reserve drugs, loose in health systems where pharmacists mixed tuberculosis pills into cough syrup, would breed strains no drug could touch.

The answer to that worry was institutional. A young PIH staffer seconded to Geneva found a precedent, a body that rationed scarce meningitis vaccine called the Green Light Committee. Kim said a Red Light Committee would sound bad, so they would call theirs the same thing and seem to be following precedent. The committee became the gate through which the cheap drugs flowed. Any programme that wanted the low prices had to show it had a sound plan and, first, a sound basic DOTS programme. The World Health Organization put the drugs in an annex to its essential list.

By 2000 capreomycin, the drug that had cost about thirty dollars a vial at the Brigham, cost ninety-eight cents, a fall of ninety-seven percent, and most of the other second-line drugs had fallen nearly as far. The drugs to treat a case resistant to four drugs cost PIH about fifteen hundred dollars instead of fifteen thousand.

Now go back to the Boston room. Goldfarb's six thousand dollars a case was his whole budget for each resistant patient. In 1996, the drugs alone for one such patient in Lima cost two and a half times that. By 2000 they cost a quarter of it. Nobody had won the argument about five hundred against five thousand. The price had moved under it, and it moved because a small charity had treated patients at the old price, published results, and pushed until the market and the list caught up.

Here is the answer I have come to, and it is not the one I started with. I began this book expecting the usual standoff between the clinician who will not count and the planner who must, with the verdict going to the planner on the arithmetic and to the clinician on the conscience. That is not what the Peru chapters show. The planners were right about their inputs and wrong to treat one of them as fixed, because the price of a drug for a disease of the poor is partly a consequence of the decision not to treat it. Farmer's side did not abolish the audit. They changed its inputs, and then they built an auditor of their own. The Green Light Committee is Kochi's South African objection written into the supply chain: you may have the cheap drugs only if your basic programme is good enough not to waste them. The man who refused triage at the bedside ended up, through Kim, designing a rationing gate for the world, a searcher in Cange, as the economist Arnold Kling called him in 2008, borrowing William Easterly's word, who became in Peru a planner working from inside the planners' institutions.

Chapter Seven: The Doer's Own Ledger

So the doer was checked, in the end, by results. Which raises the question of who checked the results.

The paper came out in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2003, with Carole Mitnick as first author and Farmer among the authors. Its abstract describes a review of the charts of the first seventy-five patients treated in northern Lima, whose strains were resistant to a median of six drugs. Of the sixty-six who completed at least four months of therapy, fifty-five were probably cured when treatment ended, which the abstract reports as eighty-three percent.

Now change the denominator of the abstract's headline. Those fifty-five cures, divided by all seventy-five who started is seventy-three. The nine who stopped short of four months are the patients a programme's critics would most want to hear about, and the abstract counts them out of the headline. The design is a chart review of a programme by the people who ran it. The patients, Kidder notes, were younger and had fewer other illnesses than Denver's, an advantage Kidder mentions himself. And the eighty-five percent the Boston meeting heard in 1998 came from fifty-three patients partway through.

None of that reverses the finding. Seventy-three percent cured, in a slum, of strains resistant to six drugs, against sixty in the best hospital in America, is an enormous result, and the World Health Organization adopted treatment guidelines close to Socios's in June 2002, the kind of audit a field gives a claim when it believes it. But the episode has been asking who checks Farmer, and the honest description is that Farmer and his colleagues checked themselves, with skill and with the generosity of authors toward their own programme, and the world took the check on trust because the alternative was patients dying.

The money was checked even less. When Farmer first asked Tom White to fund Peru, he said ten patients, no more, and knew it was what he called a fib. Farmer and Kim filled their briefcases at the Brigham pharmacy on the way to Lima until the hospital's president told a senior colleague that they owed ninety-two thousand dollars; White paid the bill with a note suggesting the hospital be more generous to the poor. Ophelia Dahl had saved about a million dollars toward an endowment, and it went on Peru. By 2000 Dahl's memo to Farmer and Kim, which Kidder prints, listed the monthly salaries and transfers and asked for ideas, and the three of them drew up what they called the post-Tom plan, for the day White's fortune ran out. The only budget committee PIH answered to in those years was an elderly developer spending down his fortune on purpose, who never said no.

What rescued them was the Gates Foundation, which in July 2000 gave PIH and a group of partners forty-five million dollars to treat resistant tuberculosis across Peru. Note the order. The large audited money came after the results, not before. Farmer's working method was to spend first, on a patron's trust, and produce the evidence that would justify the spending afterwards. That method works when the evidence comes out the right way, and when the patron's trust lasts long enough for it to arrive.

Chapter Eight: Seven Hours for Two Families

Kidder saves the hardest version of Goldfarb's question for the walk that closes the book.

Medical triage, he points out, has two meanings that point in opposite directions. On a battlefield it means attending first to the wounded most likely to survive and letting the others go, to save the most. In a well-stocked emergency room it means taking the gravest first, with nobody refused. Farmer, Kidder writes, built his life around the second kind, in a country that resembled the first.

On the walk to a town called Casse, eleven hours in all, Farmer visited two families. Kidder asks him about a boy named John, a Haitian child with a rare tumour that was curable if it had not spread. Getting him to Massachusetts General, which agreed to treat him free, took a medevac jet that cost eighteen and a half thousand dollars. In Boston the scans showed the cancer in his spine. He died in hospice in a PIH doctor's apartment. A new PIH staffer had told Kidder she could not stop thinking of what else that twenty thousand dollars could have done.

Farmer's answer, given over his shoulder on a mountain path, has three parts. The first is clinical: John was referred with a predicted cure rate of sixty to seventy percent, and the test that would have shown the spread could not be done in Haiti. That is a good answer. The second is the one his admirers quote. A young American attending physician, he says, earns about a hundred thousand dollars a year, five times what the flight cost, and nobody calls it thoughtful to point that out; the critic of the flight sounds reasonable, and anyone raising the salary sounds like a boor. The third is the stance he calls the long defeat: he has fought all his life on the losing side, he will keep doing it, and the victories are a bonus.

I think the second answer fails. It changes who is on trial without touching the sum. The doctor's salary exists whether or not John flies; the twenty thousand was a choice at the margin, and the margin is where Goldfarb was standing. Kidder, who likes him, seems to feel this too, because he records that Farmer had already argued it with him many times and that Kidder was, in Farmer's words, still not convinced.

There is a cleaner test of the same principle in Tomsk, in Siberia. Cheap second-line drugs were on their way to Russia, delayed by snags. Other groups were waiting for them. Farmer and Kim got a hundred and fifty thousand dollars from White and bought drugs at the old prices to start thirty-seven prisoners at once. Kidder put the question to him: why spend that on thirty-seven if waiting a while would treat a hundred? Farmer's answer was that project managers could afford to wait for low prices and patients could not.

Run that one through. If those thirty-seven had waited for the new drugs, some would have died in the meantime and others would have amplified their resistance in a cell shared with others. If they started at once, sixty-odd others would get no treatment from that money, though they might get it from later money at lower prices. The book does not settle it. What it shows is that Farmer settled it the same way every time, toward the patient already in front of him, and that the habit which looked like recklessness in Tomsk was the same habit that broke the price of capreomycin. The instinct that produced the second also produced the first. You cannot keep one and audit away the other.

Kidder names the serious, sympathetic critiques himself, and boils them down to two. Walking for hours to see one or two patients is a foolish use of the time of a man who could influence policy for millions. And even if it is not, too few people will follow his example to matter. The wealthy friend of a Harvard colleague, Kidder reports, declined to give because he doubted anyone could reproduce the work. Zanmi Lasante will not survive Farmer, the critics said. The organisation relies too much on a genius.

Chapter Nine: The Model and the Man

The first critique Kidder answers as an admirer: the walks keep Farmer honest and refresh the authority he spends in Geneva. The second he leaves mostly to Jim Kim.

In a new staffer's office at PIH, Kidder found a sign on the wall saying that if Paul was the model, they were golden. The last word was on a strip of paper. Lift it, and underneath was Kim's original word, a crude one meaning the opposite of golden. Kim meant it as a warning to the young people who joined hoping to become Farmer. Imitation, he told Kidder, would put the emphasis in the wrong place, and would fail. Farmer was a model of what should be done, not of how, and if the poor had to wait for many more people like him to come along, their prospects were bleak. Farmer, Kidder adds, agreed, and once told his computer screen, answering a student who said he could never do what Paul did, that he had never said anyone should do what he did, only that these things should be done.

Put that beside the story of Georges Doriot from the last episode. Doriot's firm ran on one man's judgment of people, and when he could find nobody to inherit it, the firm was sold to a conglomerate and slipped away. Farmer's admirers and critics both assumed PIH ran the same way, on a single will. Kim's sign says otherwise, and so, I think, does Part Three. What PIH produced in Peru was not a man to copy but a set of things that work without him: a regimen others could prescribe, a published cure rate others could cite, generic suppliers others could buy from, and a committee that decided who got the drugs. Those are the parts of Farmer's work that are portable, and they are the parts that spread. Kidder's afterword, written in 2002, has Peru with about a thousand chronic patients cured or in treatment and Tomsk with about two hundred and fifty receiving drugs, and the World Bank lending Russia one hundred fifty million dollars for tuberculosis on terms that included treating resistant cases.

The test the critics proposed has since been run. Farmer died in 2022. PIH, by its own description and its admirers', is a large organisation working in a dozen countries; I have not checked its later record closely enough to grade it, and its reputation is not an audit. Kim went on to run the World Bank. But the narrow claim stands. The sentence that Zanmi Lasante would not survive Farmer was a prediction about a person, and what the book shows is that the most consequential things Farmer did were the ones built to be done by others: the system that worked in his absence that he wanted in 1988, written up and handed on.

Chapter Ten: Verdict, and a Test

My verdict is that Goldfarb was right and so was Farmer, about different things, and that the book's lasting value is showing how both could be true. At the prices of 1998, treating five hundred resistant prisoners instead of five thousand more ordinary ones was the wrong use of six million dollars, and anyone who said so out loud was not a villain. Those prices were not a fact about tuberculosis, and the only way anyone found that out was by someone treating patients at the wrong price long enough to break the market. Farmer could not be audited by a ledger whose inputs his own work was changing. He could be audited by his outcomes, which he and his colleagues reported well and generously, and, more slowly, by whether the things he built outlasted him. On the first test he did well, with a denominator that needs a second look. On the second he did better than his critics expected and better than Doriot.

Where I side with the critics: his reply about the doctor's salary is a debater's trick, the spending in Tomsk was a real trade he refused to admit was a trade, and an organisation financed by one patron's willingness never to say no is a bet, not a model. Kidder's book does not hide any of this. It simply declines to weigh it, because, by his own account, watching Farmer work excused him.

For the dose, read Part Three whole, the seven chapters Kidder titles after the Peruvian doctor's sneer, adventuring doctors, from the two-colour map of the world's health to a Moscow hotel room where Kim decides to ask the Gates Foundation for forty-five million dollars. It is a rare account of a public health argument actually changing, told from inside the room. Then read the last chapter, the walk to Casse, for the triage conversation, and stop there; the afterword is a short update. The first two parts are a fine portrait and can be skimmed by a listener who wants the argument. The Mitnick paper's abstract is free on the London School of Hygiene's repository and takes two minutes to read with a pencil on the denominators.

One habit comes out of Part Three for anyone who reads cost-effectiveness analyses, as a journal reviewer, a grant panellist, or a reader with a stake. Find the most expensive input, usually a drug, a device or a salary, and ask who sets its price and whether that price would fall if the decision went the other way. Then rerun the conclusion at a tenth of it, a smaller fall than capreomycin's in four years. If the verdict flips, the analysis has told you about a market, not about the intervention, and a responsible conclusion says so in its first paragraph. A sensitivity analysis that moves the price a quarter either way would never have found Carabayllo's answer, which needed ninety-seven percent.

Next time, a new series opens, on the statistical analysis plan and the arguments behind each of its promises, beginning with Chamberlin, Platt and Meehl on what counts as a test. Thanks for listening.
