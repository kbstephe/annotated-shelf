Significance versus Hypothesis Testing (Fisher, Neyman and Pearson): the duel behind 0.05

Fisher built the significance test and Neyman and Pearson built the hypothesis test, and the two camps spent decades insulting each other. Textbooks fused them into a hybrid neither side wrote, and an analysis plan that names 0.05 inherits the seam.

21 October 2026

Chapter One: The Textbook Author

Gerd Gigerenzer, a psychologist at the Max Planck Institute in Berlin, tells of a visit to a distinguished author of statistics textbooks. The man's name, Gigerenzer says, does not matter. His book had gone through many editions and was used in psychology classes across the country. An early edition had carried a chapter on Bayesian statistics, and one sentence mentioning that statistical theory had moved on from Ronald Fisher to Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson. Both were gone from every later edition. Gigerenzer asked why. Why present statistics as a single hammer when it is a toolbox, and why mix the theories of Fisher and of Neyman and Pearson into an inconsistent hybrid that every decent statistician would reject?

The author did not deny it. He blamed three parties: researchers, who care about getting published and not about statistical thinking; his university, which promoted people by counting their papers; and his publisher, who wanted a single-recipe cookbook with no controversies. At the end of the conversation Gigerenzer asked what statistical theory the man believed in himself. Deep in my heart, the author said, I am a Bayesian.

That is a story told by a man with a case to make, and I will treat it as a scene and not as evidence. What it carries is a claim about the lineage of one habit. The habit goes like this. State a hypothesis of no difference. Run the test. If the result falls below five percent, write that it is significant and treat your own hypothesis as supported. If not, write that it is not. Gigerenzer calls this the null ritual, and he gives it three steps: set up a null hypothesis of no difference without saying what you expect instead; use five percent as the convention for rejecting it; and always do this. The claim is that no statistician ever proposed it. It was assembled in textbooks, from parts of two theories whose authors thought each other's theory was wrong.

If so, the number at the bottom of nearly every trial protocol, the 0.05 in the analysis plan, is a seam. It marks the place where two incompatible ideas were stitched together by people who did not want to explain that they were incompatible. Last time the subject was Fisher's book on experimental design, and the sentence in it that called five percent usual and convenient. That sentence is one of the stitches. This episode asks who pulled on the other end of the thread, what that person meant, and what you commit yourself to when you write the number into a plan.

The first question is simply what each side proposed. Gigerenzer's account of the textbooks can wait until the answer is in hand. I will take Fisher from his own book of 1935, and Neyman and Pearson from their own paper of 1933, which I have read in full from a scan. Gigerenzer's 2004 paper, Mindless Statistics, I also hold as text, and it supplies Fisher's later writings, which I do not have, so for those I will say each time that I am passing along his account.

Chapter Two: Seventy Ways

Begin with Fisher, and with the tea. Eight cups, four mixed one way and four the other, and a lady who says she can tell them apart. Fisher counts before pouring. The four cups she will call milk-first can be chosen from the eight in seventy ways, only one of which is entirely right, so a perfect score by guessing happens once in seventy tries. That is about one and a half percent. A single mistake, swapping one true cup for one false one, can happen in sixteen of the seventy ways, and so a score that good or better turns up by guessing in seventeen of seventy, about one in four. Fisher makes the experimenter decide in advance how improbable a result must be before it counts.

Here is the sentence from the book that the whole hybrid grows from. He imagines an experimenter who will ignore any result with a probability as high as one in twenty, and notes that such a person gains nothing from an experiment of three cups of each kind, because three objects can be chosen from six in only twenty ways, so complete success by pure chance comes in five trials out of a hundred. Then, in the next breath: it is usual and convenient for experimenters to take five percent as a standard level of significance, in the sense that they are prepared to ignore all results which fail to reach it.

Read what that sentence is doing. It is an argument about the size of an experiment. Three cups a side cannot reach the standard however well the lady performs, and eight cups can. It is a convenience, and he calls it one. The passage goes on to say that no such selection removes the effects of chance, and that when a result occurs once in seventy trials, the experimenter has still not demonstrated a natural phenomenon. For that, he says, you need a reliable method of procedure, one you know how to run so that it will rarely fail to give a significant result. Last episode that sentence did most of the work against Fisher in the smoking dispute. Here it does a different job, because it says the five percent was never meant to settle anything on its own. It was a filter for the first pass.

The same chapter, a few pages before, names the thing that is being filtered. Fisher gives it a label, the null hypothesis. In the tea experiment it is the hypothesis that the lady's judgments are unaffected by the order in which the milk and tea went in. It is never proved or established, he says, but is possibly disproved in the course of experimentation, and every experiment may be said to exist only to give the facts a chance of disproving it. It has to be exact, because it must supply the basis for working out the distribution of possible results. The claim that she can discriminate somewhat, however true, fixes no distribution to count against, so it cannot be the thing tested. A null may also contain arbitrary elements: it may assert that the death rates of two groups of animals are equal without specifying what those rates are. Nothing in the definition requires zero effect. Zero is just the case he used for the tea.

Now the sentence in which the later quarrel is already visible. In his discussion of the null hypothesis Fisher takes up what he calls an error of the so-called second kind, the error of accepting the null when it is false. For a test where a specific amount is being estimated, he says, such an error can be given a meaning. But it has no meaning, he writes, with respect to simple tests of significance, in which the only available expectations are those which flow from the null hypothesis being true. A test of significance has one hypothesis in it. It asks how surprising the data are if that hypothesis holds. It cannot ask how surprising they would be under some other one, because no other has been written down.

It is a design decision, and Fisher made it on purpose. Under his scheme a result is either significant, and the null becomes hard to believe, or it is not, and you have learned nothing. A trial of twenty children that finds no difference has, on Fisher's account, found nothing at all, and has no way of saying how large a difference it was able to detect. The null was not rejected, and that is all the apparatus can report. The person who objected to this is the next speaker.

Chapter Three: Rules of Behaviour

Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson published their paper in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in February 1933, two years before Fisher's book, and it is a very different kind of document. It opens with Bayes and with two Frenchmen. Joseph Bertrand had proposed testing a hypothesis such as whether a cluster of stars is a true system by working out how probable the observed arrangement would be by chance. If that probability were small, the hypothesis of chance would be considered probably false. Bertrand's own view was pessimistic: however small the probability, it gives no evidence of any other cause. Émile Borel thought that with a properly chosen feature of the data the method could work.

Neyman and Pearson say that the two men are answering different questions, and they agree with Bertrand on one of them. If an efficient test is supposed to tell you, of this particular cluster of stars, whether it is a system, then no test based on probability can by itself provide valuable evidence of the truth or falsehood of that hypothesis. That is a concession so large that it ends the old programme in the first paragraph. But there is another purpose a test might serve. Without hoping to know whether each separate hypothesis is true, they write, we may search for rules to govern our behaviour with regard to them, in following which we insure that, in the long run of experience, we shall not be too often wrong.

A rule of behaviour looks like this. Calculate a specified feature of the data. If it exceeds a threshold, reject H; if it does not, accept H. Such a rule says nothing about whether H is true in the particular case. What can be proved is that anyone who follows it will wrongly reject a true H not more than, say, once in a hundred times, while still rejecting a false one often enough. The target has moved from the truth of the hypothesis to the behaviour of the procedure, and the statement being made is about the long run of a method.

Once the aim is a rule that rarely errs, there are two ways to err and the paper says so plainly. If we reject the hypothesis we may reject it when it is true. If we accept it we may be accepting it when some alternative is true. These two sources of error can rarely be eliminated together, and in some cases it will be more important to avoid the first, in others the second. They reach for an old problem from Laplace, the number of votes a court of judges should require to convict a prisoner, and ask whether it is more serious to convict an innocent man or to acquit a guilty one. The answer depends on the consequences: whether the punishment is death or a fine, what the danger is of releasing criminals. From the point of view of mathematical theory, they write, all that can be done is to show how the risk of the errors may be controlled and minimised. How the balance is struck must be left to the investigator.

The theory does not choose the number. It supplies the calculus for trading one mistake against the other and hands the choice to the person who knows what the mistakes cost.

Then the engineering. The chance of rejecting the hypothesis when it is true can be made as small as you please, because it is only the probability, under that hypothesis, of landing in whatever region you chose for rejection, and a small enough region will do. Shrinking the region is no achievement, since it also shrinks your chance of ever rejecting anything. The work is in choosing, among all regions with the same small chance of a false rejection, the one that is most likely to reject when an alternative is true. They call it the best critical region, and the paper shows how to find it. That is why the alternative has to be written down. Without it there is nothing to be best against.

I should say what the paper does not contain. It does not use the phrase statistical power, which came later for the quantity they were computing, and it does not call anything a p-value. It is a technical paper for specialists, about fifty pages long, and its examples are tests of means and variances. Its philosophy occupies a few paragraphs at the front. Almost all of the later fame of the two-error scheme rests on those paragraphs and on Neyman's later writing, which I have not read and will not describe.

A worked case is needed for what this looks like in use, and Gigerenzer supplies one that I will use, with a correction. A manufacturer makes metal plates for medical instruments. A mean diameter of eight millimetres is what she wants, and ten is dangerous. From past experience she knows the spread of the diameters, so she can work out how the daily sample mean will behave under each of the two possibilities. Stopping production when the plates are fine is the lesser error, and shipping bad plates the greater. So she sets the false alarm rate at a tenth of one percent and tolerates a miss rate of ten percent, and from these two numbers and the spread she computes how many plates to measure each day. When the daily mean falls in the rejection region she stops the line, which does not mean she believes the plates are bad. She acts as if they were. Gigerenzer's text slips once, quoting the miss rate where the false alarm rate belongs.

The example needs what the tea did not. It needs two stated hypotheses, a cost for each error, and a sample size worked out before any plate is measured. It needs a decision at the end, and decisions are symmetric: accepting one hypothesis means rejecting the other. A result that falls short of the rejection region is not a failure to find anything. It is a decision, to keep the line running.

Chapter Four: Absurdly Academic

The two were never going to share a department for long. In Gigerenzer's telling, Neyman and Pearson worked at University College London, and Neyman moved to Berkeley when the tension with Fisher grew too heated. What the record I hold shows is the later Fisher answering in print.

Gigerenzer reports, from Fisher's 1955 paper in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society and his 1956 book Statistical Methods and Scientific Inference, neither of which I hold, that Fisher rejected the whole two-error apparatus. The 1956 book, in Gigerenzer's account, called it absurdly academic and said it came from the phantasy of circles rather remote from scientific research, and by the second phrase he meant mathematicians, specifically Neyman. In the 1955 paper Fisher called Neyman's position childish and said it was horrifying for the intellectual freedom of the west. The reasoning behind the insults, as Gigerenzer relays it, is a real argument. A scientist does not repeat the same experiment over and over under the same conditions, so a long-run error rate describes a situation scientists are never in. What the scientist has is one body of data and one hypothesis, and the thing to report is how far the data depart from it, say 0.02 and not merely less than 0.05. On Fisher's late position that figure is a property of the data, a relation between them and a theory. On Neyman and Pearson's, the error rate is a property of the test, fixed before any data exist. Fisher's scientist communicates information and does not make yes or no decisions.

The 1955 paper, according to Gigerenzer, went further and compared Neyman's view with Russian planning: research in pure science geared to technological performance, a five-year plan for the nation, and in America the importance of organised technology making it easy to confuse the process for drawing correct conclusions with the one aimed at speeding production or saving money. Gigerenzer notes that Neyman was born in Russia and had by then moved to America, and calls it unlikely to be an accident. That is Gigerenzer's inference, and it is a plausible one, but a reader should know where Fisher's text stops and where the commentary starts.

Now put Fisher against Fisher, because that is the part with consequences. In 1935 the standard was usual and convenient, a level at which experimenters ignore results. By 1956, by Gigerenzer's account, he thought a routine five percent was a sign of poor statistical sophistication, and wrote that no scientific worker has a fixed level of significance at which from year to year, and in all circumstances, he rejects hypotheses. He gives his mind to each particular case in the light of his evidence and his ideas. The book that first called it usual also carries the section added in 1960, the one that closed the last episode. There Fisher warns against calling tests of significance rules of action, which suit settings where an irreversible decision must be taken automatically, without thought. In such settings, he says, the cost of wrong decisions can be assessed in advance, as in industrial acceptance, but in pure research any such assessment can be no more than a pretence. A good deal of confusion, he adds, has come from formalising tests of significance in a logical framework different from the one for which they were developed.

Read with the 1933 paper open beside it, that section is a reply to a paper it never names. Neyman and Pearson leave the weighing of the two errors to the investigator. Fisher says that in pure research the weighing is a pretence. The person whose judgment their scheme relies on is the person he thinks should not be asked.

Which of them is right? I think the answer depends on what is being decided, and the textbooks hid that. Where an action follows, and the action is irreversible or expensive and has to be taken on a schedule, the Neyman and Pearson machinery is the correct one, because it is the only one that asks what each mistake will cost. The manufacturer who has to ship or stop does not have the luxury of reporting a degree of surprise. Where the output is a belief held provisionally in a literature, Fisher's reply has force: no one will run this experiment ten thousand times, and the exact strength of the evidence is better information than a verdict.

There is a hard point on each side that is not in the other's account. Fisher's scheme has no way to say what a negative result means. The 1935 book states that a second kind of error has no meaning in a simple test of significance, and the consequence is that a trial too small to detect anything can report, with the same dignity as a large one, that the null was not rejected. Neyman and Pearson's scheme, for its part, makes the whole procedure hinge on a pair of error costs that an investigator in practice cannot state. Ask a research team what they would pay, in the currency of harmed children, for a false positive versus a false negative, and you get silence or a number made up to fit a sample size. Both of these are the defects of the theories, as written, by their own authors. The hybrid inherits both of them and the benefits of neither.

Chapter Five: The Hybrid

Gigerenzer's argument is that the textbooks made a third thing out of the first two, and that it takes pieces from each in a way both would have refused. Set the three procedures side by side as he does.

Fisher's version, in his account of the late Fisher: set up a null hypothesis, which need not be zero. Report the exact level of significance, say 0.049 or 0.051, and do not use a conventional cut-off or talk about accepting and rejecting. Use the procedure only if you know very little about the problem. Neyman and Pearson: set up two hypotheses, and decide the two error rates and the sample size before the experiment, based on cost and benefit. If the data fall in the rejection region of one, accept the other, which means acting as if it were true and not believing it. Use it where you really have a choice between stated hypotheses and can weigh costs. The null ritual: set up one null hypothesis of no difference, say nothing about what you expect instead; use five percent as a convention and if it is passed accept your own hypothesis, reporting the result as p below 0.05, 0.01 or 0.001, whichever comes next; and always do this.

The ritual takes Fisher's single null, and drops his instruction to report the exact figure. It takes Neyman and Pearson's decision at the end, and drops the two stated hypotheses, the costs and the sample size calculation. It then does the one thing Gigerenzer says no statistician proposed, which is to apply the whole thing mechanically. If Fisher and Neyman and Pearson agreed on anything, he writes, it was that statistics should never be used mechanically.

He gives the researcher at the centre of this a name, Dr. Publish-Perish. The doctor knows he ought to set the significance level before the experiment, as Neyman and Pearson require. He fears that if he sets one percent the result will come in at one point one, so he picks five. Then the data come in at 0.004 and he regrets not having claimed a smaller level. So he waits, sees the data, rounds the exact value up to the next conventional level, and reports it at that level. The result, Gigerenzer says, is neither an error rate set in advance nor an exact level of significance. It is the product of an unconscious conflict, and the doctor feels vaguely guilty without knowing why.

The Freudian apparatus Gigerenzer builds on this, with Neyman and Pearson as the superego, Fisher as the ego and the Bayesian wish for the probability of a hypothesis as the id, I take to be a flourish, and he presents it as a hypothesis from earlier work of his own. The evidence that people hold false beliefs about the number is much firmer. He reports a quiz. A treatment is compared with a control, twenty subjects in each group, by a simple t-test, and the result is significant with p equal to 0.01. Six statements follow, each marked true or false. The null is absolutely disproved. The probability that the null is true has been found. Your own hypothesis is absolutely proved. The probability that your hypothesis is true can be deduced. Rejecting the null carries a known probability of a wrong decision. And a repeat of the experiment would be significant ninety-nine times in a hundred. All six are false, says Gigerenzer, and all err in the same direction, flattering the result.

The p-value is the probability of data this extreme, given that the null is true. It is not the probability of the null, given the data, and the six statements swap the two. The fifth sounds sensible. A rejection can only be a mistake if the null holds, so the chance of a mistaken rejection would be the chance of the null holding, which no test gives you.

Haller and Krauss put the six statements to people at six German universities: thirty teachers of statistics, thirty-nine other psychology staff, and forty-four students. Every student endorsed at least one false statement. So did ninety percent of the staff, and eighty percent of the people who taught the method. Statement five, the one about the probability of a wrong decision, was endorsed by about seventy percent of all three groups. Earlier, a study of British academic psychologists by Oakes had found eighty-six percent believing it. In another study, Israeli students were offered none of the statements is correct as an answer and had first read a classic article warning against these errors, and eighty-seven percent still picked one or more illusions.

The ritual is old. Gigerenzer dates its institutionalisation in psychology to the mid-1950s and shows how the textbooks wavered. He quotes the 1942 book by Guilford, which suggested that the hypothesis is probably correct if the result comes out one way, and a later textbook that, within three pages, described a significant result nine different ways, among them the probability that an observed difference is real, the odds of ninety-five in a hundred that it will hold up, and finally that all of these are different ways of saying the same thing. If the people writing the instructions cannot keep the meaning fixed, the student who reads them will, in Gigerenzer's phrase, blame his own lack of intelligence.

There are two more findings in the paper that matter beyond psychology, because they are about what the ritual does when it is not reined in. The first is a prediction by Paul Meehl from 1978. In observational settings with large samples, he argued, the null hypothesis of exactly no difference is almost never true, because some difference exists between any two natural groups. Given enough power, then, nearly everything is significant, and if you guess the direction of a difference at random you will be right about half the time. Niels Waller, according to Gigerenzer, tested this on the responses of a very large sample to a personality inventory. A computer picked an item, chose a random direction for the hypothesis, and checked whether the difference between men and women went that way and was significant. Forty-six percent of the random predictions were confirmed, often with very large test statistics. A hypothesis chosen by a coin passed the bar nearly half the time.

The second is about power, the quantity Fisher's scheme had no place for. Jacob Cohen reported in 1962 that the studies published in a major psychology journal had on average a fifty-fifty chance of detecting a medium-sized effect that was really there. Twenty-four years later, according to a check Gigerenzer and a colleague made on the same journal, only two of sixty-four researchers mentioned power at all, and average power had gone down, because the corrections for multiple tests shrink it. Had there been a medium effect, Gigerenzer notes, a coin toss would have found it as often as their elaborate experiments. In the years 2000 to 2002, among some two hundred and twenty empirical articles, nine computed power.

Goodhart's law, from earlier in the series, says a measure used as a target stops measuring. The 0.05 is a target with a deadline attached, and the researchers in these figures are doing the sensible thing for people judged on whether it is met. Gigerenzer's account of Geoffrey Loftus shows how little a single editor can do about it. Loftus edited the journal Memory and Cognition from 1994 to 1997 and asked authors to give figures with error bars instead of bare test statistics. While the previous editor served, fifty-three percent of articles relied on significance tests alone. Under Loftus it fell to thirty-two. Under his successor it rose again, to fifty-five percent in the year 2000.

Chapter Six: What the Case Against Gets Wrong

Gigerenzer's paper invites four objections.

The first is that his evidence is from psychology, and what I hold about medicine in this episode is nothing. He says the ritual spread into the social, medical and biological sciences, and cites economics, sociology and ecology as examples, but the surveys, the journal data and the textbook quotations are all psychology. A reader in clinical trials should not assume the same proportions. The shape of a registered trial, with a stated primary outcome, a sample size calculation and a fixed analysis plan, is very much closer to the Neyman and Pearson design than to Gigerenzer's Dr. Publish-Perish. A trial that computed its sample size from a stated effect, and that reports its primary outcome with a confidence interval, has already left the ritual behind on two of its three steps. I do not know how often that happens in the trials this show's listener reads, and nothing I hold says.

The second is that the paper's remedy fits the diagnosis badly. He writes that in scientific problems good descriptive statistics are necessary and mostly sufficient, and that attempts to give an optimal answer to the wrong question are a third kind of error, which John Tukey had urged us to avoid in favour of an approximate answer to the right one. That may be true for a laboratory psychologist, whose subjects are not sampled from any stated population. It is hard to say to the manufacturer with the metal plates, and harder to a data monitoring committee that has to stop or continue a trial. They need a rule, and a rule needs the two error rates. Gigerenzer's own Loftus story is the evidence against him here. When an editor removed the test and asked for the figure with its error bars, most authors insisted on their p-values anyway, and the editor found that many of them confused standard errors with standard deviations. Taking the ritual away does not by itself produce statistical thinking.

The third is the title claim: that the hybrid is a mess. Gigerenzer's point is that it is logically incoherent, and I accept it as far as the interpretation goes, since reading an exact p-value as an error rate does mix the two meanings. But the practices of a field can be incoherent in the textbook and still useful in the lab, and the numbers above are not about usefulness. A statistician such as Erich Lehmann is remembered, as I recall, for arguing that the two theories converged more than the quarrel suggests and that the usual practice is a reasonable compromise between them. I am passing on that from memory and have no text of his in hand, so weigh it as a pointer and not a finding. What would be needed to decide the matter is a study of whether results reported under the hybrid are less often true than results under either pure form. Nothing in the paper measures that. What it measures is that people misunderstand the number, and a misunderstood number can still discriminate.

The fourth is the plain point about who is to blame. The story of the textbook author passing the blame to colleagues, a university and a publisher is told as a dodge. It is also a description. A published statement about a hypothesis is read by an editor, and Gigerenzer's own fifty-three, thirty-two and fifty-five percent are a record of how much one editor can move a field. If the incentive is a deadline and a threshold, the hybrid is what that incentive grows.

Chapter Seven: What the Profession Said in 2016

In March 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a statement on p-values, the first such statement from its board, by its president's account. I hold the press release that announced it and not the statement itself, and I will say so wherever it matters. Its executive director, Ron Wasserstein, is quoted saying that the p-value was never intended to be a substitute for scientific reasoning, and that the aim was to steer research into a post p less than 0.05 era. The association's president, Jessica Utts, said the number had become a gatekeeper for whether work is publishable, at least in some fields, with consequences she named: the file drawer, in which significant studies are published and others never seen, and practices called p-hacking and data dredging.

The release lists six principles. A p-value can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model. It does not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or that the data were produced by chance alone. Conclusions and decisions should not rest only on whether it passes a threshold. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency. It does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result. And by itself it is a poor measure of evidence about a model or a hypothesis.

Lay these over the argument so far and the statement is a map of the quarrel. The first principle is Fisher's: incompatibility with a stated model, reported as a degree. The second is the removal of the six false statements. The third and fifth rebuke the ritual's threshold and Meehl's problem, since a vast sample makes a tiny effect significant. Principle four, full reporting, answers a story Feynman told. A psychologist ran rats through a T-maze, they failed to behave as predicted, and he noticed that they seemed to alternate left and right. He asked Feynman, then a graduate student, to calculate the probability of that pattern by chance, to see whether it fell below one in twenty. Feynman, as Gigerenzer quotes him, replied that it probably did, but that it did not count, because the peculiarity had been found in the data and then selected. To test the idea, the psychologist would have to run another experiment. He did, Feynman reported, and it did not work. Whatever was found by looking has to be tested by looking again.

What the release does not do is choose a replacement. It says that statisticians often supplement or replace p-values with estimation, intervals, Bayesian methods, likelihood ratios, decision-theoretic modelling and false discovery rates, and then leaves the choice open. The quarrel of 1933 to 1956 therefore ends as a list. I find that honest, since the dispute was never settled, and the profession could not have declared a winner. It also means the statement does not tell the writer of a protocol what sentence to type.

Chapter Eight: The Sentence With the Number In It

So take the sentence. A trial's analysis plan says that the primary outcome will be compared between arms by a test at the two-sided five percent level. What has that committed the trial to?

Under Neyman and Pearson it commits the trial to a good deal more than the sentence says. There must be an alternative, a stated size of effect, and the sample size must have been worked out from that effect, the chosen error rate and a stated power, before a single participant is enrolled. Here is the arithmetic for a constructed case, with made-up numbers. A trial compares recovery rates, seventy percent in the control arm and a hoped-for eighty percent in the treatment arm. At a five percent two-sided level with eighty percent power, each arm needs about two hundred and ninety children. The ten-point difference, the five percent and the power are one design, and none of them means anything apart from the others.

The decision that follows is also a Neyman and Pearson feature. If the trial's analysis crosses the line, the treatment is declared effective, and if it does not, the trial declares no effect found. The second statement is the one that needs power: with two hundred and ninety per arm, a ten-point difference would have been missed one time in five. A negative result from an adequately powered test is a statement about what the trial could have seen. Fisher's scheme had no sentence for it.

Now suppose the trial finishes and finds seventy-seven percent recovery against seventy, a seven-point difference, with a p-value of about 0.06. Three readings are available, and they disagree. The null ritual says: not significant, no effect. Neyman and Pearson say: the decision was to continue to treat the arms as equivalent, and the long-run error rates are as designed, but it is also the case that the trial was powered for ten points and found seven, and nobody planned how to act on that. Fisher, in his late form, says: report 0.06, and an interval, and let the reader judge how incompatible the data are with no difference. A difference of seven points, with an interval running from a little below zero to about fourteen, is a different piece of information from the words not significant. The ASA principles back Fisher's reading and, I think, Neyman and Pearson's too. The ritual's reading is what the hybrid yields, and what it yields in practice is that a seven-point effect in children's recovery gets filed as nothing.

So the instruction for a protocol writer is to decide, for each place the number appears, which of the two theories it belongs to, and to write the sentence that way. If the number is an error rate, the plan places it with the effect size, the power and the sample size, and the plan says what decision follows each outcome. If it is a yardstick for reading the data, it comes with an instruction to report the exact value and an interval and to describe the result as a degree of incompatibility with the null, and the plan avoids the word reject. A plan may carry both a decision and an exact value, but each should be labelled with its job, so that the next reader does not do what Dr. Publish-Perish does. Secondary outcomes need the same labels. Twelve of them, each tested at five percent, do not make a set whose long-run error rate is five percent, and the Neyman and Pearson sentence about controlling the two sources of error applies to the whole family. A plan that claims no family-wise rate should say that those results are reports of incompatibility and not decisions.

I would give the late Fisher one point and the 1933 paper another. Fisher is right that a study is a single event, and that the evidence it holds is better reported than ruled upon. Neyman and Pearson are right that anyone who must act owes the people affected a stated cost for each error, and a sample size that was chosen to make one of them small. The trialist is in both positions in one week. In March the protocol commits, as a rule of behaviour, to a number. In October the paper describes, as a reading of one set of data, what happened. Written in the language of March, the October sentence reports as a verdict what was a measurement.
