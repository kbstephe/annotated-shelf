Streptomycin Trial (Bradford Hill and the MRC): sealed envelopes and a scarce drug

In 1948 the Medical Research Council reported what is usually called the first randomised trial of a drug in tuberculosis. This episode reads the paper itself: the envelopes, the ethics of scarcity, the blinded x-rays, and the resistance finding that outran the headline.

22 October 2026

Chapter One: Fourteen and Four

Of fifty-two young people with acute tuberculosis in both lungs, who were put to bed in a hospital and given nothing but rest, fourteen were dead within six months. Of fifty-five similar patients, who had the same bed and the same rest and also four injections a day of a new drug, four were dead. The authors give the chance of a gap that large arising by luck as less than one in a hundred. Beyond the deaths, they looked at the x-rays. Twenty-eight of the treated patients had considerably improved lungs at six months. Among the controls, four did. For that comparison the paper puts the chance of such a gap at less than one in a million.

That is the Medical Research Council's report on streptomycin in pulmonary tuberculosis, published in the British Medical Journal on the thirtieth of October 1948. I read it as text, in full, and the figures I give you are from that text. It is usually described as the first randomised controlled trial of a drug, and as the origin of the sealed envelope. Both descriptions are roughly right, and neither says what the paper is like to read. What is on the page is a document by a committee of fifteen people, most of whose energy went into worry: about what kind of patient to admit, about who might see which result, about a drug that nobody had enough of.

The question I want to put to it is one the paper asks in its introduction. How did anyone, in 1946, know that a treatment for tuberculosis worked? The disease has a habit that makes the answer hard. Its course is so variable and unpredictable, the authors write, that improvement or cure after a new drug in a few cases cannot be accepted as proof the drug did anything. Some people with tuberculosis got better on bed-rest alone, some got worse on anything, and nobody could tell in advance which. A drug given to a handful of patients, some of whom recover, leaves the physician in front of the old Latin dilemma, the journal's editorial in the same issue calls it: after this, therefore because of this, or the body's own healing power.

The introduction gives an example of how long that dilemma can last. Gold treatment for tuberculosis, the authors say, had its exaggerated claims persist for fifteen years. In the whole literature they found one properly controlled trial of gold, from 1931, by Amberson, McMahon and Pinner, which reported negative results. It was also the only such report in tuberculosis that they could find. By 1946, then, the trial of a tuberculosis drug against a concurrent comparison group was almost a thing nobody had done. And a drug had arrived that looked, in test tubes and in guinea pigs, strikingly better than anything before it. Streptomycin had been discovered two years earlier by Waksman and his colleagues. Early American clinical reports were encouraging and inconclusive.

So this episode follows the trial in the order its problems arose. Who goes in. How the lottery runs. Why a lottery was defensible when a physician's first duty is to the patient in front of her. What the readers of the x-rays were allowed to know. And then the part of the paper that the headline leaves out, in which the drug works and then, in most of the patients who got it, stops working. It is a short trial by today's standards, a hundred and seven people over six months, and what it shows can be held in the head.

Chapter Two: Who Goes In

The first design decision in the paper was about the patients, and it was a decision to throw most of them away. A committee of the Medical Research Council sat down in September 1946 and decided, in the report's words, that a part of the small supply of streptomycin allocated to it should be spent on a rigorously planned investigation with concurrent controls. Then the committee had to say whose lungs to put the drug in.

They wrote a definition. Acute progressive bilateral pulmonary tuberculosis of presumably recent origin, bacteriologically proved, unsuitable for collapse therapy, age fifteen to twenty-five, later extended to thirty. Each clause is doing a job. Bilateral and acute and recent, because old disease with thick-walled cavities would not respond to any drug and would add noise. Unsuitable for collapse therapy, because that was the other treatment then in use, in which a surgeon or physician deliberately deflated part of a lung to rest it, and a patient who might have it was a patient whose outcome would depend on a second variable. Bacteriologically proved, so that every case had the disease. A narrow age band, because the total could not be large. The authors say plainly why the narrowness mattered: no two patients have the same form of the disease, and the committee wanted to remove as many of the obvious differences as it could.

In today's vocabulary this is the eligibility criteria, and the reason it comes first is that it fixes the denominator. Everything the paper later says is about this population. It is not about tuberculosis in general, and the discussion section is firm about that. The trial, they write, was designed to give an affirmative or negative answer to the question whether streptomycin is of value in pulmonary tuberculosis. It was not designed to find in what type of the disease the drug could be effective, or the best dose, or the best length or rhythm of treatment. A trial that tried to answer those too would, in the editorial's phrase, give doubtful answers to many points and no decisive answer to any.

The definition also did something quieter, which was to make the comparison group defensible. Patients of this type had one accepted treatment, which was bed-rest. The paper says the selection of this disease constituted full justification for a parallel series treated only by bed-rest. No competing therapy was being withheld. Whether that settles the ethics is the question for Chapter Four, but you can see the argument being assembled in the design.

Then came the part nobody had planned for. At first the impression was that cases of this type were seen often. In fact, such cases are not common, the report says. After three months it was clear that London and Middlesex could not supply enough, and the Council went to Wales, Scotland and Leeds, opening centres at Sully, Bangour and Killingbeck, and a further one in London at the Northern Hospital. Letters went out through the tuberculosis departments of the councils to officers and medical superintendents, with a request for particulars and x-ray films of possible cases. Visits were paid to clinics, to show by representative films the type of case sought and to explain what a controlled trial was. A selection panel looked at each film. When a patient was accepted, the local authority was asked to admit him to a streptomycin centre, and in spite of long waiting lists these patients were given complete priority; most were in within a week.

By September 1947, a hundred and nine patients had been accepted, and no more were admitted. Two died within the preliminary observation week and are left out of the analysis. That leaves a hundred and seven: fifty-five allocated to streptomycin, which the paper calls S cases, and fifty-two to bed-rest alone, the C cases.

Those two early deaths mark the first place the paper departs from what a modern analyst would do. The report does not say which arm they were in. They were admitted to the centre after the envelope was opened, since the observation week took place at the centre, and so they had already been allocated. A modern protocol would count them in the group they were assigned to, whether or not they lived long enough to be treated. With two deaths out of a hundred and nine, and a difference of ten deaths between arms, the choice is unlikely to alter the verdict. I mention it because the habit of dropping people who die early is the habit that later trial methodology was built to stop, and because the paper is silent on which side lost them.

Chapter Three: The Envelope

Now the allocation, the sentence people quote. I will read it from the paper in my own words, staying close. Whether a patient got streptomycin and bed-rest, or bed-rest alone, was settled by a statistical series based on random sampling numbers, drawn up for each sex at each centre by Professor Bradford Hill. The details of the series were unknown to any of the investigators or to the co-ordinator, and were held in sealed envelopes bearing on the outside only the name of the hospital and a number. After the panel accepted a patient, and before admission to the centre, the appropriate numbered envelope was opened at the central office. The card inside said S or C, and that information was then passed on to the medical officer of the centre.

There are two ideas folded into that paragraph, and the second is the one that mattered more at the time. The first is randomness. The sequence came from tables of random sampling numbers, drawn up separately for men and for women at each of the seven hospitals, so that each sex at each site was its own little lottery. That is a blocked design in the sense of the Fisher episodes: the sexes and the centres were not left to chance to balance, and within each cell the assignment was random. The numbers in the paper's first table show how well it worked. Thirty of the treated patients, fifty-four percent, were in poor general condition at the start, against twenty-four of the controls, forty-six percent. Twenty-four treated patients and nineteen controls had evening temperatures of a hundred and one degrees or more. Thirty-six treated and twenty-nine control had a sedimentation rate over fifty. The sexes split evenly too. The report says that random distribution has equalised the groups, and adds that if anything there were more severe cases in the streptomycin group. That is the useful way for it to have gone wrong, if it was going to.

Fisher, whom we met two episodes ago counting the seventy ways eight cups of tea could be arranged, would have recognised the logic. Randomisation was not there to make the groups equal. It was there so that whatever inequalities arose, the probability statements at the end would still be valid. In this trial the inequalities went against the drug, and the verdict survived.

The second idea is concealment, and it is a different thing. The BMJ's editorial in that issue explains why the committee did not simply alternate: take the first patient to the drug, the second to bed-rest, and so on. If alternate patients had been taken, the editorial says, the selection panel might conceivably have been influenced in accepting or rejecting a patient by knowing beforehand which group he was to be in. Imagine the panel looking at a film on the table and knowing that the next place in the series is the streptomycin place. A doctor who thought the drug might save a life, with an ambiguous film in front of him, would be tempted to accept it if the drug was due and to call it borderline if bed-rest was. Nobody has to be dishonest for that to happen. It is a doctor doing what doctors are trained to do.

The sealed envelope removes the reason. The panel decides with the allocation unknown, and the allocation is revealed only after the decision is made and cannot be reversed. The editorial praises exactly this, calling it an ingenious system, and says it removed personal responsibility from the clinician and possible bias in his process of choosing patients. That line, personal responsibility removed, carries a good deal of weight in the argument of the next chapter, because it is part of how the committee could square the lottery with the clinician's duty.

The paper also notes something that is easy to miss: the secret had to be kept. The authors write that it was important for the success of the trial that the details of the control scheme remain confidential, and that it was a matter of great credit to the many doctors concerned that the information was not made public throughout the fifteen months. A trial in which the local tuberculosis officers learned the sequence, or even the principle, would have found ways to send their favourite cases to the centres on days when the envelope series was in the right place. Concealment is a social achievement as much as a technical one, and the paper says so in a sentence of thanks.

The report does not use the word randomised in its summary of method. It says patients were assigned to one or the other group by random selection, and the claim rests on the sealed envelopes, the numbers and the central office. The word that stuck to the trial afterwards was the one the tables suggested.

Chapter Four: The Scarcity Argument

The question that has to be asked of this design is whether it was right to do it. Half of the patients in a deadly illness were assigned, by a number in an envelope, to not receive a drug that the earlier reports called strikingly better than anything before. The control arm lost fourteen of fifty-two. Ten more of them died than would have, on the treated arm's rate. How can a profession whose ethic is the individual patient run that experiment?

The paper's answer, and the editorial's, is scarcity. The streptomycin in the country was a small supply, allocated to the Medical Research Council for research, and the introduction says that all of it was in any case being used: this trial took some, and the rest went to two rapidly fatal forms of the disease, miliary and meningeal tuberculosis. The editorial is the clearest. In developing the trial, the Committee was clearly relieved of this particular moral responsibility, it says. It had allocated much of its limited streptomycin to the two fatal forms, it had a quite insufficient supply to treat all possible cases of pulmonary tuberculosis, and in that situation it rightly set about planning a rigorously controlled investigation.

Follow the argument through, because it is stronger than it first sounds in one way and weaker in another. It is stronger because it is a statement about how a shortage can be divided. If there is enough drug for fifty-five and there are a hundred and seven eligible patients, then some will go without. Which ones? The choices are first come, first served, or the doctor's judgement, or the family's connections, or a lottery. A lottery is, on any reading, the fairest of these. Seen that way, the sealed envelope was a fair way of deciding who got the scarce drug, and the control arm was simply the group who lost it. They would have lost it with or without the trial. In one respect they did better than before, and the paper records it: the controls were admitted to the centres with complete priority, over the long waiting lists, and were then treated as patients had been in the past.

It is weaker because scarcity justifies a lottery and does not by itself justify a comparison. A lottery for the drug would have been fair if the committee had given it to fifty-five patients and never looked at the other fifty-two. The trial adds the look. It adds the monthly forms, the x-rays read by three people, the weighing of the controls against the treated. That addition is what turns the lottery into knowledge, and the knowledge is what the next thousand patients were owed. The argument has to say that the controls, who agreed to nothing and were informed of nothing, were asked to carry a cost for people they would never meet.

And the controls were told nothing. The report is plain about this. Patients were not told before admission that they were to get special treatment, and the C patients did not know, throughout their stay, that they were control patients in a special study. The paper records no consent from anyone. The treated patients were not told beforehand either, and the paper does not say what they were told once the injections began. The controls were put to bed, as they would have been before, and were not informed that the bed was one half of an experiment. In 1948 the norm of consent for research on patients did not exist in anything like its present form, and I would be inventing a controversy if I said the paper's authors were evasive about it. They were not. They wrote it in the methods section as a feature: it was important for the success of the trial. The reader today has to hold two thoughts. One is that the lottery was a defensible way to divide a shortage. The other is that a trial run this way now would be stopped by any ethics committee in the world, and for a reason the scarcity argument does not touch.

I would also put the editorial's boldest sentence alongside its quietest. The bold one says that the committee was relieved of the moral responsibility of the controlled trial by the scarcity of the drug. That is a claim that scarcity can discharge a duty, which is a position and not an observation. The quiet one, a few lines earlier, says that the application of controlled trials was gaining ground before the war, subject always to the fundamental ethical problem inherent in using human beings as the subject of experiment. The editorial writer knew the ethical problem was real. The streptomycin trial was a case where it could be argued to be smaller than usual, and the writer offered the design as a model all the same, including for cases where no such argument was available. The conditions that make the argument work, a drug in short supply and an untreated alternative that was the standard of care, are the ones under which trialists still make the case today. Where those conditions fail, as in a drug that is plentiful and promising, the lottery loses its excuse and a different justification, genuine uncertainty among experts, has to carry the design. That is later ethics, and the paper does not contain it.

Chapter Five: What the Readers Did Not Know

The worst threat to a comparison of this kind, once the groups are alike at the start, is a judgement made afterwards by someone who knows which group a patient was in. The treated patients had four injections a day for months; no one at the bedside could be unaware. Neither could the clinicians keep the allocation from themselves. So the committee protected what it could protect, and it chose the x-ray.

The paper says the analysis rests on the radiological picture, which in the authors' opinion was the weightiest single factor, and it adds that in the great majority of cases clinical and radiological changes followed similar trends. The films were read by a panel of two radiologists and a clinician. Each read independently, and none knew whether a film was from a C or an S case. One of the radiologists had been attached to a centre in the trial. The other two had no connection to it. They were in fair agreement, and at a final session they met to discuss the films on which they had differed, and the report says agreement was reached without difficulty on all films. The results in the main tables are the agreed results.

I would give this part of the design full marks, with one caution. It is the first thing a modern reader should look for, and it is there. Whoever decided who improved did not know who had the drug. The patient's temperature chart or sense of well-being, which a clinician who knew the arm might have read generously, is a secondary outcome here, and the paper flags it as such: general condition is based on the clinician's general impression and the patient's feeling of well- or ill-being, and is mentioned only briefly.

The caution is that the choice of x-ray as the primary yardstick is described as the authors' opinion, in a sentence in the results. The paper does not say it was fixed before the trial began, and I cannot tell from the text whether it was. The result is so large on every measure the paper reports, mortality included, that it would not matter here. In a trial with a smaller effect it would matter a great deal, and in the episodes ahead on analysis plans the question of who chose the outcome, and when, becomes the whole subject.

The results themselves are given in the paper with probabilities, not in verdict words, and the manner is plain. Four deaths of fifty-five against fourteen of fifty-two: a chance of less than a hundredth. Twenty-eight improved lungs against four: under a millionth. The paper does use the phrase statistically significant, and it attaches a probability each time. There is no sentence that reads like the null ritual of last episode, in which a result is declared to have cleared five percent and the matter is considered closed. The authors report the size of the surprise and let the reader see it.

Chapter Six: The Drug Works and Then Stops

Here the paper does something a short account of the trial leaves out. After a good start, many of the treated got worse again, and the authors worked out why.

Look first at the shape of the benefit. Improvement in the treated patients was greatest in the first three months. After that, in the summary's words, many S cases began to deteriorate, and the gap in general condition narrowed. By clinical impression forty of the fifty-five treated, seventy-three percent, were better at four months, against twenty-six of the fifty-two controls. At six months the counts were thirty-three, sixty percent, and twenty-four, forty-six percent. The drug had been stopped, for most patients, at the four-month mark, a change the committee made in July 1947 on advice from American observers and its own impression that the maximum effect came within three or four months. A plan for six months of treatment was shortened midway. Forty-one patients got exactly four.

The decline that began while the drug was still being given needs another explanation, and the laboratory work supplies one. The pathologists tested the tubercle bacilli from each patient's sputum before treatment and during it. Before treatment, every strain was as sensitive to streptomycin as the standard laboratory strain. During treatment, in thirty-five of the forty-one patients for whom there was data, the bacilli became resistant to more than ten times the original concentration. In thirteen of those thirty-five, resistance was more than two thousand times. The paper gives a case: a man whose lungs looked considerably better after two months, and then in the third month the temperature rose again, he began to lose weight, the films showed extension and further cavities, and a spontaneous pneumothorax followed in the sixth month. From the second month on, the bacilli in his sputum were thousands of times less sensitive than the ones that had been there before. The drug had selected the survivors.

Then the authors do the arithmetic that matters, and do it with care. Of the six patients whose bacilli stayed sensitive, never more than ten times resistant, all had improved at the end, and none had a setback along the way. Among the twenty-two whose resistance reached between ten and a thousand times, five had deteriorated, one fatally. Among the thirteen with the highest resistance, eight had, and three of the eight died. The paper calls the differences among those three groups statistically significant. It is a graded effect: more resistance, worse outcome.

The authors next ask whether the correlation could be a confound. Perhaps resistance grows fastest in the sickest patients, who would do worst anyway. Then resistance would be a marker and not a cause. They check. Of the thirteen with very high resistance, six had been acutely ill at admission, with high fever and large cavities, against nine of twenty-eight among the rest. The difference is small. They divide the patients again by severity and find the trend toward worse outcomes with more resistance in both groups, though steeper among the very ill. They conclude that it remains difficult to assess the relative importance and the interdependence of the two factors, clinical condition at the start and the degree of drug resistance developed during treatment.

That sentence marks the limit of the second half of the paper, and the reason is Fisher's. In Chapter Three the envelope produced two arms alike in everything but the drug. The contrast between S and C is the only comparison in the trial that randomisation protects. When the authors then sort the treated patients by how resistant their bacilli became, they are sorting people by something that happened to them after allocation, and the lottery does not stand behind that grouping. The patients did not draw their level of resistance from an envelope. The comparison of the six without resistance and the thirteen with a great deal is an observational study nested inside an experiment, and its authors treated it as one. That is the discipline I would ask of any paper, and the one the series keeps returning to: say which comparison the randomisation protected, and label the rest as what they are.

The authors could not tell whether resistance caused the setbacks or merely accompanied them. They said the probable explanation was that the drug had selected resistant organisms in tissues with poor defences. They also did not wait for the answer to draw practical conclusions. The method of testing sensitivity was too slow to guide treatment at one or two months. Until the problem was solved, they wrote, it was fair to assume that after two to three months of streptomycin in open pulmonary tuberculosis, further treatment or a repeat course later was unlikely to be effective. They worried about public health: patients with unsuitable lesions, treated ineffectively, could spread resistant organisms, with cases of miliary or meningeal tuberculosis that would not respond. And they said organised investigations were needed to determine whether the emergence of resistant strains could be prevented by combining streptomycin with another drug, or by a special rhythm of treatment. Combination therapy is now how tuberculosis is treated, and the clinical trials that established it followed within a few years, as I recall, though I did not read those and cannot give you their figures.

The vestibular findings complete the picture of a drug with a short useful life and a price. Thirty-six of the fifty-five treated patients had giddiness, usually from the fourth or fifth week of treatment. Nystagmus and blurred vision followed in some. Several patients were left with unsteadiness that eased with visual compensation but remained a handicap in the dark. The authors worry in print that many may carry a disability that shows only at bad moments, on stairs in the dark or in a crowded street. The dose was two grams a day; American reports at the time suggested one gram reduced the toxicity, but not by enough to make it negligible. No patient had treatment stopped on account of it. The paper reports the toxicity in a few paragraphs and keeps it out of the main comparison. Fourteen against four counts deaths, and the x-ray counts lungs. Neither count includes what it cost a patient who lived and could no longer be trusted on a dark staircase.

Chapter Seven: The Case Against the Paper

The criticisms I would make are mostly of what the paper allowed itself, and they can be stated hard.

The first is size and scope. A hundred and seven patients, of one kind, with a six-month window. The paper is clear that the answer applies to acute bilateral disease in people up to thirty who were not suitable for collapse. It concludes that streptomycin probably has a place in rapidly advancing disease where immediate collapse would be dangerous, may be given in acute spreads after surgery, and has little place in the common chronic fibro-caseous forms. These conclusions, it says, are of necessity lacking in precision. A reader who takes the headline, streptomycin cures tuberculosis, away from the paper is reading a different document.

The second is the protocol that moved during the trial. The treatment period was cut from six months to four in July 1947, while patients were still being admitted, and while the assignments up to then had been made under the six-month plan. Patients were also allowed collapse therapy when their course changed so that it became indispensable, and clinicians used it in the control group more often early on: five of the fifty-two controls received it in the first four months and none of the treated, and eleven of the controls and eleven of the treated received it by the end of six. The paper reports all of this, and its authors judged that in most of those cases the course of the disease was not altered. I would still want the analysis to say how the result changes if those patients are counted another way. It does not, and the effect is large enough that I doubt it would matter, but it is a thing a reader must trust and cannot check.

The third is the one-year addendum, which the authors added before going to press. They collected the condition of each patient a year after admission, from the clinicians' general assessment, without the radiological panel. Of the controls, twenty-four of fifty-two were dead by a year, forty-six percent. The corresponding figure for the treated arm is lost in the scan I read, where the row is garbled, though its other cells leave room for about a fifth of the group. The authors say the difference in mortality between groups was statistically significant. The addendum is not comparable with the six-month tables, as they say themselves. I mention it as the weakest page in a paper that is otherwise careful about what it can say.

The fourth concerns the patients, and I have said it. They were not told. In a trial whose defence rested on scarcity, the people who bore the cost were not asked.

As for reception, the paper's own journal was admiring on the day. The BMJ's editorial in the same issue said the Council's trial would serve as a model for future investigations. The editorial went on to say that the plan was worthy of careful study quite apart from the results to which it led. That is the verdict the trial has since received, and I think it is deserved. From memory, and without the sources in front of me, I know that later historians of trials have found earlier studies that allocated patients by alternation or by some random device, and that the paper's own claim is modest: it says only that the trial was the first controlled investigation of its kind to be reported. Whether it was the first of anything beyond its kind is a question for people who count, and it is less interesting than what it did. It put a drug against a comparison group chosen by a method no clinician could steer, assessed the lungs without knowing the arm, and then reported the bad news along with the good.

Chapter Eight: The Allocation Paragraph

Take the sentence on the sealed envelopes and ask what it would take for it to be true of a trial today. The envelope was opened at a central office, after the decision to accept the patient, and before the patient reached the clinic. The people who decided on eligibility did not hold the sequence and could not guess the next place. The sequence was set by someone outside the study, and it was blocked by sex and centre. Every clause of that is a control on a way of cheating that nobody intends.

The trialist who writes protocols has the same paragraph to write every time. The test I would apply to it is a small one. At the moment someone decides that a particular person is eligible and enrols her, could that person know, or guess, what she will be assigned? If yes, the randomisation has been made worthless at its first step, however good the random numbers. In an individually randomised trial the usual repair is the envelope's modern descendant, a central system that releases the assignment after enrolment is locked. In a cluster trial the problem moves. The clusters can be randomised in a single draw before anyone is recruited, which looks safe, but then the people who recruit participants inside each cluster know the arm, and a recruiter who knows can choose whom to ask. The unit that was randomised and the unit that was enrolled are different units, and the concealment has to be argued for the second.

The second lesson is the one from Chapter Six, and it applies to any protocol. The result of a trial has two kinds of sentence in it. Some are about the contrast the lottery protected: treated against not treated, as allocated. Everything else, who responded, who developed resistance, who had the best outcome among the treated, is an observation made on people sorted by something that happened after the draw. Both kinds belong in a paper. The Medical Research Council's authors wrote both and marked the border. A table of response by baseline severity or by adherence or by some measured intermediate in the treated arm should be introduced in the sentence that makes the claim, as the committee did, as a finding of the second kind.

A trial answers the question it was built to answer. The Medical Research Council's trial, by looking at the bacilli as well as the lungs, found the next question, and said so in the discussion: whether a second drug, or a different rhythm of the first, could stop the resistance. I believe the trials that followed went after that question, though I did not read them.
