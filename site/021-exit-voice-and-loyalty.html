Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: What to Do When a Place You Belong to Gets Worse

Albert Hirschman's 1970 book as the first of four antidotes to the shelf's cynical season – the Nigerian railway, the lazy monopoly, loyalty as a brake, the East German test that broke his own theory, and what a ward loses when its best nurses leave without a word.

27 August 2026

Chapter One: A Change of Plan, and a Question

Last time I promised Notes on Nationalism, and it is coming, but not yet. Shooting an Elephant was the darkest half hour this feed has produced, and it closed a month on the machinery of decline: Jackall's managers arranging blame, Davies's sinks, Whyte's organization man, Parkinson, Peter, Graeber's feudal retinues, Scott's high modernists levelling villages, Orwell's soft snow and his rifle. Read one after another, those books teach a lesson none of them intended, which is that nothing can be done, and that lesson is false. Every one of those authors got up in the morning and did something. So the next four episodes are the shelf's antidote season: four books about what a person can do inside an institution, on the assumption that the person is you, and the institution is one you cannot simply leave. The first is Albert Hirschman's Exit, Voice, and Loyalty, from nineteen seventy, and it begins with a question so plain that it took an economist of unusual character to ask it.

The question is this. Something you belong to, a firm, a clinic, a party, a church, a country, has started to get worse. Not collapsed; slipped. The product is a little shoddier, the meetings a little emptier, the waiting list a little longer. What do you do? Hirschman's answer is that there are two things you can do and only two. You can leave, which he calls exit. Or you can stay and complain, which he calls voice. Everything in the book follows from the observation that these two responses are not just different for you; they are different for the institution, because one of them carries information and the other one carries a customer out the door.

Chapter Two: The Man Who Asked

Hirschman is the kind of author who makes the biography compulsory, because the book is the biography with the names removed. He was born Otto Albert Hirschmann in Berlin in nineteen fifteen, left Germany at eighteen, a few weeks after Hitler came to power, studied in Paris and London and Trieste, fought for the Republic in Spain, and then, in nineteen forty, did the thing that his biographer Jeremy Adelman puts at the centre of the life. In Marseille he became the operations man for Varian Fry's rescue committee, the American who smuggled some two thousand refugees out of Vichy France, among them Chagall, Arendt, and Marc Bloch's manuscripts. Fry called him Beamish, for the grin. Hirschman forged documents, changed money on the black market, and walked people over the Pyrenees. When the Vichy police came for him he walked over the mountains himself.

Hold that man in mind, because he is the author of a book about leaving. He knew what exit costs and what it saves, in the only currency that counts, and he knew that voice inside a regime that has decided to kill you is a death sentence with extra steps. Then he spent the fifties as an economic adviser in Colombia, where he learned to distrust the grand plan, and wrote The Strategy of Economic Development, which argues that the way to develop a poor country is not to balance everything at once but to create imbalances that force the next step. He would go on to write The Passions and the Interests, on how commerce was once sold as a civilising force, and The Rhetoric of Reaction, which catalogues the three arguments every conservative uses against every reform: it will backfire, it will change nothing, it will endanger what we already have. That book is a machine for hearing your own excuses, and it belongs on this shelf on its own.

Exit, Voice, and Loyalty came in between, and the story of how it started is in the first chapter. In the sixties Hirschman was looking at the Nigerian railways, which were bad, and getting worse, in a country where they mattered, and he wanted to know why nobody was fixing them. The standard answer was inefficiency and corruption. His answer was trucks.

Chapter Three: The Railway and the Trucks

Here is the mechanism, run all the way through, because it is the book. The Nigerian railway carried freight, and it carried it badly. In most theories of the market, that is a self-correcting condition. Customers desert the bad supplier, who then either improves or dies; either way the problem is solved. Nigeria had a road haulage industry, so the customers deserted. Peanut shippers and cotton shippers moved their freight to lorries. And the railway did not improve and did not die. It got worse, and it went on getting worse, for years.

Hirschman's explanation begins with a question about who left. The customers who moved to the trucks first were the ones who cared most about delivery times, the ones who had been complaining loudest, the ones with the alternative and the motive to use it. Those were the railway's most useful customers, and their value had nothing to do with the freight charges; they were the ones who would have kicked. Once they were gone, the railway was left with the shippers who did not much mind, or could not move, and the pressure that might have driven a repair went out onto the road with the freight. The railway was a public enterprise, so it did not go bankrupt either; the state covered the deficit. Exit had drained the voice and the treasury had cancelled the death, and in that condition an organisation can decline indefinitely, with a kind of peace.

There is a refinement in the book that I think is its most practical page, and it concerns who leaves first. Hirschman distinguishes customers by how much they care about quality as against price. When a product deteriorates, the quality-sensitive customers, the ones he calls connoisseurs, feel it first and leave first, because for them the drop in quality is a large price rise. The price-sensitive customers barely register it. So the order of exit is fixed by the structure of the customers' preferences, and it is the worst possible order for the organisation: it loses its most discriminating members at the first sign of trouble and keeps the ones who cannot tell. The department losing its best registrars in the year the teaching went bad is not suffering bad luck. It is running Hirschman's sequence on schedule.

This is the argument's first turn, and I want to state it the way Hirschman does, because it is a claim against his own discipline. Economics had treated exit as the whole of the story. Competition works because people leave. Voice, the complaint, the letter, the delegation to the manager, the vote, was something political scientists talked about and economists treated as noise. Hirschman's claim is that these are two mechanisms of recovery, that they interact, and that in a large class of cases the presence of an easy exit destroys voice by draining off the people who would have used it. The competition that was supposed to discipline the railway had instead anaesthetised it.

He then generalises, and the generalisation is the part that most readers remember. Think of American public schools in a city where some parents can afford private ones. When the public school slips, the parents who see it first and care most are, disproportionately, the ones who can pay to leave. They leave. The school loses the very parents who would have turned up at the board meeting, and its decline proceeds with less resistance than before. Hirschman is careful here, more careful than the people who later quoted him: he does not say private schools are wrong, or that exit should be blocked. He says that an option which is good for each leaver can be bad for the institution and for everyone who stays, and that anyone designing the choice needs to know this.

He runs the same logic through politics, in a chapter that reads oddly in a season when the shelf has been so hard on parties. Anthony Downs had argued that in a two-party system both parties drift to the centre, because the voters at the extremes have nowhere else to go. Hirschman's reply is that a voter who cannot exit does not go quiet; he gets loud. The captive member of a party is the member most likely to use voice, since voice is all he has, and a party that takes its base for granted because the base cannot leave has misread the situation as thoroughly as the railway did. The prediction was made in nineteen seventy. Anyone who has watched a party's activists drag it away from the centre since then has watched the prediction come in, and this is one place where the book, rather than its critics, has aged well.

Chapter Four: The Monopolist Who Would Rather Be Left Alone

A second mechanism, and this one comes with a label I have never been able to forget. Hirschman's term for it is the lazy monopoly. The textbook monopolist is greedy, and squeezes the customer. Hirschman describes a different creature: the monopolist who is tired rather than greedy, who would rather have a quiet life than a large profit, and for whom a certain amount of exit is a relief. If the customers who leave are the fussy ones, the ones who write letters and demand to see someone, then their departure improves the day of everybody who works there. The organisation is not fighting exit. It is welcoming it, because exit is how it sheds its critics.

He gives examples from the sixties and I will give one from a hospital, constructed, but assembled from real parts. A department has a difficult senior nurse, the kind who reads the drug chart the doctor has just written and asks about the dose. She is right often enough to be a nuisance. She has been raising the same three issues at the staff meeting for two years: the missing night cover, the broken observation machine on bay four, the handover that takes place in a corridor. Nothing changes, because nothing has to; her complaints are absorbed, minuted, and filed. One day she takes a job at the private hospital across town, where the observation machines work. The department's official response is regret. Its actual response is relief. The meeting is shorter now. The drug charts are not read as closely, and it will be some months before anybody notices the dose that gets through. Hirschman's insight, and it is an insight about institutions rather than people, is that the department did not push her out. It simply made staying pointless and leaving easy, and the mechanism did the rest.

The shelf has met this before under other names. Davies's accountability sink, from episode two, is a structure that ensures the complaint can find nobody to land on. Hirschman gives you the flow that the sink is built to handle. Voice arrives, is absorbed, achieves nothing, and the person who raised it does the arithmetic and exits, and the sink's designers count the exit as a problem solved. Whyte's organization man, from the sixth episode, is the opposite figure: the man who has decided never to exit and has, over the years, lost the capacity for voice too, so that loyalty in him has become mere presence. Hirschman is the writer who explains why both figures are stable.

Chapter Five: Loyalty, the Brake

Which brings the book to its third term, and the term Hirschman himself was least sure of. If exit drains voice, then something has to hold people in place long enough for voice to be tried, and that something he calls loyalty. He does not mean sentiment. He means whatever it is that makes a person delay exit, that makes them say, this place has been good and I will give it another year, or, if I go, who will say this. Loyalty in his sense is a brake on exit and therefore a subsidy to voice. It buys the organisation time in which the complaint can work.

He is clear that loyalty can be manufactured, and that the manufacturers are not always friends of the institution. High entry costs, initiation rituals, the years of training that a profession demands, all raise the price of exit and so all increase the reliance on voice. A medical training is, among other things, a loyalty device: it is so expensive to become a doctor that most doctors stay, and complain, rather than leave. Whether that is good depends entirely on whether the complaints are heard. Hirschman also flags the perverse case: the member so loyal that they never leave and never speak, who has converted the brake into a parking brake. That is Whyte's man again, and it is the loyalty of the party member who stays through the purges. Loyalty is only a virtue in his scheme when it is the kind that ends in voice, and, if voice fails, in exit. The threat of exit, kept credible, is what gives voice its force. A complaint from someone who will never leave is a complaint the institution can afford to ignore.

Here the clinician's lens has something to add. Medicine's culture teaches loyalty of the wrong kind. The unwritten rules, do not go over your consultant's head, do not embarrass the department, do not put it in writing, are all devices for suppressing voice while keeping the member. The result is the department where the problems are known to everyone and said by no one, where the exits happen silently and are read as personal decisions, and where the first outsider to look sees a pattern the insiders could not. Hirschman would say such a department has arranged its incentives to receive neither signal. It has made voice futile and exit quiet, and it has done so while congratulating itself on staff retention.

Chapter Six: When Exit Became Policy

The book did not stay on the shelf, and the place it went is one a clinician should know about, because it went into the hospital. In the two thousands the British government, advised by the economist Julian Le Grand, rebuilt the National Health Service around patient choice: publish the hospitals' results, let patients choose where to be treated, let the money follow the patient. Le Grand's book on the reform, The Other Invisible Hand, from two thousand and seven, argues the case in Hirschman's vocabulary. Voice, he says, had been tried for fifty years in the health service, through complaints procedures and community health councils and the ballot box, and it had failed, because voice favours the articulate. The middle-class patient who knows how to write a letter gets heard; the patient who does not, does not. Exit, by contrast, is available to anyone who can get on a bus. Give every patient the power to leave, and the hospitals will have to improve for everyone, since they cannot tell in advance which patient will walk.

That is a real argument and a fair use of the book, and the shelf owes it the steelman. It is also, on Hirschman's own account, incomplete, and he would have said so. First, the exit has to be real. A patient with a broken hip does not shop; a patient in a rural district has one hospital; and a patient whose general practitioner makes the referral is exercising the doctor's exit, not their own. Second, and this is the railway again, the exits that happen will be the connoisseurs', the informed patients with cars, and their departure will lower the pressure on the hospital they left unless the money that follows them is large enough to hurt, and in a public system it rarely is. Third, choice was introduced alongside a deliberate effort to keep hospitals open, because closing one is a political catastrophe, and that combination, easy exit with no death, is the Nigerian railway's exact configuration. The evidence since has been argued over for two decades, with studies from Zack Cooper and colleagues at the London School of Economics finding modest gains in mortality and management quality in areas with more competition, and others finding little. What I take from it is smaller than either side's claim: Hirschman gives you the checklist for whether an exit-based reform can work, and the checklist was not run.

There is one more figure in the book that the health-service story needs, and it is the boycott. Hirschman notes that a boycott is exit used as voice: the customers leave, but they leave loudly, with a demand attached, and they announce the terms on which they will return. It is the one form in which the two mechanisms combine on purpose, and it foreshadows the East German case he would not understand for another twenty years. A profession that wanted to change a hospital could learn from it. The junior doctors who leave one at a time, each with a personal reason, are Hirschman's silent exiters, and their departures teach the hospital nothing. The same doctors, leaving together with a stated reason and a stated condition of return, are a boycott, and a boycott is the only exit an institution is built to hear.

Chapter Seven: The Case Against, and the Case He Made Against Himself

Now the objections, and there are serious ones. The first came in nineteen seventy-four from the political theorist Brian Barry, in a long review that admirers of the book still have to answer. Barry's complaint was about the structure. Hirschman presents exit and voice as if they were one choice, and Barry pointed out that they are two. First comes the choice to stay or go. Then, having stayed, a second choice: speak or keep quiet. Silence is an option, and a common one, and the book's neat pairing hides it. Barry also went after loyalty, and the phrase he used has stuck: loyalty, he said, was an equation filler, a term Hirschman brought in to make the model come out, defined mostly by what it needed to do. Keith Dowding and colleagues, reviewing the whole literature in two thousand, reached a similar place from a gentler direction: the schema is more complicated than it looks, the empirical basis of loyalty is thin, and to test the theory you have to pull its parts apart in ways the book never did. There is a family of formal models since, William Clark and Matt Golder's among them, that rebuild the argument as a game and find that some of Hirschman's results hold and some depend on assumptions he did not state.

I think Barry is right about the structure and mostly right about loyalty, and I think it costs the book less than it should. The two-stage version is better, and it is the version a clinician needs, because silence is the modal response in any hospital, and a theory that has no box for it is a theory that cannot see the ward. Loyalty as Hirschman used it is underdefined, and its best defence is that he said so: he calls the chapter a first try and ends the book with a list of the cases he could not handle.

Then there is the objection that Hirschman made against himself, and it is the reason I trust him. In nineteen eighty-nine East Germans started leaving through Hungary, and then through Czechoslovakia, by the tens of thousands. On the theory, that exit should have drained the voice: the people who could leave would leave, the regime would be left with the compliant, and the Leipzig demonstrations should have fizzled. The opposite happened. The exodus and the demonstrations grew together, each feeding the other, until the state fell. Hirschman, then in his late seventies, wrote a paper about it in nineteen ninety-three, and its argument is that his own book had been wrong about the relation of the two mechanisms. He had treated them as substitutes, one draining the other. In East Germany they were complements: the exit proved that the regime could not hold its people, and the demonstration proved that the people who stayed were not consenting, and each message made the other credible. He described the paper as the correction of an error of his own, with a clarity that few authors manage about their best-known work. A theory whose author can be seen revising it against evidence has a property the shelf has been looking for all season, and most of the cynical canon does not have it.

Chapter Eight: What This Gives a Manager

The four books in this antidote season are for the person inside, and Hirschman's is the most useful of the four for the person who is not yet in charge, because it is a theory of what your options are and how the institution reads them. But it also says something to the person who is in charge, and I want to draw it out, because it is not in the book as a chapter and it is the reason a manager should read it.

If you run anything, Hirschman tells you where your information comes from, and it is not from the people who are happy. It comes from the people who complain and stay, and it comes, in a form you have to learn to read, from the people who leave. The manager's first job on this theory is to make voice cheap. That means answering it, visibly, even when the answer is no, because a complaint that is minuted and filed teaches the complainant that the mechanism is dead, and the next person will not bother. It means protecting the people who use voice, since the nurse who reads the drug chart is the department's early warning system and every manager who has ever found her tiresome has confused the sensor with the noise. And it means reading exits as data: when someone leaves, the useful question is what they knew, and whether the department has now lost the last person who would have said it.

Let me run that through one case, constructed again, so that the abstraction has a shape. A clinical director inherits a department with a turnover problem: five junior doctors gone in eighteen months, each departure explained at the time by a personal reason, a partner's job, a fellowship, a move home. She does the thing Hirschman implies and nobody does, which is to go back through the previous two years of staff meeting minutes and find what each of the five had raised. Four of them had brought up the same item: the weekend handover, at which the departing team briefed the arriving one in a room where the phone rang continuously and the list was on a whiteboard that had been half-erased. Each had raised it once, been told it was on the list, and not raised it again. None of them mentioned it when they resigned. That last fact is the one to hold onto. By the time a person exits, they have usually stopped using voice, and the exit interview asks the question a year too late. The director moves the handover to a room with a door, prints the list, and unplugs the phone for twenty minutes. She cannot prove that this is why the next eighteen months lose one junior instead of five. She can say that the two years of minutes had contained the answer, in the voices of the people who then left, and that nobody had been reading them as a message because each one, alone, had looked like a comment.

The second job is to keep exit visible. An organisation that can watch its exits and ask what they mean is one that can still learn. Hirschman's railway could not, because the state paid the deficit and the exits were just numbers on a freight sheet. A department that counts staff turnover as a cost line rather than a message has built the same railway. The manager who reads Hirschman and does one thing should do this: for every departure in the next year, find out what the person had been saying for the previous two, and whether anybody had answered.

There is a third thing, and it is about the manager's own exit. Hirschman's argument gives the person in charge a reason to keep their own exit credible. A manager who cannot leave, who has staked the mortgage and the identity on the job, is a manager whose voice upward has no force, because the people above know they will stay through anything. The shelf's cynical books were full of such people, and Jackall's managers, from the first episode, were their purest form. The reading of Hirschman that helps is that loyalty is a brake you apply for a purpose, and a brake that is never released is a wheel that no longer turns.

Chapter Nine: Verdict, Dose, and a Question

The verdict is that this is a short book, a hundred and sixty pages, which every person who belongs to anything should read, and that it should be read with Barry's correction in hand and Hirschman's East German paper afterwards. Its predictions fail often enough that you should not use it to predict. Its categories are right so consistently that you will not be able to stop using them to see. The reading prescription is the book, in one sitting; Barry's review if you can find it, which is in the British Journal of Political Science for nineteen seventy-four; and the nineteen ninety-three paper, which is in World Politics and is the best thing on the list.

The dose is different from the cynical books, and I want to be explicit about why. Jackall and Davies are diagnostic in small amounts and poisonous as an identity, because a person who has fully absorbed them stops acting. Hirschman is the reverse. He is a theory of action, and its side effect is a slightly too neat belief that your options are always two. Take him in full doses, and correct for the neatness with Barry.

The question to take back to work is in two parts, and you should ask it about the place you work rather than about yourself. First: who has left in the last year, and what had they been saying? Write the names down; there will be fewer than you think, and the pattern will be clearer than you expect. Second: who is still there, saying the same thing for two years, heard by nobody? That person is your railway's last complaining customer. Find out what it would cost to answer them, and then compare it to what it will cost when they take the job across town.

Next time, the second antidote, and the one that answers a question this shelf's whole cynical season has been assuming has no answer: whether people can run a shared resource without a boss and without a market. Elinor Ostrom's Governing the Commons, on the Swiss villagers, the Spanish water courts, and the Nobel Prize that went to the woman who went and looked. Thanks for listening.
