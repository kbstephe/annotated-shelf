The Women's Health Initiative (Rossouw and colleagues): a trial that reversed the cohort studies

In 1996 a cohort of fifty-nine thousand nurses said hormone therapy cut coronary risk by more than half. In 2002 a randomised trial said it raised it. This episode follows the reversal through the papers that tried to reconcile the two.

26 October 2026

Chapter One: Two answers with opposite signs

In August 1996, the New England Journal of Medicine published a paper from the Nurses' Health Study. Its authors had followed fifty-nine thousand, three hundred and thirty-seven women for up to sixteen years, counted seven hundred and seventy heart attacks and coronary deaths among them, and compared the women who took hormones after the menopause with the women who did not. For women taking estrogen together with a progestin, the relative risk of a major coronary event was 0.39, with a confidence interval running from 0.19 to 0.78. For estrogen alone it was 0.60. For the combination, a risk cut by more than half, from a drug that millions of women were already swallowing.

In July 2002, the Journal of the American Medical Association published the first large randomised trial of the same combination in healthy women. Sixteen thousand, six hundred and eight women aged fifty to seventy-nine, at forty centres, were assigned by lottery to one tablet a day of conjugated equine estrogens with medroxyprogesterone acetate, or to a placebo. The trial was stopped on the last day of May after an average of 5.2 years. The hazard ratio for coronary heart disease was 1.29, with an interval from 1.02 to 1.63. The sign had flipped.

The trial's principal-results paper itself would not open from here, so what follows takes its figures from the abstract and from the later reports that restate them in full text.

This is the sixth stop in a series about how medicine learned to tell a cause from an association, and it is the first where the quarrel is between methods rather than inside one. The old question is why the best-looking observational evidence of its decade was wrong, and the question it opens is harder: wrong in what way? The usual retelling says that hormone users were healthier women to begin with, and that the cohorts mistook the women for the pill. That story is partly true, and one experiment supports it, which I will run to its end. But a re-analysis published in 2008 by some of the cohort's own investigators suggests the story is also incomplete, and the incompleteness changes what a trialist should take away.

The case was not obscure. At least forty percent of postmenopausal women in the United States were using hormone therapy shortly before the trial reported, according to the WHI investigators' own later overview. Professional organisations were recommending it for the prevention of heart disease and of osteoporosis, and about a third of prescriptions, in the account of two of the trial's investigators, went to women over sixty. A treatment at that scale, resting on that evidence, tests how much a pile of consistent cohort studies is worth.

Chapter Two: The case for the cohorts

The strongest version of the position that hormones protect the heart is not a naive one. In 1991, Meir Stampfer and Graham Colditz published a quantitative review of the epidemiology in Preventive Medicine. They did not average everything together and call it a day; they sorted the studies by design and judged each group on its own weaknesses. Five hospital-based case-control studies gave inconsistent, roughly null results, and the authors set them aside as hard to interpret, because choosing controls from a hospital's patients means choosing people who are in the hospital for reasons that may relate to hormones. Six population-based case-control studies showed lower risk among users. Three cross-sectional angiography studies showed markedly less atherosclerosis in estrogen users. Of sixteen prospective studies, fifteen came out lower for users, mostly with statistically significant intervals. Framingham alone came out higher, and the abstract says that stopped being significant when angina was left out.

The pooled relative risk was 0.56, and for the internally controlled prospective and angiographic studies, 0.50. The authors concluded that the bulk of the evidence supported a protective effect that was "unlikely to be explained by confounding factors."

There was also a mechanism, and it was the right sort of mechanism: the kind that can be measured. Oral estrogen lowered low-density lipoprotein and raised high-density lipoprotein. A drug that moves the lipids in the direction every cardiologist wanted, in a cohort that shows fewer heart attacks, is a story with two legs. Add that the nurses were a well-run cohort, with biennial questionnaires and a long list of adjustments, and the position looks as strong as non-randomised evidence gets.

The Nurses' Health Study paper of 1996 was the most direct test of the combination, and it did one more useful thing. It asked about progestin, which women with a uterus take with estrogen to protect the uterine lining, and found the combination at least as protective as estrogen alone. That was the preparation the trial would later test. On the stroke side, the cohort found no clear association: 1.09 for the combination, with an interval from 0.66 to 1.80. So the cohort was not claiming a free lunch. It said the heart gained and the brain was not obviously harmed.

Now the first crack. In August 1998, the Heart and Estrogen/progestin Replacement Study, known as HERS, reported in the same journal that later carried the WHI. HERS was a randomised, blinded, placebo-controlled trial, and its question was narrower: women who already had coronary disease, two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-three of them with a mean age of nearly sixty-seven, given the same combination or a placebo for an average of 4.1 years. Heart attacks and coronary deaths came to a hundred and seventy-two in the hormone group and four more than that on placebo. Relative hazard 0.99. And this happened while the hormone arm's LDL cholesterol was eleven percent lower, and its HDL ten percent higher, than the placebo arm's. The lipid mechanism did what it was supposed to do, and the events did not follow.

The abstract adds two details that come back in this episode. Within the null result there was a time trend, with more coronary events in the hormone group in the first year and fewer in years four and five. And venous thromboembolism was nearly three times as common, thirty-four events against twelve, with gallbladder disease also more frequent.

The WHI investigators' 2013 commentary describes the reading that was often offered: observational studies represented healthy women who started hormones close to the menopause, while trial participants were older or had vascular disease already. On that reading HERS had enrolled the wrong women, and the cohorts could still be right. The authors of HERS themselves said the favourable late pattern made it reasonable for women already on treatment to continue. The field did not abandon the cohorts in 1998. It adjusted its sense of whom they described.

The trial that would settle the matter was already running. The National Institutes of Health had launched the Women's Health Initiative, as two of its investigators wrote a decade afterwards, to find out whether the lower coronary risk seen in observational studies "was real." The trial existed because the cohorts were believed enough to justify a test, and doubted enough to want one.

Chapter Three: What the pill-takers were like before the pill

Before the trial reported, one study had already asked about the women themselves, and its answer should have worried people more than it did. It is no trial of hormones. It followed the women who would later choose them.

In 1996, Karen Matthews and colleagues in Pittsburgh published a paper whose title is its question: prior to use of estrogen replacement therapy, are users healthier than nonusers? They had a cohort of five hundred and forty-one premenopausal women whose cardiovascular risk factors and psychosocial characteristics were measured at entry, before anyone had made a decision about hormones. About eight years later, three hundred and fifty-five of them had become postmenopausal, and more than two in five of those reported using estrogen during follow-up. The authors went back to the baseline measurements and compared the future users with the future non-users.

Those who would go on to use estrogen were better educated: eighty-one percent had at least some college, against sixty-three percent of the others. Their HDL cholesterol was higher, 1.59 millimoles per litre against 1.49. They exercised more in their leisure time and drank slightly more alcohol. Systolic blood pressure averaged 107.1 against 112.1, body weight 64.2 kilograms against 68.5, and fasting insulin 7.66 against 9.10. All of this was measured before the first pill.

Run that to its end. A group that is leaner, fitter, better educated, with better lipids and lower insulin before any exposure, will have fewer heart attacks than a comparison group whether or not hormones do anything at all. That is the healthy-user effect: the people who take up a preventive treatment differ from those who do not in ways that themselves predict the outcome, and the difference exists before the treatment starts. The Pittsburgh authors drew the obvious conclusion, that part of the apparent benefit of hormones was due to preexisting characteristics, and ended by underscoring the importance of randomised trials.

The Pittsburgh study shows that the groups differed on things that were measured. The Nurses' Health Study adjusted for many such things, and the later re-analysis I will describe adjusted for more than twenty, among them education, body mass index, smoking in five categories, physical activity and a diet score. Adjustment removes the part of the healthy-user effect that the questionnaire captured and leaves whatever the questionnaire could not see. The gap in Pittsburgh was large and spread over many dimensions, the situation in which residual confounding is hardest to rule out. Whether it stayed large after adjustment, or made the cohorts' answer wrong, the paper could not say.

That is why a randomised trial was the right instrument. The lottery in an experiment does not need a list of what the women had in common. It balances the things nobody thought to measure, on average, when the numbers are large, and sixteen thousand women are a safe bet for that. Had the cohorts been right, the draw would have confirmed them. Had the healthy-user story been the main thing, it would have removed the benefit. What it could not do, as things turned out, was say which of several other stories was in play.

Chapter Four: The trial

The Women's Health Initiative was large enough to be several studies. The part that concerns us was its estrogen-plus-progestin trial: eighty-five hundred and six women assigned to the active tablet and eighty-one hundred and two to the placebo, all with an intact uterus, recruited from 1993 to 1998. The primary outcome was coronary heart disease, defined as nonfatal heart attack or coronary death. The primary adverse outcome, set in advance, was invasive breast cancer. A global index summed the benefits and harms: those two, plus stroke, pulmonary embolism, endometrial cancer, colorectal cancer, hip fracture and death from other causes.

On the last day of May 2002, the data and safety monitoring board recommended stopping. The breast cancer statistic had crossed its boundary for harm, and the global index supported the view that risks exceeded benefits. Here are the abstract's hazard ratios, with the case count each rests on. Coronary disease, 1.29, from two hundred and eighty-six cases. Breast cancer, 1.26, and stroke, 1.41. Pulmonary embolism, 2.13. On the other side, colorectal cancer, 0.63, and hip fracture, 0.66. Total mortality, 0.98.

The trial's authors converted these into absolute terms, a form closer to what a patient needs. Per ten thousand women taking the tablet for a year: seven more coronary events, eight more strokes, eight more pulmonary emboli and eight more invasive breast cancers, against six fewer colorectal cancers and five fewer hip fractures. The paper's global index, which also counts the smaller components, comes to nineteen more events per ten thousand person-years. That is about one woman in five hundred, each year, who had an event she would not have had on a placebo. It is a small risk per woman, and with hormone therapy so widely used, a large number across a country.

The coronary finding was not the one that stopped the trial. The coronary hazard ratio of 1.29 had a nominal interval that excluded one by a hair. In the final analysis, reported the next year in the New England Journal of Medicine, the ratio was 1.24 with an interval from 1.00 to 1.54, and when the interval was adjusted for the trial's sequential monitoring, it ran from 0.97 to 1.60. In plain terms, a trial that looked at its data repeatedly cannot treat a barely significant interval as significant, and once the repeated looks were counted, the heart finding was compatible with no effect. What the trial excluded, with some confidence, was the large benefit the cohorts had reported. A halving of risk is a long way from an interval whose lower end is 0.97.

That is a more careful statement than "hormones cause heart attacks," and the authors made it: the 2003 abstract says the combination does not confer cardiac protection and may increase the risk, especially in the first year, when the hazard ratio was 1.81, with an interval from 1.09 to 3.01. The year-one excess is the same pattern that HERS had shown, a few years earlier, in women with established disease.

The other thing usually skipped is who was enrolled. The women were fifty to seventy-nine, and in the two hormone trials together, as the WHI commentary tabulates them, about seven thousand were within ten years of the menopause and more than eight thousand were twenty or more years past it. The Nurses' cohort was recruited in 1976 at ages thirty to fifty-five, and its users, as I will describe, did not all start near the menopause either. The two were asking one question about overlapping but different groups of women at different moments, and a lottery can guarantee a fair comparison only inside the women it has.

After the stop, a second hormone trial in the same programme, which gave estrogen alone to women who had had a hysterectomy, continued until early 2004. It also stopped early, because of an increase in strokes, but its heart result was neutral, as the later overview reports it: a hazard ratio of 0.94 during the intervention phase, with an interval from 0.78 to 1.14. So even inside the trial programme, two different preparations gave two different answers.

Chapter Five: Who is left to be counted

By 2005, there were two competing readings of the discrepancy. In the first, the cohorts were confounded by healthy-user bias, and the trial had found the truth. In the second, the cohorts and the trial were describing different things and could be brought into line.

Ross Prentice and the WHI investigators tested the second reading in an unusual way, because they had a cohort of their own. Alongside the trial, the WHI ran an observational study of fifty-three thousand and fifty-four women, a third of them taking estrogen plus progestin at enrolment. The same programme ran both, with one set of women randomised and another set not. After adjusting for age, the observational hazard ratios for coronary disease, stroke and venous thromboembolism came out thirty-nine to forty-eight percent lower than the trial's. That is the discrepancy, reproduced inside one programme.

Then they looked at time. Hazard ratios tended to fall with the years since a woman started hormones, and the two studies weighted those years differently: the trial's estimate reflects a few years of treatment from the day of the draw, while the observational study's estimate is dominated by women who had been taking hormones for a long time. When they controlled for time since initiation and for confounding, the two hazard ratios became rather similar, the abstract says, although some difference remained for stroke.

The WHI investigators' later commentary puts a number on the weighting. In the observational study, only fifteen percent of the women counted as current users at baseline had used the combination for less than two years. Everyone else had already come through the early period. If the combination raises the risk of a coronary event in the first year or so, as the trial and HERS before it showed, and the risk then falls back to neutral or below, a cohort of current users has been screened for survival of the dangerous part. The early casualties are not in it. They are not misclassified. They are absent, and the absent are the people the drug harmed.

That is a different mechanism from the healthy-user effect, with a different remedy, and it does not need the women to differ before treatment at all. Imagine a drug that is mildly dangerous in its opening weeks and harmless afterwards, with made-up numbers to show the logic. A study that recruits everyone currently taking it meets only people who got past that early stretch. The danger happened before anyone was on the books, and next to non-users the drug looks at least harmless. Epidemiologists call this prevalent-user bias, and the remedy is the new-user design, which starts counting on the day treatment begins.

Chapter Six: The Nurses, reanalysed

The paper that carries this argument furthest was published in Epidemiology in 2008, with Miguel Hernán as first author and a group that included Francine Grodstein, the first author of the 1996 Nurses' Health Study paper. The paper is the cohort's own analysts, with new tools, taking the cohort back to the trial's design and seeing whether the two could be made to agree. I have the full text of this one.

The method later acquired a name, target trial emulation, though this paper's own phrase is "analyzed like randomized experiments." The idea is to write down the protocol of the trial you wish you had, and then to build the observational dataset into that protocol as closely as it will allow. For the WHI that meant a woman had to be postmenopausal and without a recent hormone prescription at the start; she had to be one of two kinds, an initiator who began estrogen plus progestin in the interval, or a non-initiator who did not; she had to have a uterus, no history of cancer or heart attack, and so on. Follow-up began at the moment of initiation and was counted from there. And the analysis was intention to treat, as in the trial: initiators against non-initiators, whether or not the initiators later stopped.

The Nurses' questionnaires came every two years, so the cohort became a sequence of eight such nonrandomised trials, one for each questionnaire from 1984 to 1998, pooled. The pooled sample held thirty-four thousand, five hundred and seventy-five eligible women and a little over a thousand coronary events.

Over the whole follow-up, the hazard ratio of coronary disease for initiators against non-initiators was 0.96, with an interval from 0.78 to 1.18. That is the Nurses' Health Study, whose published estimates had been 0.39 and later 0.68, now null. In the first two years it was 1.42, with an interval from 0.92 to 2.20, close to the trial's 1.68 for the same period. Over the first eight years it was 1.00, against about 1.24 in the trial. Same cohort, same women, same disease; a different design, and the protection was gone and the early excess appeared.

Then the paper walked, step by step, from that design back to the old one, and the walk shows how a number moves. I will give four of the steps. In the trial-like design, the first-two-years hazard ratio was 1.42. Changing only the moment follow-up began, from the month of initiation to the month the next questionnaire arrived, gave 0.98. This one change removes women who started hormones and had a nonfatal heart attack before the questionnaire reached them. They are never counted as users who had an event, so the early excess disappears. Then changing the exposed group from fresh initiators to anyone currently using, the hazard ratio fell to 0.77. After further changes in which covariate values were used and which women were eligible, it reached 0.67, which the authors say matches almost perfectly the two-year estimate of the most recent earlier publication from the cohort, reported at 0.68 for current users against never users.

So the benefit was manufactured in stages, by a chain of ordinary design choices, each defensible when it was made. Start the clock at the questionnaire, because that is when you know the exposure. Compare current users with never users, because that is the clean contrast. Update exposure every two years, because that is how the data arrive. None of these is a mistake in the way a transcription error is a mistake. Together, they delete the people the drug hurt.

Now the second finding, which concerns the timing hypothesis. Stratified by time since menopause, the Nurses' initiators within ten years of menopause had a hazard ratio of 0.84 (0.61 to 1.14), against 0.88 (0.54 to 1.43) in the WHI for the same stratum. For women further out, 1.12 in the cohort and 1.23 in the trial. The two bodies of evidence, so far apart in their headline numbers, agree stratum by stratum. In the further-out stratum, eighty-six percent of the initiators had started therapy ten to twenty years after menopause, and the WHI commentary adds that as the cohort aged, the share of Nurses who began later than that rose sharply between 1984 and 1998. The cohort's users were not simply women who began at the menopause. The authors' summary is that the discrepancy between the two could be largely explained by differences in the distribution of time since menopause and by length of follow-up. Their discussion adds that residual confounding for the effect of starting therapy "seems to play little role."

The paper is careful about the limits of that second claim. With the adjustments in a new-user design, the confounding that the Pittsburgh study predicted did not show up as a visible gap against the trial. That is no proof that unmeasured confounding was absent. It is an observation that the dominant source of the discrepancy was somewhere else.

The stratified results sit inside wide intervals, as every quoted one shows. In the Nurses' analysis, the interaction by time since menopause had a p-value of 0.08. In the adherence-adjusted analysis, the hazard ratio was 0.54 in women within ten years of menopause and 1.20 in the others, interaction 0.01, and the authors warn that either effect, and the interaction between them, could be sampling variability. So the paper is weak on whether early hormone use protects, and strong on the question it was built for: whether cohort data, handled like a trial, would disagree with the trial. They did not.

Chapter Seven: Early, late, and the zodiac

One claim survived the reversal and now lives on its own. The timing hypothesis says that hormones may help the heart if started close to the menopause, and harm it if started late, because healthy arteries respond to estrogen differently from diseased ones.

The case for it rests on a subgroup analysis. In 2007, Jacques Rossouw and colleagues pooled the two WHI hormone trials, the combination and the estrogen-alone, and sorted the coronary hazard ratio by years since menopause. For women less than ten years past it, 0.76 (0.50 to 1.16). For ten to nineteen years, 1.10 (0.84 to 1.45). For twenty or more, 1.28 (1.03 to 1.58). In absolute terms, six fewer events per ten thousand person-years in the first group, four more in the second, seventeen more in the third. The trend test had a p-value of .02, and the abstract says the trend did not meet the authors' own criterion for statistical significance. By age the pattern was similar in direction, weaker in statistics.

This is a subgroup finding. Episode seventy-one told how, in a trial of seventeen thousand patients with suspected heart attacks, the patients born under Gemini and Libra got no benefit from aspirin at all, and how a subgroup read as an answer harms the people it is applied to. Does the timing hypothesis escape that verdict?

In part. It has what the zodiac lacks: a reason stated before the data, in the vascular biology; it replicates in direction in the observational stratification of the Nurses' reanalysis, which is independent information, although not independent in its authors; and it was proposed as an explanation for a discrepancy rather than as a claim extracted from a table. The later WHI overview is itself cautious. It notes that none of its age and menopause-timing p-values were adjusted for multiple subgroup comparisons, that for the combination trial the trend test across time since menopause gave 0.08 for coronary disease, and that the results in younger women "remain inconclusive."

What it lacks is a prediction that came true. A hypothesis that arrives after a surprise and explains it is, in the terms the Kuhn episodes borrowed from Lakatos, in danger of being a patch, and a patch earns credit only by forecasting something it was not built from. The commentary by Rossouw and colleagues in 2013 reports the attempts to supply that forecast. Two small trials in women close to menopause, using imaging outcomes (carotid wall thickness and coronary calcium) as surrogates, were under way: one, KEEPS, had preliminary results showing no effect on either imaging measure over four years, though with benefits for quality of life, and the other, ELITE, had not yet reported. I know no more about them than that commentary says, and I did not read the trials. The commentary also records why the definitive trial, with clinical outcomes in women near menopause, is unlikely to be run: it would need about thirty thousand women for ten to fifteen years.

So the timing hypothesis stands where a good subgroup claim stands without a confirming trial. It is plausible, it is not nothing, and nobody should write a prescription on it. The same cautious position is the one Manson and colleagues take in the 2013 overview, with thirteen years of cumulative follow-up and both trials in hand. Their summary is that hormone therapy remains a reasonable option for moderate-to-severe menopausal symptoms in healthy women early in menopause, and that the trials do not support either regimen for the prevention of chronic disease, even in younger women, because of stroke, venous thrombosis and the limits of subgroup analysis.

One more result in that overview complicates the story I have been telling. In the estrogen-alone trial, the cumulative breast cancer hazard ratio was 0.79 (0.65 to 0.97): fewer cancers on the drug. The authors call this unexpected, and say it differs from many observational studies. Here the cohorts were wrong in the other direction, and one explanation they offer is differing use of mammography between users and non-users, which would be a third mechanism, neither a pre-treatment difference nor a survival screen but a difference in how closely the two groups were looked at.

Chapter Eight: The case against the simple story

The version of this episode that circulates in the lecture hall runs as follows: observational studies said hormones protect the heart, a randomised trial said otherwise, and so observational studies cannot be trusted, and anyone who prefers a cohort to a trial has been warned. I think that version is wrong in three ways, and the third is the one to keep.

First, the observational field could see its own flaw once it looked. The WHI commentary reports that after HERS appeared, several investigators re-analysed their secondary-prevention cohort data and found the early rise in risk their first analyses had missed. It adds that observational studies and the trials broadly agreed on stroke, on venous clots, on breast cancer for the combination, and on the reductions in fracture and colorectal cancer. A method that agrees with the trials on all of those and disagrees on coronary disease has a specific, identifiable flaw, which is a different matter from a method that cannot be trusted. Jan Vandenbroucke's essay in the Lancet in 2009 is titled "The HRT controversy: observational studies and RCTs fall in line." I did not hold its text, so I cannot say how he builds the case, but the title is a fair summary of where the reconciliation papers ended up.

Second, the people who rescued the cohorts are the same people who had run them. The Nurses' Health Study investigator JoAnn Manson is on the 1996 paper and the 2003 trial paper, and Grodstein is on the 1996 paper and the 2008 reanalysis. This is a point in favour of the science and a caution about the telling. It means the reconciliation was done by people with a stake in cohort methods being mendable, and the WHI insiders who wrote the 2013 commentary have a stake in their trial being sound. I have no reason to think either group slanted its numbers, since the 2008 paper puts its own sampling uncertainty in plain sight. But the reconciliation is an inside job, and a skeptic would want someone outside to repeat it. I found no such replication, though my search went no further than the sources named here.

Third, the trial was not a clean verdict either. It tested one dose, one formulation and one route, in women many of whom were far past the menopause, for a few years. It stopped early on a breast cancer boundary, and its heart finding after adjustment for monitoring was compatible with no effect. Nearly a fifth of the surviving participants declined consent for the extended follow-up, which the 2013 overview lists among its own limitations. The estrogen-alone trial pointed in a different direction on the heart and on breast cancer. The trial showed that the large benefit the cohorts reported was absent. It left open whether the drug is simply harmful, and whether the cohorts' mistakes were mostly about healthy women.

The cohorts were wrong for a reason that sat in the design and not in the women. The clock is not a feature of the data. It is a choice by the analyst, and the choice that made hormones look protective was where the analyst started counting.

The healthy-user story is not refuted either. Matthews showed real pre-treatment differences. Hernán's adjustment may have absorbed them, or the new-user design may have shrunk whatever it did not absorb, and the paper cannot tell which. The evidence in hand supports two statements that are easy to run together: the healthy-user effect was real, and it was not the main thing.

Chapter Nine: What a trialist does with this

For someone who writes analysis plans and reads cohort papers, the use comes in two parts, one for each side of the design.

On the observational side, a test can be applied to any cohort estimate in a few minutes. Write down the trial the analysis imitates. Who was eligible? What does it mean for a person to start treatment, and on what date? What are the two arms at that date? Then ask where the clock begins in the data. If it begins at a survey, an enrolment visit or a registry entry, and exposure is defined as "currently doing the thing," then everyone exposed is a survivor of the period between starting and being counted. A paper that cannot write the paragraph has no time zero, and a result without a time zero is, like the Nurses' 0.68, correct arithmetic about a group nobody ever assembled on the day treatment began.

On the trial side, the lesson sits in the plan. The timing hypothesis was formed after the data. If the effect of a treatment is expected to depend on when it is started, who is started, or how long, that dependence belongs in the analysis plan beforehand, as one named effect modifier with a stated direction, and everything else belongs in a labelled exploratory section. A trial that reports the year-one hazard ratio and the later ratios separately, as the WHI and HERS both did, tells its readers something a single average hides, and two trials agreeing on the early excess is what made it believable.

On reading: Hernán 2008 is the paper to read in full, about an hour. Its methods section shows how to write down the protocol you wish you had, and the paragraph that walks from 1.42 to 0.67 shows how design choices become effect estimates. The Rossouw 2013 commentary is shorter and gives the trial side's account, with the caveat that it is theirs. The 2002 trial paper I held only as an abstract and through its restatements, so I cannot advise on the rest of it.

A treatment taken by a large share of postmenopausal women for prevention, on the strength of cohort evidence, did not lower their heart risk and did raise a small, countable number of strokes and clots. The main error in the cohorts was that their clock started after the harm had happened.
