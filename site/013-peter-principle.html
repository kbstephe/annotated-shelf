The Peter Principle: promotion to incompetence, and the data that proved it

Laurence Peter and Raymond Hull's 1969 satire on the last promotion everyone eventually gets – hierarchiology's mock science, the sales-floor data that later proved the joke true, and the one individual-level antidote the book actually offers.

25 August 2026

Chapter One: The Nurse Who Ran the Ward

Think of the best nurse you have ever watched work a ward. Fast without being rushed, the kind of competence that reads as calm because nothing is ever a crisis by the time she reaches it. Eighteen months later she is the nursing supervisor, and the ward is worse. Not because she stopped caring, and not because anyone lied about her file. The schedule she builds does not survive contact with a bad flu week. The staff meetings run long and settle nothing. She is doing, with total sincerity and her full former competence, a job that does not reward the thing she was competent at. Swap the ward for a sales floor, a classroom, a fire hall, an engineering team, and the story does not change shape. The best individual performer becomes the department's weakest manager, on schedule, almost every time, and everyone who has worked inside an organization for more than a few years has watched it happen at least once without being able to say exactly why. This series has spent thirteen episodes asking where the competence of an organization actually goes – Parkinson found it drowned under paperwork that multiplied by its own arithmetic regardless of the work available; Michels found it converted into apparatus, the party's radicalism migrating into a formula recited at congresses while the practice protected the treasury; last episode found it denied an address altogether, dissolved into a friendship network that could not even be named by the people it governed. Those were all organizational stories – something happening to the institution as a whole. Tonight's book asks the same question one level down, about a single career, and answers it with a mechanism so mechanical that once you have heard it you cannot stop finding it. It was published as a joke. It has since been run through a hiring dataset and a physics simulation, and the joke came back confirmed.

Chapter Two: A Schoolteacher's Fieldwork

The man who noticed this first was not a management consultant. Laurence Peter started teaching in Vancouver in nineteen forty-one and spent the next two decades inside one of the most legible hierarchies available for the purpose, a public school system, watching the specific way it broke its best people. A gifted classroom teacher gets promoted to vice-principal, a job made of budgets, discipline hearings, and parent complaints, none of which the gift had anything to do with; a natural administrator is made district superintendent, where the job is politics, a different animal again. Peter earned a doctorate at Washington State in nineteen sixty-three and three years later moved to the University of Southern California, where he ran a centre for what the job title called prescriptive teaching and coordinated programs for children the system had already found difficult to place, which meant his working life was now two systems deep in the same observation: competence at one rung predicts almost nothing about the rung above it, and the promotion happens anyway, because promotion is how a hierarchy says thank you, and a hierarchy has no other language for gratitude. He needed a co-writer, and he found one in a theatre lobby. Raymond Hull, a Vancouver playwright, was complaining to Peter during an intermission about a bad play that had somehow made it to the stage, and Peter explained the mechanism to him on the spot, the same one he had been watching for twenty years: every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence. It is the kind of sentence a playwright recognizes as a good line before he recognizes it as a theory, and Hull did both, and the two of them spent the rest of the decade trying to sell it. Fourteen publishers said no over roughly two years. The idea that finally worked was to stop asking whether the book was serious. Hull wrote almost the entire manuscript, in the voice of a mock scholar founding a new discipline he calls hierarchiology, complete with a case-study apparatus, a glossary, and footnotes citing evidence the reader is clearly not meant to check – a deadpan academic costume worn over a rigorous piece of observation, the arrangement that got it past fourteen editors who could not tell whether they were buying a joke or a diagnosis. William Morrow published it in nineteen sixty-nine. It was an immediate bestseller, and both men had, without quite planning to, built the costume as a delivery mechanism, because a diagnosis this uncomfortable goes down easier wearing a lab coat that is visibly a prop.

Chapter Three: The Mechanism, Run to Its End

Strip the costume off and the argument is small enough to fit in one sentence, which is why it survived the joke: in a hierarchy, every member tends to rise to the level at which they are no longer competent, and there they remain, because there is no longer anything at that level to see and reward. Follow the logic all the way through, because the ending is the part people usually skip. Selection for promotion runs entirely on performance in the current job – it has to, since performance in a job nobody has done yet cannot be observed – so a person keeps climbing exactly as long as they keep being good at whatever they are currently doing. Sooner or later the climb reaches a job whose demands are different enough from the last one that the very skill that earned the promotion stops applying: the best technician need not have the temperament to manage technicians; the best manager may lack the political stomach to manage managers. At that rung, performance drops, and the mechanism that has been pulling this person upward for years simply has nothing left to grab. No further promotion follows an incompetent performance – organizations promote strength, not weakness – and demotion is rare enough in most institutions to round to never, because admitting the promotion was a mistake costs the person who made it more than leaving the mistake in place costs everyone else. The climb does not overshoot and correct. It overshoots by exactly one level and stops there, permanently, and Peter and Hull gave that terminal rung a name with the flat precision of an engineering term: final placement. Run this rule across every member of an organization for long enough and Peter's Corollary follows without needing a single new assumption – every post, eventually, tends to be filled by someone who has reached that state with respect to that post. That is no claim that most people are incompetent at their jobs on day one. It is about where the arrow of any sufficiently long career points, which is a claim about physics as much as about people, and it produces the sentence in the book that classroom teachers have been forwarding to each other for fifty years without needing the rest of the argument: the actual work of any organization is done by the people who have not yet reached that rung, the ones still one promotion away from their own ceiling, which means every functioning institution is, at bottom, running on borrowed time from its own newest arrivals.

Chapter Four: The Glossary, and What Each Word Is Covering For

Hierarchiology's costume comes with vocabulary, and the vocabulary is where the book stops being a single clever observation and starts being an actual taxonomy of the ways an organization manages a problem it refuses to name out loud. Percussive sublimation is the polite kick upstairs – a title with more prestige and less contact with anything that could go wrong, invented specifically to move a final-placement case out of the way of the work without ever writing the word incompetent on a form. Whyte's cheat sheet for beating the personality test, three episodes into this series, and Peter's percussive sublimation are solving the identical organizational problem from opposite ends: one is the individual's countermeasure against being correctly read, the other is the institution's countermeasure against having correctly read someone. The lateral arabesque does the same job sideways – a longer job title, an office with a window, and a portfolio emptied of anything the person could damage, dressed as a lateral move so nobody involved has to use the word demotion. And here the frames from earlier in the season click into place with an almost audible sound, because a lateral arabesque is a sinecure, and this series has met the sinecure before, twice: Parkinson found the committee that exists to have no power, and Davies found the reporting line that exists to receive complaints and generate nothing – both of them structures built, with real institutional cleverness, to hold a person or a process in a place where their native incompetence can do no further harm, which means hierarchiology's glossary is not describing a separate phenomenon from the sink and the bikeshed committee. It is describing the individual-sized version of the exact same repair, arrived at independently by an organizational theorist, a naval historian, and an economist writing forty, thirty, and ten years apart about ships, corporations, and audits. And then there is Peter's Inversion, the driest entry in the glossary and the one that should sting the most on a second listen: an employee whose internal, procedural rules eventually matter to him more than the external service the procedure was built to produce – the man who is a stickler for the form because the form is legible to him and the customer is not, promoted, of all things, for the tidiness of his desk. Run that past Michels: the SPD official whose caution protected the apparatus rather than the cause is Peter's Inversion at institutional scale, the same reflex, formalized into an oligarchy instead of into one man's in-tray.

Chapter Five: The Way Out the Book Actually Offers

The book's own answer to all of this is small on purpose, and its size is the first thing to understand about it, given the size of the problem it follows. Peter does not propose redesigning the hierarchy – no rotation scheme, no rival power center, none of the structural repairs the last two episodes spent their final chapters assembling. He proposes something an individual can do alone, on a Tuesday, without anyone's permission: creative incompetence. Once you have found the job at which you are both good and, this is the part almost every summary of the book leaves out, happy – find one visible, survivable, thoroughly harmless flaw and cultivate it in public. Park badly. Keep a memorably disorganized desk. Decline the golf outings with the partners. Something that reads to a promotion committee as evidence you have already reached your ceiling, so that they stop climbing you toward the next rung and let you keep doing the job you are actually good at. It sounds like a joke inside a book that is dressed as one, and it is also, read carefully, the only individual-level antidote this entire series has encountered. Every other repair on this shelf has been structural – a charter, a channel, a rival organized minority, a formal address for a complaint. Creative incompetence asks nothing of the institution at all. It asks the employee to defect from a promotion track built with no exit ramp, since almost no hierarchy has ever built one, and to do it by exploiting the single input the whole final-placement machine actually reads, the appearance of competence. Peter is not fixing the mechanism. He has found the one lever a single person, alone, without allies or committee votes, can reach from inside it.

Chapter Six: What the Data Did to the Joke

For most of its life this stayed what it looked like: a wickedly precise piece of satire, unfalsifiable by design, its case studies openly invented and its citations openly decorative, the kind of book a serious economist would enjoy at a dinner party and never cite. Tim Harford put the skeptic's objection into print in twenty eighteen: maybe there is no promotion error here at all, only an illusion produced by the fact that top jobs are simply harder than the jobs beneath them, so of course a manager who thrived one rung down looks worse at the top – any random person would, since the job itself, not the selection process, is what is failing them. It is a real objection, and it survived until someone finally went and checked with a large enough dataset that the illusion argument had something to fail against. Alan Benson, Danielle Li, and Kelly Shue pulled personnel and performance records from two hundred and fourteen firms' sales operations and tracked exactly what happened to salespeople after they were promoted into management on the strength of their sales numbers – the single most literal test the theory could be given, since a sales floor keeps better records than almost any other job in the economy. The finding held Peter's shape closely enough to unsettle anyone still filing this under humor: the better a salesperson had been, the more likely the promotion, and the worse, on average, that same person performed as a manager, and the firms had other information available to them – tenure, the sales team's own turnover under a given lead, prior management experience – that predicted managerial success better than sales numbers did, and used it less. That is Harford's objection answered directly rather than argued around: the job being harder cannot explain a company ignoring a better predictor it already had sitting in its own files in favor of a worse one it liked better because the worse one was easier to point to in a meeting. Two years earlier, an Italian physics team modeling promotion inside artificial hierarchies had reached the same place from a completely different direction, and their finding is the one that won the Ig Nobel Prize for management in twenty ten for being funny and true in the same sentence: when competence at one level does not reliably transfer to the next, an organization that promotes its best performers is measurably less efficient, on average, over time, than one that promotes at random, or one that alternates between promoting its best and its worst. Randomness cannot be systematically wrong about a skill that does not transfer; merit-based promotion, applied to the same skill, is systematically wrong every single time, which means the theatre-lobby joke about a play good enough to make it to a Vancouver stage turned out, decades later, to be exactly what its subtitle from later editions claims, an accurate account of why things go wrong, checked against two hundred and fourteen real sales floors and a simulation built by people with no reason to want it to be true.

Chapter Seven: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

The verdict, then, is that this is the season's missing individual case file, filed under the funniest jacket of any book on the shelf. Parkinson found the arithmetic that grows an office regardless of its workload. Michels found the apparatus that survives the cause it was built to serve. Freeman found the elite an organization cannot even locate well enough to complain to. None of them, until tonight, answered the question of what actually happens to one competent person inside a system built on those three findings, and the answer is almost tender in its mechanics: nothing is done to them. They simply stop being promoted at the exact point where the organization's own selection process runs out of information to act on, and they stay there, doing a job slightly beyond them, for the rest of their working life, while the actual output of the institution is carried by whoever has not reached that point yet. Competence does not vanish in an organization. It settles, like sediment, one final placement at a time, until the visible leadership of any sufficiently old institution is largely made of the residue of everyone's individual successes rather than a working sample of its talent. The dose is diagnostic and cheerful: read hierarchiology's glossary the way you would read a field guide, because naming percussive sublimation and the lateral arabesque out loud the next time your own organization deploys one of them is most of the cure available to a bystander, and pair the book with the Benson, Li, and Shue paper for the fifteen minutes it takes to watch the joke get its data back. And the question for Monday has the same two-part shape this series keeps returning to, because it keeps working. Find the person in your organization who is visibly straining in a role they were promoted into – not failing dramatically, just working harder than the job should require to produce ordinary results – and ask what they were good at one rung down, because that talent has not disappeared, it has simply stopped being what anyone is measuring. Then turn the question on yourself, since the book's real target was never the boss, it was the reader: is your current title the ceiling of your competence, or the floor of your next promotion into somebody else's problem, and if it is the second one, what is your version of the badly parked car – the small, survivable, entirely deliberate flaw that keeps you doing the work you are actually good at, since the hierarchy you work inside almost certainly has no exit ramp built for you and is not going to build one on your behalf. Next time, the shelf turns from what a hierarchy does to the people caught inside it to what it does to the rules it writes for itself, in David Graeber's The Utopia of Rules. Thanks for listening.
