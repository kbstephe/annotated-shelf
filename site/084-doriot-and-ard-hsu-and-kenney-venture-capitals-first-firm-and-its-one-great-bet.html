Doriot and ARD (Hsu and Kenney): venture capital's first firm and its one great bet

General Georges Doriot's American Research and Development, read through Hsu and Kenney's history of the firm and Harvard's Doriot archive: the Digital Equipment bet, a record that falls below the Dow without it, a two-thousand-dollar bonus, and what the partnership fixed and broke.

17 November 2026

Chapter One: Four Typed Pages

In January 1957 two engineers from the Lincoln Laboratory at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology sent a four-page typewritten proposal to a small investment company in Boston. Kenneth Olsen was a Navy veteran finishing a master's degree; Harlan Anderson had worked beside him on a computer built from transistors rather than vacuum tubes. They had no photocopier, so they made contact prints of the pages, and the proposal arrived reversed, white letters on a black ground. In May they followed it with a longer version, nine pages, promising a computer faster and more capacious than anything on the market, at a price of about four hundred thousand dollars, which they said was a good deal less.

The company was American Research and Development, ARD for short, and its president was Georges Doriot, a retired brigadier general who taught at Harvard Business School. ARD offered seventy thousand dollars for seventy percent of the new firm, and lent it another thirty thousand. Olsen and Anderson rented space in an old woollen mill in Maynard, Massachusetts. In the early days, Olsen remembered, they silk-screened their own circuit boards with real silk on wooden frames, made their own tools and swept their own floors. The firm was Digital Equipment Corporation, and within a decade its small machines had made it the leading maker of what came to be called minicomputers.

By 1971, when ARD distributed the last of its shares, the seventy thousand dollars had become a gain of about three hundred and fifty-five million, on the figure the firm's historians use. A reviewer of the 2008 biography of Doriot puts the final return above four hundred million. Either way it is one of the best investments in the history of the business, and the business itself, organised venture capital, is usually dated from the founding of ARD in 1946.

Patrick Liles, who studied ARD's books in the 1970s, calculated its annual return over its first twenty-five years. With Digital included, 14.7 percent compounded. Take the one company out and the figure falls to 7.4 percent, against 12.8 percent for the Dow Jones Industrial Average over the same years. A shareholder who had put money into an index fund that did not yet exist would have done better than one who backed the inventor of venture capital, on every company except one.

So which was Doriot, the man who signed the cheque in Maynard? A great judge of people who was also unlucky with most of them, or an ordinary judge who was lucky once? The series on doers has been asking of each of its subjects who could check them. His job was to check other doers, to pick strangers with nothing but an idea and decide which deserved money. The question for him is who could hold the checker to account, and what his own record could tell anyone.

The biography is Spencer Ante's Creative Capital, from 2008, and I could not open it; the copies I could find are lending-only. This episode is built instead on the firm's history written by David Hsu and Martin Kenney, published in Industrial and Corporate Change in 2005 from Doriot's own papers and ARD's annual reports, on the Doriot collection that Harvard's Baker Library has put online, and on a 2020 essay by Josh Lerner and Ramana Nanda about what venture capital became.

Chapter Two: The Professor of Manufacturing

What qualified a business-school professor to judge an engineer's computer?

Doriot was born in France in 1899. His father was an engineer for Peugeot and later ran his own car company, and the boy spent his time in the factory. He served as an artillery officer in the First World War, and afterwards his father decided that France, bled and indebted, had less to offer than America. Georges arrived at twenty-one with a plan to study at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and a letter of introduction to the president of Harvard, Lawrence Lowell. In my family, he said later, we had never heard of Harvard. Lowell steered him to the business school instead. His first job, at an affiliate of the bank Kuhn, Loeb, was assessing investments in new technologies.

By 1926 he was teaching at Harvard, and from 1937 he taught a second-year course he called Manufacturing, which ran until his compulsory retirement in 1966 and passed nearly seven thousand students through his hands. The course broke the school's rules. Harvard taught by the case method, students arguing over a written business problem; Doriot lectured, and students were told to listen and take notes but not to ask questions. Then he sent them out in teams of eight to ten to work inside two real manufacturing firms around Boston, and he made each team write a report on a technology ten or twenty years in the future. One team's report on the automatic factory, led by John Diebold, became a published book. Another, in 1962, was on masers and lasers. His guest lecturers included the industrial designer Henry Dreyfuss, the founder of Pepperidge Farm, and General Leslie Groves, whom the last episode left at the 1954 hearing.

The war gave him the rest. Doriot became an American citizen in 1940 and joined the Quartermaster Corps the next year, where by 1942 he ran research and development for the Army's supplies. That meant cold-weather boots, water-repellent cloth, rations, insecticide, and a lightweight armour of fibreglass and plastic plates developed with Dow and Monsanto and named Doron, after him. He finished a brigadier general and was called the General for the rest of his life, including by people who had never seen him in uniform. Ante's judgment, as Harvard's exhibit quotes it, is that Doriot learned to be a venture capitalist during the war, and it is easy to see why: he had spent four years finding inventors in laboratories, deciding which of their things could be made at scale, and pushing companies to make them.

So the qualification was not technical. He could not have designed a transistor circuit. What he had was a method for looking at a small manufacturing organisation from inside and judging whether it could do what it said, and he had been teaching that method to the men who would later run Boston's firms and banks for two decades. The question that followed was where the money would come from.

Chapter Three: The Scarce Thing

ARD was not one man's idea. It was the end of fifteen years of committees in New England, and their most useful conclusion was about what was actually missing.

The region was losing its industries. Cotton manufacturing in Massachusetts employed a hundred and twenty-four thousand people in 1919 and seventy-one thousand ten years later, and fewer than thirty thousand by 1940, while the Midwest built cars and radios. The Depression closed thousands of banks and small manufacturers. The reforms that followed separated deposit banking from investment banking and raised taxes on the rich, and the men who would later found ARD believed, rightly or not, that these changes had shut the old channels by which a wealthy family put money into a neighbour's new factory. Savings were flowing instead into investment trusts and insurance companies run by professionals, who bought safe things.

Ralph Flanders, who ran the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston at the end of the war and later became a senator from Vermont, put the worry in a line Hsu and Kenney quote from 1946. The country needed, he wrote, a reasonably high birth rate of new undertakings.

In 1939 a New Products Committee of the New England Council, with the president of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Karl Compton, on it and Doriot beside him, looked at the problem and concluded that New England was not short of venture capital at all. What it lacked, the committee wrote, was organisation and technique to appraise opportunities. The scarce thing was judgment.

The experiments of the next few years show how hard that was to sell. One Boston corporation founded in 1941 made a preliminary look at any firm that applied, and if it liked what it saw, charged the firm for a full investigation, a fee that a company with no cash could rarely pay; within a few years it drops out of the record. Another group, New Enterprises, produced confidential engineering reports on candidates and then left each of its members to invest or not as he pleased. By 1949 it had made five investments, and nothing is heard of it after 1950. Compton himself proposed a foundation that would live on fees for its investigations. It was never built.

Each of these designs separated the judging from the money, and each one stalled. By the end of the war the promoters had reversed the logic. If evaluation, advice and monitoring were the scarce product, the body that supplied them had better also be the investor, so that its judgment cost it something when it was wrong and paid it when it was right. That reversal made funding strangers into a job, and ARD was the first firm outside a family office built to do it.

Chapter Four: A Public Company for Private Bets

What sort of organisation could hold that job? The answer they chose shaped everything that went right and wrong for the next twenty-seven years.

The family funds that started at the same time, the Rockefeller brothers' office and J. H. Whitney's, invested family money. ARD was to raise money from strangers, and specifically from the institutions that had been soaking up savings. The trusts said they could not put money directly into small illiquid firms, because their own investors could ask for cash back at any time. They could, however, buy shares in a closed-end fund, a company whose own shares traded on the stock market, so that anyone who wanted out sold to another investor rather than demanding money from the fund. So ARD became a publicly traded investment company. It needed exemptions from the Investment Company Act of 1940, which among other things limited how much of a company a fund could own; Hsu and Kenney think the Securities and Exchange Commission granted them partly because of who was asking, and partly because the founders agreed that a large share of the first money would come from institutions.

The target was five million dollars. By February 1947 ARD had raised a little under three point six million, from investment trusts, two insurers, several universities including the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, which put in a hundred thousand, and individuals, who supplied nearly half. The minimum individual stake was five thousand dollars, to keep out anyone who could not afford to wait.

Doriot, president from December 1946, spent the next decades explaining that ARD was not what its form made it look like. In the annual report for 1951 he wrote that people had been comparing it to ordinary investment companies, and that this was a mistake: the Corporation does not invest in the ordinary sense. It creates. It risks. Results would take longer, expenses would be higher, and the eventual profits larger. He was asking a stock market that priced funds every day to judge him over decades.

That request has a cost the founders saw from the start. Fortune put it bluntly in 1952: ARD knew that if it failed, or did only passably, the experiment might not be repeated. A firm whose failure would end the whole idea has every reason to avoid failure in the short run, and in venture investing the early years are where the failures live.

Chapter Five: What the Judge Did All Day

Hsu and Kenney's tables give the work its scale. Between 1946 and 1973 ARD evaluated six thousand eight hundred and eighty-four business proposals and funded a hundred and twenty of them. In no year did it fund more than four percent of what came in. In the late 1940s chemicals and industrial equipment were two-thirds of its deals; over time it drifted into electronics, instruments and data processing. Merrill Griswold, who ran the largest investment trust in Boston and sat with the founders, told Fortune in 1952 how that drift felt from inside. Friends had complained about one longhaired science project after another and asked why ARD did not back something commercial. We learned our lesson, he said. Now we realize that our best things are longhair.

Picking was the smaller part of the job. Doriot or one of his officers typically took a seat on the board of each company ARD backed. He kept logbooks on them. In one, from 1949, on the instrument maker Tracerlab, he speculated about the effect of the founder's marriage on his work, and made a note to hire the man a maid so that he would spend less time on housework and more on the company. Before investing in Digital he reportedly insisted on meeting Olsen's wife. Where ARD had control and a company drifted, he would act: the 1950 annual report records that Circo Products, a maker of automotive tools, had run at about break-even for three years without paying down its debt, so ARD took voting control in a recapitalisation and installed a new head. Fortune reported in 1967 that critics thought he did this too slowly, and he told the magazine he found it unpleasant.

He also rationed. An undated memorandum in his papers sets out the rule that every round of money should be raised so that the next one comes cheaper, because something has been proved in between. New companies, he wrote, should start with little capital, since in inexperienced hands money tends to vanish fast, and being limited as to expenditures is a good form of training. That is staged financing, now the standard practice of the industry, written down by a professor of manufacturing as a lesson in character.

And he held on. Xerox and Hewlett-Packard both made approaches to buy Digital, and ARD would not sell. Olsen's account, quoted in Harvard's exhibit, is that ARD did not buy and sell companies at the first opportunity, that the General preached this and practised it, and that any other company would have sold when Digital was doing well and taken its profit. Doriot's own version, from the 1967 Fortune profile, was that the question of return would apply if he were a speculator. I am building men and companies, he said.

Lerner and Nanda, writing about the modern industry, list the principles that still define it: hard scrutiny and frequent rejection of plans, oversight along with the money, financing in stages, and the eventual return of capital and profit to the outside investors. They credit Doriot with articulating and practising all four. On the steelman's terms, the job he invented is the job that exists, and he did it as a teacher would, with a logbook for each pupil.

But a description of good practice is not evidence that the practice picked well, and the evidence is where the trouble starts.

Chapter Six: Two Ledgers

How would you know whether Doriot was a good judge? The obvious answer is his record, and the record is one company.

Take the shareholder's ledger first. Seven point four percent a year without Digital, below the Dow. Of the hundred and twenty companies, only eight went public. Hsu and Kenney built a rough measure of their own: did ARD's eventual sale of its stake in a company come out ahead of what it paid? About two-thirds did. That sounds respectable until you remember that a small gain on a modest stake, held for a decade by a firm with salaries to pay, can lose to an index while still counting as a gain.

The fund's form also changed what it bought. Investors in a public fund wanted dividends, and ARD needed income to pay its staff, so in its first fifteen years it leaned toward established firms that could pay interest or management fees. Only about one investment in five in that period went into a company at an early stage. In the later period, more than half did. The inventor of venture capital spent his first fifteen years doing a good deal of something closer to small-business lending, because the structure built to let strangers own his bets also needed him to show a yield. Hsu and Kenney's verdict on the early money is that requiring dividends or fees from young firms is perverse, since they are consuming capital to grow.

And the market audited him every trading day. For much of its life ARD's shares traded below the value of what it owned. At the end of 1968, Liles found, the discount was nearly thirty percent. Public investors, who could see the whole portfolio including Digital, priced the General's judgment at less than the sum of his holdings.

Now the other ledger. Venture capital's founders had set ARD four goals: to nurture new firms, to commercialise technology, to revive New England, and to show that a private firm could fund innovation profitably enough to be copied. On technology and on the region, Digital is the whole answer. It started the cluster of minicomputer firms along Route One Twenty-Eight, the ring road around Boston, and its alumni went on to found and run the next generation. On being copied, the copying happened faster than anyone planned. Arthur Rock, a student of Doriot's in Harvard's class of 1951, formed a partnership with Thomas Davis in 1961; when it was wound up in 1970 it had paid out a hundred million dollars on three million invested. Several of ARD's own officers left to start firms of their own, and the best known, Greylock, is still running.

This shelf met the same split in the episode on Xerox PARC, where whether the laboratory paid off depended on whose ledger you opened: Xerox's, or the industry's that took the laser printer and Ethernet and the windowed screen and built on them. ARD's investors got an index-minus return on everything except one company. The economy got an industry.

I am less interested in which ledger wins than in what either one can tell you about the judge. Both are dominated by a single draw. Tom Nicholas, in his 2019 history of venture capital, traces this shape back to nineteenth-century whaling voyages, where agents took a fee plus a share of profits, a third of voyages came back with nothing and a handful returned fortunes, as David Price summarises the book in his review. A business whose outcomes look like that cannot audit its own managers by results within one career. In a clinician's terms, the trial of Doriot's judgment has a hundred and twenty participants and one event, and the event explains the whole effect.

Hsu and Kenney found what signal there is in the rest. Among ninety-nine exits, investments in research-heavy firms were more likely to end in a gain, and so were investments in Massachusetts, by about twenty-one and eighteen percentage points. Both fit the claim that Doriot knew something: he did better with what he could understand by visiting, near enough to visit. Neither is strong enough to convict or acquit him. The record cannot tell you whether he was good. Which leaves the question of what else could have checked him, and the answer turns out to run through his staff.

Chapter Seven: The Two-Thousand-Dollar Bonus

In the early 1960s an ARD investment officer named Charles Waite spent a great deal of effort on a failing portfolio company called Optical Scanning. He helped revive it and take it public. Afterwards the company's founder was worth ten million dollars. Waite received a bonus of two thousand.

That was not stinginess on Doriot's part, or not only. ARD was a registered investment company, and the 1940 Act, written to prevent conflicts of interest in funds that held other people's money, forbade its staff to hold stock or options in either the fund or the companies it owned. So ARD paid salaries and bonuses, kept them low because the returns came slowly, and could offer its best people nothing like a share of what they built. Doriot fought this for years. In a confidential memorandum of 1968 he wrote that the Commission never understood, and never tried to understand, the problem of paying ARD's people, and that it was hard to persuade capable young men to work for a firm that could not offer options, or to explain why they should work hard for an ARD company rather than one where they could own a piece.

Then the alternatives arrived. In 1958 Congress created small business investment companies with cheap government loans and tax advantages, and one licensed in 1959 raised eighteen million dollars on the stock market, which made it larger than ARD on its first day. Most of those faded in the corrections of the mid-1960s. The form that lasted was the limited partnership, borrowed from oil wildcatting. It passed profits to investors without corporate tax. It paid its managers a fee for their costs and a share of the gains, usually a fifth. It had a fixed life, often ten years, after which it sold everything and returned the money, and its investors were barred from interfering. Davis and Rock was one. In 1965 two of ARD's senior officers, William Elfers and James Morgan, left to start Greylock. Henry Hoagland, who had been Doriot's aide in the war, left in 1970. Two more went in 1971.

Doriot saw the decline from inside. In a memorandum of 1964 he complained that his officers were relying on old knowledge instead of hunting for new ideas, and in 1965 that they no longer felt a heavy responsibility for bringing in good projects. Hsu and Kenney's reading, which seems right to me, is that he was describing the effect of the pay rule without quite naming it: officers who share in nothing stop searching hardest. William Elfers added another reason in his history of Greylock. Successful officers wanted to run their own firms, and at ARD that could not happen, because Doriot was the firm.

He tried to choose a successor. At the end of the 1960s he asked a former student, Thomas Perkins, then running research administration at Hewlett-Packard. Perkins declined politely; what he told Ante much later, as Mark Williams Pontin's review in Technology Review reports it, was that there was no way to make significant money because of the structure of ARD. In 1972 Perkins helped found Kleiner Perkins, as a limited partnership. The same year ARD agreed to merge into Textron, the conglomerate. Doriot judged it no longer competitive. Venture capital, he said around then, seemed to have shifted from a constructive, difficult task to a new method of speculation.

The ending has a precedent in an earlier episode. Donald Braben's Venture Research unit at BP funded scientists by conversation, worked, and ended when its patron left. Doriot's version took longer and was gentler, but it has the same shape: a judgment carried by one person, which the institution around him could not pay anyone else enough to inherit.

Chapter Eight: What the Partnership Fixed

So the obvious lesson is that Doriot had the right practice in the wrong vessel, and the partnership was the right vessel. Hsu and Kenney's conclusion says nearly that: ARD was improperly organised and sought money from the wrong institutions. I believed that for most of the reading. It is half the story.

The partnership fixed the problem that killed ARD. Managers who take a fifth of the gains have every reason to keep searching hard, and the people who were leaving ARD for partnerships were its best. It also fixed the dividend problem: the fee came out of committed capital, so nobody needed a young company to pay interest. And the fixed life made the managers return to investors every few years to raise a new fund, which is an audit of a kind. Nicholas's point, in Price's summary, is that venture firms that do well tend to keep doing well, unlike managers of public shares; whether by better access, better choosing or better advice, the persistence suggests that some judges are better than others, and the next fund-raising is where investors act on it.

Now look at what the vessel cost. ARD bought into Digital in 1957 and was still holding when it distributed the shares in 1971, fourteen years later, after turning away would-be buyers. A partnership of the modern kind, ten years long and formed in 1957, would have been obliged to sell or distribute by 1967, and the pressure to show a result would have started years before that. Lerner and Nanda argue that this clock now governs what the whole industry will fund. Because funds must exit within their life, they favour ideas whose uncertainty resolves fast, and software resolves fastest. An index they cite, of gross returns on venture deals over nearly three decades, gives software twenty-four percent a year and clean technology two. They illustrate the narrowing with one Boston firm, Charles River Ventures, whose first fund in 1970 backed birth control for dogs, high-strength fabric for balloons and turnkey systems for pig farming. Its holdings now are almost all information technology.

The fee grew with the funds. Lerner and Nanda report an estimate that most of what managers earn now comes from fees that are paid whether the fund does well or badly, which rewards raising bigger funds faster; the early partnerships negotiated a budget with their investors each year instead, and one that still does, they note, is Greylock, founded by the two men who walked out of ARD in 1965. And the governance Doriot practised has been relaxing, with founder-friendly terms and seed investors who scatter small sums across many companies and govern none. The industry calls the scattering spray and pray. It is the opposite of a logbook with a note about hiring a maid.

Doriot built three things into one firm: the judgment, the patience and the governance. The vessel that succeeded him kept the judgment, solved the pay, and added a clock; the clock narrowed what could be judged, and the scale of the fees thinned the governance. And the question this series keeps asking has an answer for him that is less flattering than the legend and more interesting. A judge of doers whose outcomes are this skewed cannot be audited by his record. He can be audited only on his process, the scrutiny, the stages, the boards and the willingness to wait, and those are what the market for his services stopped paying for, because the market sees outcomes. ARD's shareholders priced him by the portfolio and gave him a discount. His officers judged him by the pay and left. The one audit that went his way, Digital, was a single draw he could not have repeated on demand, and he never claimed he could.

Chapter Nine: The Case Against the General

The strongest objection is that this essay has let Doriot's process off too easily, on the grounds that it cannot be checked.

His own memoranda say his deal flow aged. In the 1960s ARD put a smaller share of its money into research-heavy firms, the kind that had made its gains, and Hsu and Kenney suspect part of the reason was that Digital had grown so large that Doriot wanted to diversify away from it, and part was that his officers had stopped finding the best technology deals. A judge whose process is his asset, and whose process decays while the hits keep the reported numbers up, is a familiar figure in any field that publishes only its winners.

The paternalism is part of the same objection. Meeting a founder's wife before investing, a lecture for the wives of his students on their part in their husbands' careers, a note on a founder's marriage: these were the habits of a man who saw his companies as pupils. Pupils grow up. The firms that left ARD were partly escaping its pay, and partly escaping him.

Then the comparison that started this episode. Seven point four percent against 12.8 for the Dow is a real failure for the people whose money it was, and the institutions that bought into ARD in 1946 did not do so to found an industry. Hsu and Kenney's own judgment is that the closed-end form was a design error and that the mutual funds, as they tell it, never became an important source of venture money. The defence that ARD's mission was to demonstrate the model, so that losing to the index while inspiring Arthur Rock counts as success, is the kind of defence every underperforming fund would like to make.

My verdict takes the objection seriously and still lands on Doriot's side, for one reason. My guess, and it is only a guess, is that 7.4 percent is near what a careful judge earns in that business without the one event, and in a business shaped like whaling, the honest test of a judge is whether he was in position to catch the event, and whether he held on once he had. Doriot read four typed pages, met the wife, bought seventy percent, refused Xerox and waited fourteen years. The process that produced that is the one he wrote down. Pontin, reviewing Ante for Technology Review, ended with Hsu's line that by the time ARD sold itself to Textron, venture capital had become part of the economy and ARD simply slipped out of existence with its historical mission accomplished. That is generous, and I would add one clause to it: the form that accomplished the mission was not the form that inherited it.

Chapter Ten: What to Read, and a Test

Start with Hsu and Kenney, whose paper Kenney posts free on his university page; its sections on compensation and on managing portfolio firms carry the Doriot memoranda quoted here. Harvard's Baker Library put its Doriot exhibit online, with the Digital proposal and the wartime work. Lerner and Nanda's essay, in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2020 and as a free working paper, is the present tense of this story. Ante's biography is the obvious next step, and I have not read it.

The listener of this show directs a fellowship and decides how its early-career physicians spend their protected time and which of their projects go forward. That is a small venture portfolio, with ARD's two problems: the outcomes are skewed, and the record is short. Suppose a programme has trained twelve fellows over a decade, and one of them now runs a large funded group that every brochure mentions. Take that one person out, and if the programme's record then looks like anyone's, the record was never evidence about choosing. What can be checked is the process Doriot wrote down: whether each pilot got its money in stages, each stage meant to prove one thing, and whether someone sat with each fellow often enough to keep a logbook. A director who kept those notes would have something the alumni list cannot give, a record of judgment that does not wait for the one event.
