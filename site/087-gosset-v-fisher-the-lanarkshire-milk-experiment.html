The Lanarkshire Milk Experiment (Student and Fisher): teachers, twins and where the coin goes

Twenty thousand Scottish schoolchildren, a pint of milk a day, and teachers who rebalanced the groups by eye: how a brewer's nine-page critique and a lifelong quarrel with Fisher over field plots settled what an allocation plan must promise.

20 November 2026

Chapter One: The Head Teacher's Afterthought

Early in 1930, in sixty-seven schools across Lanarkshire, head teachers drew up two lists of children. One list would get a ration of milk every school day for four months. The other would get nothing extra and be measured all the same. The teachers had been told how to choose. In some schools they drew lots, in others they went down the register alphabetically, and both methods would have been fine. Then the instructions allowed one more step. Where the lottery or the alphabet had given a group an undue share of well-fed or ill-nourished children, the teacher could swap some of them, in order to obtain, as the official report put it, a more level selection.

They swapped. When the children were weighed and measured at the start, the controls, chosen to go without milk, were taller and heavier than the feeders who would receive it, by an amount equal to about three months' growth in weight and four in height. Nobody had set out to do this. A teacher looking at a thin child from a poor family, and at a sturdy child whose father was in work, and asked to make the groups fair, had made a choice almost anyone would make. The poorer children got the milk.

The question under this episode is who should decide which patient, or pupil, or plot of barley gets the treatment. The usual answer now is a coin, or its electronic descendant, and the usual story is that Ronald Fisher won that argument against everyone who preferred judgment. What happened is stranger. The most careful public critic of the Lanarkshire teachers was William Sealy Gosset, the brewer who wrote as Student, and for the rest of his life he was also Fisher's most stubborn opponent on randomisation. Both men read the milk experiment the same way. They fought, bitterly by the end, over a field of barley.

This series follows the statistical analysis plan, the document a trialist signs before unblinding, through the arguments that produced each of its promises. The last episode was about the promise to test something. This one is about two promises that sit together in the allocation section: how the coin will be used, and that the analysis will respect whatever the coin was allowed to do.

Chapter Two: Twenty Thousand Children

The experiment was run by the Department of Health for Scotland, and its report, by Gerald Leighton and Peter McKinlay, came out from His Majesty's Stationery Office the same year. The design is easiest to hold as two halves. Ten thousand children got milk, five thousand raw and five thousand pasteurised, both of the best tuberculin-tested grade. Another ten thousand were controls. Each school chose between two hundred and four hundred children and split them into feeders and controls, and each school got only one kind of milk. That last restriction was administrative. Nobody wanted to bet that a teacher could hand the right bottle to each of two hundred children every morning for four months.

The report concluded three things. Milk made children grow faster in both height and weight. The benefit did not differ much between boys and girls, or between younger and older children. And raw and pasteurised milk were, as far as anyone could judge, equal. That last finding mattered to the dairy trade and to public health, since pasteurisation cost money and its critics claimed it destroyed something nourishing.

Within months the third conclusion had been challenged. An agricultural scientist, Stephen Bartlett, published a reanalysis in the Journal of the Ministry of Agriculture, and in April 1931 he and Fisher, then at Rothamsted, the agricultural research station north of London, wrote a short note in Nature arguing that the report's claim of equality was open to some question. Their reading of the tables favoured raw milk, in both height and weight.

That note seems to be what drew Gosset in. Stephen Senn, the statistician whose 2023 paper in the European Journal of Epidemiology reconstructs the episode from Egon Pearson's biography of Gosset, gives the sequence. In mid-July 1931 Gosset told Karl Pearson he was reading the report and the Nature note; nine days later he sent a draft, and after a quick exchange of detailed comments the proofs went back in August. The paper appeared in Pearson's journal Biometrika that December. Nine pages, and Senn, a man not given to gush, says one can only marvel at the speed.

Gosset was fifty-five. He had spent his whole working life at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, where he had become head experimental brewer, and his employer let him publish only under a pseudonym. Stephen Ziliak, the economist who has spent years in the Guinness archive, reports that the name may come from the notebook he carried, which bore the imprint The Student's Science Notebook. Ziliak also reports the brewery's first assessment of him in 1899, made by its managing director, which ends with the words "Seems generally speaking suitable." He had invented the t test in 1908 to deal with samples of barley and hops too small for the statistics of his day. He was also, by every account, kind; a childhood friend credited him with an immovable foundation of niceness. Those details help explain the paper's tone. It opens by praising the organisation of the whole enterprise, and says the author would like to be wise after the event only so that next time the light thrown by the defects can be used.

Chapter Three: Clothes in February

Then he takes the experiment apart.

He starts with the school restriction. Since no school got both kinds of milk, raw and pasteurised were tested on different children in different schools, which may not have been comparable groups. Then comes the teachers' substitution, which he calls a fatal mistake. He does not accuse anyone. He suggests the teachers were swayed by the very human feeling that the poorer children needed the milk more, and unconsciously put too many of the ill-nourished among the feeders.

Next, a coat. The children were weighed in their indoor clothes, first in February and again in June. In a Scottish school in 1930, a child's February clothes weigh more than its June clothes, so every child's recorded weight gain is its true gain minus whatever clothing it shed. If feeders and controls had been chosen at random, that error would have hit both groups alike and cancelled in the comparison. But Gosset has just argued that the feeders were poorer. Poorer children have fewer clothes to shed. So the controls, the better-off children, would lose more recorded weight to the change of season than the feeders did, and the milk would look better on the scales than it really was.

That is a hypothesis, and he tests it with the report's own numbers. Height has no clothing problem; nobody grows shorter in June. Weight does. So if the clothes are inflating the milk effect, the milk should look much more powerful for weight than for height. It does. Relative to the controls' growth, milk added about eight percent to the boys' height gain and ten percent to the girls'. For weight it added thirty and forty-five percent.

He pushes further. Little children have small wardrobes and few layers to discard; older children, he guesses, wear about a pound more clothing in February than in June. So if he is right, the apparent milk benefit for weight should be modest in the youngest children and enormous in the oldest, while height should show no such pattern. Using Bartlett's condensed tables, Gosset splits the children into three age bands. In the youngest band, the milk's advantage was nine to thirteen percent of the controls' weight gain. In the oldest it was seventy-three to seventy-eight percent. Height stayed small and roughly flat across the ages throughout. He also plotted the averages, and in his diagrams the June weights of the older children drop below where any growth curve should put them. The report's authors had found that older pupils benefited more from milk, at least in weight. Gosset's reading is that they had more to take off.

So by the end of page five he has an explanation for every peculiar feature of the data that needs no milk at all, except for a modest effect on height, which he thinks probably real. He believes milk helps children grow. What he will not accept is that this experiment showed it. The conclusion, he writes, has been moved from scientific inference to "the less satisfactory foundation of mere authority and guesswork."

Chapter Four: The Randomiser's Note

His last objection concerns the controls. All ten thousand had been pooled into one group, so the raw-milk children were being compared partly with controls from pasteurised schools, and the other way round. With sixty-seven schools, Gosset writes, at best thirty-three against thirty-four, in a district so mixed racially and socially, the two sets of schools could easily have differed both in the children's starting size and in how fast they grew. A pooled control fits neither.

Senn adds a detail that makes this worse. The report never says how many schools got which milk. Students of the experiment, Gosset included, have assumed the split was about even. Senn notes that of the milk-drinkers who provided data, forty-five point six percent drank raw milk, which, if the schools were of similar size, implies thirty-one raw schools and thirty-six pasteurised. Nobody knows. And the probable errors the report printed, its measure of uncertainty, were almost certainly calculated as if the children were one big sample rather than pupils grouped in schools.

Here the roles cross. Fisher, the man about to spend the decade insisting that only a randomised experiment gives a valid estimate of error, had signed a note in Nature drawing a firm conclusion about raw milk from groups that had not been randomised to it. Gosset restates their reasoning politely: if the raw and pasteurised groups had been random samples from one population, the differences would be decisive. Then he answers that they were not; they were selected samples from populations that may have differed, compared with controls appropriate to neither. He would be, in his word, chary of any conclusion. He adds that he personally believes raw milk is better. He just does not think this experiment could prove it.

Senn's assessment of the Fisher and Bartlett note, from a man who admires Fisher, is that it is not particularly impressive. In fairness, he adds, they had only the printed summaries to work from. But the roles in 1931 are the reverse of the ones history assigns. The brewer who distrusted the coin was demanding one. The champion of the coin had reasoned as if it had been tossed.

Chapter Five: Fifty Pairs of Twins

Gosset's paper ends with two proposals, and the first is not what a sceptic of randomisation is supposed to write. If the experiment is repeated on the same grand scale, he says, the teachers should choose children in pairs, same sex, same age group, and as alike as possible in height, weight and, above all, how well nourished they look. Then a coin should be tossed for each pair to decide which child gets the milk. Each pair becomes a unit, and the error is calculated from the differences within pairs. The teachers would use everything they knew about the children, and then the final choice would be taken out of their hands.

The second proposal is the one people remember. Among twenty thousand children there will be twins. Counting from the twin birth rate and the size of Lanarkshire's school population, he expects a couple of hundred pairs, about fifty of them identical. Give one twin raw milk and the other pasteurised, a coin deciding which. Weigh them weekly, without clothes. Since mischievous twins will swap places, mark them, and since fingerprinting is associated in some minds with crime, use an indelible mark on a fingernail that will grow out when the experiment ends. Fifty pairs of identical twins, he says, would give more reliable results than the twenty thousand, at a tiny fraction of the money and the trouble.

That claim sounds like a brewer's boast, and the arithmetic shows it is not. The numbers that follow are made up to show it, except one. When Ethel Elderton later matched children from the original cards, the spread in four-month weight gain from child to child was about twenty ounces, in feeders and controls alike. Suppose that held across all twenty thousand. Compare ten thousand feeders with ten thousand controls and the standard error of the difference, the usual measure of how far chance alone could push it, is about a quarter of an ounce. But Gosset has just shown that the comparison carries a bias from clothing alone that could easily run to several ounces in the older children, plus whatever growth difference came from the teachers' choice of poorer children. A quarter-ounce error bar around a number that may be off by eight ounces is a precise wrong answer.

Now the twins. Suppose the difference in weight gain between two identical twins, fed the same and dressed alike, has a spread of eight ounces, a guess on the generous side. Fifty such pairs give a standard error of a little over an ounce. Four times wider than the big trial's. But it is an ounce around the truth, because the coin, not the teacher, decided which twin drank which milk, and because there are no clothes on the scale.

Both men would have signed what follows from the contrast. A standard error measures only the uncertainty that chance introduces. It says nothing about the bias the design let in, and it shrinks with more children while the bias stays put.

Chapter Six: Matching After the Fact

Someone did try to rescue the data. Karl Pearson borrowed the original record cards from the Department of Health for Scotland, and his colleague Ethel Elderton published a reanalysis in the Annals of Eugenics in 1933. Her method was Gosset's pairing done in reverse. For each child who had received milk, she searched the cards for a control of the same sex and year of birth with the same starting height, a starting weight within four ounces, and an age within a month. There were too few matches, so she relaxed the weight limit to eight ounces, justifying it by a study of twins that had found identical pairs differing by as much as ten.

Her findings are cautious. Children who had milk generally gained more height than matched controls. In weight the picture was messier, and her controls barely gained at all. Over four months a child of that age should put on more than a pound. Her control boys gained, on average, under twelve ounces and her control girls under nine, and a fifth of the boys and a quarter of the girls recorded a loss. Gosset's coats again. On raw against pasteurised, matched directly, she found no evidence that either was better.

Pearson then added an appendix of his own, combining her age groups into single probabilities, and concluded that raw milk had a special advantage for the height of girls but not of boys. He offered a physiological speculation about why: perhaps young male and female bones need different constituents from milk. It is a passage that reads now like a warning label for analysis after the fact. Of the four raw-against-pasteurised comparisons he ran, one came out in favour of raw milk, in one sex, in one measure, and a theory of sexual difference in bone growth arrived to explain it.

Elderton herself saw her method's limit and said so plainly. To find a control with the same height and weight as each milk child, she could use only average children. The very short and very light, and the very tall and heavy, had no partners and dropped out. Her matched sample was roughly thirty to forty percent less variable than Glasgow children of the same age. And the children dropped were those most likely to show a benefit from milk, the undergrown ones the teachers had been trying to feed. She ends by heartily endorsing Gosset's proposal: pairs chosen by the teachers, a coin for each pair.

So two careful analysts, given the cards of twenty thousand children, could not recover what one coin per pair would have given them for free. Matching after the fact can balance the things you measured. The teachers had balanced on something nobody wrote down, a look of being poorly fed, and no later analysis could find it on the cards.

Chapter Seven: A Field of Barley

If Gosset and Fisher agreed about Lanarkshire, what did they fight about? Barley, and a very specific question: once you know which way the soil gets better, do you still need the coin?

Gosset's working life was spent testing varieties of barley for Guinness, with the barley breeder Edwin Beaven, on real farms where fertility drifts across a field from one hedge to the other. Their favoured layout was the half-drill strip. To compare two varieties, call them A and B, you sow long strips side by side in the order A, B, B, A, then repeat. The pattern balances any steady slope in fertility: the first and fourth positions sum to five, the second and third to five, so neither variety gets the better end of the field on average. It is also easy to sow with a seed drill, which matters to a farmer. Gosset had been recommending it since a 1923 paper in Biometrika.

Fisher, by then at Rothamsted, had been building the opposite principle into the foundations of experimental design. Group the plots into compact blocks, so that neighbouring plots are alike; then, within each block, let chance decide which plot gets which treatment. The two men argued in letters for about ten years, and in 1936 they argued in public. At a Royal Statistical Society meeting on cooperative trials, Gosset gave Fisher credit for introducing randomisation fifteen years before, a method that gave a certainly valid estimate of the variability of results, though, he added, usually at the cost of making that variability larger than balanced arrangements would. Fisher replied that the actual errors of the half-drill method were always unknown, and that no standard error attached to it by some plausible process could ever be on the same scientific level as one of known validity.

That exchange can sound like a dispute over taste. Fisher's argument, set out in The Design of Experiments, first published in 1935, is mechanical, and it runs like this. Imagine a field sown with a single variety, so that every difference in yield is soil. The total variation in that field is fixed. Fisher's analysis splits it into the part that differs between blocks, the part that lines up with whatever you labelled as treatments, and the remainder, which is what you use to estimate error. Now suppose a clever agronomist arranges the labels so that the two treatments get equal shares of good and bad soil. He has made the treatment comparison more accurate. But the total has not changed, so whatever variation he removed from the treatment line has gone into the remainder, and the estimate of error rises. His experiment is more precise than it looks, and will be believed less. If his arrangement is unlucky and lines up with the soil, the opposite happens: the real error grows while its estimate shrinks, and he will believe a result he should not. Either way the error estimate no longer measures the error. Fisher's point is that a systematic design can be good or bad and the experiment cannot tell you which.

Chapter Eight: Five Thousand Eight Hundred and Seventy-Five Grams

The cleanest test came in a five-page paper Fisher published that same year, 1936, with the Polish geneticist Stefan Barbacki, in the Annals of Eugenics, a journal Fisher edited. Its title gives its tone: a test of the supposed precision of systematic arrangements. They opened by noting that pairing is often wrongly credited to Student, who had expressly disclaimed inventing it.

They took a uniformity trial reported by the American agronomist Gustav Wiebe: fifteen hundred rows of wheat, all one variety, grown in Idaho in 1927 and harvested row by row. They grouped the rows into strips, as a half-drill trial would, and lettered them A, B, B, A down the field. There was no treatment. A and B were the same wheat.

The A strips outyielded the B strips by five thousand eight hundred and seventy-five grams, about 1.7 percent of the average yield, from labels painted on identical wheat. Then they computed what the systematic layout's own analysis would have claimed as its standard error: 0.66 percent. A difference more than two and a half times its own claimed error, in a field where the true difference was zero.

Had each group of four strips been ordered at random instead, ABBA or BAAB by the toss of a coin, the standard error would have come out a little larger, and it would have been a valid one. On this field, the balanced design did both of the bad things Fisher's algebra predicted at once: a larger real error, and a smaller estimate of it.

Gosset's reply took him the last months of his life. In September 1937 he was made head brewer of all Guinness. He died of a heart attack in October, at sixty-one, and his paper comparing balanced and random arrangements appeared in Biometrika the following year. Its argument, as Ziliak quotes it, grants Fisher's arithmetic and refuses his conclusion. It is true, Gosset writes, that over all possible arrangements exactly as many misleading conclusions will be drawn as the tables allow, and anyone prepared to spend a blameless life repeating the experiment would confirm it. But it would be pedantic to keep using an arrangement known beforehand to be likely to mislead. So there is a dilemma: either you sometimes run experiments you know are likely to give misleading results, or you abandon the strict applicability of the tables. And once the tables are abandoned, why not avoid as many misleading results as possible by balancing? He states what balance costs him in plain terms: balanced arrangements more often fail to call small differences significant, and make up for it by catching large ones.

Rosemary Bailey and Anthony Atkinson, in their annotated history of design papers in Biometrika, call this a very important paper and gives its gist: balance for a suspected trend gives smaller bias in the estimate of the treatment effect but tends to overestimate the error unless the trend is fitted. Gosset's conclusion was to balance. Fisher's was to block and randomise.

Chapter Nine: Where the Coin Goes

Put the milk and the barley side by side, and the two quarrels turn out to be one argument conducted at two temperatures.

In Lanarkshire, Gosset asked teachers to use their knowledge first, by forming pairs of similar children, and chance second, by tossing a coin within each pair. In The Design of Experiments, Fisher's own first worked example is Charles Darwin's experiment on maize, fifteen pairs of plants, crossed against self-fertilised, grown in the same pots. Fisher's instruction is that once the sites for each pair are chosen, and every environmental difference within them is fixed, one should assign at random, as by tossing a coin, which site gets the crossed plant. Pairs chosen by knowledge; a coin within each pair. It is the same design. Senn makes the point about the barley from the other direction: had Gosset proposed choosing at random among the six possible orders of two A strips and two B strips in each block of four, Fisher would have had no objection.

So the disagreement narrows to the last inch. Both men said: use everything you know to make the comparison fair, by pairing, blocking and grouping like with like. Both said the analysis must then follow the grouping, so the error is estimated from differences within pairs or blocks. Where they parted was the leftover: whether, once you have balanced on what you know, a coin is still needed to protect you from what you do not. Gosset thought a known fertility slope made the coin a costly formality. Fisher thought the coin was the only thing that made the standard error mean anything. On the barley, I side with Fisher, and Barbacki's wheat is the reason: a field with no treatment in it at all produced, under Gosset's layout, a confident 1.7 percent result.

Lanarkshire shows how the two failures combine. The teachers did balance, by eye, on how well fed children looked. That was the step both men allowed. But they did it after the lottery, in place of the lottery, and on a characteristic nobody recorded. So the coin was gone, and the grouping it was supposed to protect could never be put into the analysis. Then the analysts made it worse: the report pooled the controls across schools that got different milks, calculated errors as if the schools did not exist, and never said how many schools got which. Elderton, with the cards in hand, also ignored the schools. Even Gosset worked from Bartlett's condensed tables and said in a note that his own errors were only as good as Bartlett's reading of the report.

The last episode ended with Paul Meehl's potash: a fertiliser trial is a strong test because, with randomised plots, a difference in yield has nowhere to come from but the fertiliser. Lanarkshire is what happens when a kindly farmer chooses which plots get the potash. The statistical test still runs; it just stops being about potash.

Senn sums up Fisher's defence of randomisation in a phrase: as ye randomise, so shall ye analyse. Gosset's version would have stressed the first half more, use what you know when you allocate, and Fisher's the second.

Chapter Ten: The Case Against

Against Gosset, the case is Barbacki's wheat, and it is strong. His half-drill strips balanced a straight-line slope and nothing else, and he knew it; he wrote that periodic fertility slopes might occur but, apart from those due to the works of man, must be rare enough to ignore. Fields contain old drains, headlands and past cropping, and hospitals contain wards, shifts and the day of the week. Senn notes a subtler habit too: Gosset regarded a conservative error estimate as a virtue. Fisher's reply, in the Barbacki paper, is that this is a back-handed compliment, because an overcautious error passes over real results, and the trial's cost is then spent on nothing.

Against Fisher, the first charge is that he did not always follow his own rule. His Lanarkshire note is one example. Senn gives another, from 1944: a paper Fisher signed on vitamin C, comparing two groups of men who had not been randomised, having dropped a third group and chosen how to split the results after seeing the data. The second charge is harder. Fisher argues about the validity of the error estimate over all possible arrangements. Gosset cares about the one trial in front of you. Once you have tossed the coin and seen that, by bad luck, nearly all the A plots are on the poor side of the field, it is cold comfort that over many tosses this happens rarely. Fisher's answer, blocking, deals with the imbalances you anticipated. The ones you did not anticipate are exactly where the coin protects you, and exactly where you cannot check whether it has. This is the territory the next episode takes up.

Gosset's modern champion, Stephen Ziliak, goes further than the record on one point. He writes that randomisation biases the estimate of the treatment difference, and illustrates it with a random layout that happens to put most of one variety on the poor soil. That is bad luck in one draw, not bias; averaged over draws, the random layout is centred on the truth, which is Fisher's whole argument.

Finally, Senn's own modern lesson cuts against the comfort that randomisation fixes everything. He compares Lanarkshire with a drug trial called TARGET, about eighteen thousand osteoarthritis patients in more than eight hundred centres, in which some centres compared lumiracoxib with naproxen and others lumiracoxib with ibuprofen. Like the milk schools, it was really two studies sharing one drug. Unlike Lanarkshire, the analysis kept each comparison with its own control. And Senn ends on observational epidemiology: many studies, he suspects, contain hidden clusters, like Lanarkshire's undisclosed schools, that make the true uncertainty larger than the printed one. That would explain why such studies disagree with each other by more than their standard errors allow.

Chapter Eleven: Verdict

Start with Gosset's nine pages on Lanarkshire, all of them, including the twins and the fingernails; nothing in them is wasted, and they read like a detective's report. Then read Fisher's sections on the bias of systematic arrangements and on systematic squares in The Design of Experiments, about ten pages, where the fixed-total argument is set out; and Barbacki and Fisher's five pages, for the wheat. Senn's 2004 paper in Statistics in Medicine, called Added Values, gives the fairest short account of the quarrel in its second section, and his 2023 paper is the best modern reading of Lanarkshire. Ziliak's two papers, in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2008 and the Journal of Wine Economics in 2011, are lively and partisan; read them for Gosset the man and the brewery archive, and read Senn and Barbacki for the barley.

My own view is that Gosset was right about Lanarkshire, wrong about barley, and right about something underneath both that Fisher's side tended to forget. Knowledge used at allocation is not the enemy of the coin. The teachers' mistake was to use it after the coin, by eye, unrecorded, instead of before the coin and on paper.

Picture a head teacher in a modern trial with a thin child in front of her and a list that says the child goes without. The trial is invented: a school-feeding study that randomises forty schools. Twenty get a daily meal and twenty do not; the schools are first paired on enrolment and on the share of children who are underweight at baseline, and a coin decides within each pair. The allocation section of the plan says that. The analysis section must then say three more things, each one a Lanarkshire failure turned into a promise. The school is the unit of analysis, so the error comes from differences between schools, not children. The pairs used in the allocation appear in the primary model. And any departure from the allocation list, a head teacher who moves a child into or out of the meal group, is recorded with the reason and analysed in the arm the coin assigned. The first two promises came from Fisher. The last came from Lanarkshire, where the head teacher's afterthought was never written down.
