Scott Alexander, Meditations on Moloch: the coordination-failure engine that explains why nobody in a bad system is the villain

A 2014 essay's argument that arms races, races to the bottom, and rigged markets share one mechanism with no operator, tested against the antibiotics that took sixty years to get off American farms, and against the charge that the whole picture conveniently erases who profits from letting it run.

10 September 2026

Chapter One: The Farms That Couldn't Stop

In the early nineteen fifties, farmers feeding their livestock a low, constant dose of antibiotics discovered something nobody had gone looking for: the animals grew faster on less feed. Nobody could fully explain why. It didn't matter why. A farmer who fed his herd the drug got fatter animals to market sooner than a neighbor who didn't, and a market that pays by the pound doesn't ask a rancher to explain his biochemistry before it pays him. Within a decade the practice had spread across the American livestock industry. No single farmer had to think it was wise. A farmer who refused to do it was simply competing, at a real and measurable disadvantage, against every farmer who did.

By two thousand fourteen the United States Food and Drug Administration was reporting that livestock accounted for roughly eighty percent of all antibiotics sold in the country, some seventeen thousand tons a year, most of it going into healthy animals that had nothing wrong with them, purely to make them grow. Doctors and public health researchers had been warning since at least the nineteen seventies that dosing an entire national herd with low, constant antibiotic exposure was close to the best possible design for breeding resistant bacteria: a slow drip that kills the weak strains and hands the survivors, the ones already carrying resistance genes, the run of an empty field. The warning was not obscure. The British government's own advisory committee said as much in nineteen sixty-nine. And still the practice ran for another forty-eight years before a federal rule, Guidance for Industry number two thirteen, finally took the medically important drugs off the growth-promotion market on the first of January, twenty seventeen, and sales for that specific purpose fell, in a single year, from five point seven million kilograms to zero.

Ask why it took that long and the answer has nothing to do with any single person along the chain being careless or corrupt. A veterinarian who told a client to stop dosing his herd was giving him correct advice that would cost him money the day he took it, before any collective benefit to the human species could possibly reach him. A regulator who moved too fast risked a rule nobody in the industry would treat as anything but an attack on their livelihood, from ranchers who had done nothing differently from every rancher around them going back three generations. Everyone downstream was borrowing against a shared resource, the future usefulness of penicillin and its descendants, and no single farmer's restraint could have protected that resource on its own, since the bacteria on the next farm over don't respect a property line. This is the shape of problem Scott Alexander spent an essay in the summer of twenty fourteen trying to name properly, and the name he settled on was older than agriculture itself.

Chapter Two: The Vision on Nob Hill

On the night of October the seventeenth, nineteen fifty-four, a twenty-eight-year-old poet named Allen Ginsberg was in his girlfriend's apartment on San Francisco's Nob Hill, working through a peyote high, when he looked out the window and saw the Sir Francis Drake Hotel a few blocks away transform, in the fog, into a lit, skull-faced monster staring back at him. He rushed downstairs into a cafeteria and started writing. What came out of that night became the second section of "Howl," the poem that would make his name the following year and put his publisher on trial for obscenity the year after that. In it he gave the monster a name pulled from the Hebrew Bible: Moloch, the god to whom the ancient Canaanites were said to sacrifice their own children by fire, in exchange for victory in war or a good harvest. Ginsberg's Moloch became a stand-in for everything about mid-century American civilization he thought was devouring its young: the arms race, the asylum, the factory, the bank.

Scott Alexander's essay takes Ginsberg's monster and gives it a job description rather than a mood. "He is the god of child sacrifice," Alexander writes, and goes on to describe him as the furnace a person feeds their own children into for the promise of winning a war. Read the sentence as biography and it's a description of a bronze age cult. Read it as economics and it describes something considerably more contemporary: any system in which the actors competing inside it can improve their odds by discarding something they'd rather keep, and where any actor who declines to discard it simply loses the competition to one who will. Nobody worships this god on purpose. Everybody feeds him anyway, because the alternative to feeding him is losing, and losing is the one outcome the system actually punishes.

Chapter Three: The Man Who Already Owed You a Paragraph

The essay's author writes under the name Scott Alexander, itself a near-anagram of the blog he wrote it for, Slate Star Codex, and for most of the blog's life that pseudonym was doing real work: the man behind it, Scott Siskind, is a psychiatrist practicing in the San Francisco Bay Area, and he kept his clinical name off a blog that discussed, among a great many other things, drugs, sex, and his own patients' cases in generalized form. He started writing in twenty thirteen, built an audience in the low hundreds of thousands mostly through word of mouth among programmers, academics, and doctors, and became one of the few bloggers of his generation whose individual essays functioned as shared reference points the way a well-known book does, cited by strangers who had never read anything else he'd written. In June of two thousand twenty, told that a New York Times reporter intended to publish his legal name against his wishes, he deleted the entire blog rather than let the story land as anything but his own decision, then relaunched seven months later under his own name on a new platform, Astral Codex Ten, once the choice to be identified had become his rather than a newspaper's.

This show has actually already quoted him once, seventeen episodes ago, without properly introducing him: Scott Alexander wrote a widely read review of James Scott's "Seeing Like a State," and that review supplied one strand of the case against high modernism this feed built into its own account of that book. He earns a full episode now for the same reason a magazine profile eventually earns its subject a cover: enough of what he's written has kept turning up in enough other people's arguments, years after publication, that treating him only as a source for somebody else's case has started to undersell him.

Chapter Four: The Trap, Defined

Strip the essay's examples away and the underlying shape is almost insultingly simple. Take any competition that rewards one measurable outcome, whether it's speed to market, military strength, or a fatter cow. Somewhere inside that competition sits an opportunity to trade away some other value, one the competition doesn't measure or reward, for a better score on the value it does. The competitors who take that trade do better in the competition than the competitors who don't. So the ones who refuse the trade lose ground to the ones who take it, generation after generation or quarter after quarter, until refusing simply isn't a viable long-term strategy anymore. Alexander calls the resulting arrangement a multipolar trap, and the "multipolar" part matters: nobody is in charge of it. A monopolist who behaves badly can be regulated, boycotted, or replaced. This kind of arrangement has no single actor whose choice would fix it, because any one competitor who unilaterally stopped feeding it would simply be out-competed by everyone who kept feeding it, and the whole thing would carry on unchanged with one fewer name in it.

The economist Martin Shubik built a small laboratory version of the same mechanism in nineteen seventy-one that Alexander uses to show the logic at its most naked. Shubik's dollar auction auctions off an actual dollar bill, with an ordinary rule and one vicious addition: the highest bidder wins the dollar, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their own losing bid and gets nothing for it. Bidding starts innocently, five cents, ten cents, each bidder trying to win a dollar for less than a dollar. Once the bids pass fifty cents, though, the second-place bidder is now facing a guaranteed loss either way, and the only question is how large: drop out and lose the fifty cents outright, or bid ninety-five cents and lose only a nickel if you win, or lose almost a dollar if you don't. Real auctions run this way have closed above twenty dollars for the same single dollar bill, both finalists trapped by a structure that makes escalation individually rational at every step even as it makes both parties worse off collectively across the whole climb. Nobody at the table is stupid or greedy in any unusual sense. Both are doing the locally correct thing, over and over, and the local correctness is exactly what drives the total outcome off a cliff.

Chapter Five: Two Traps Run to Their Ends

Move the same logic onto a fish farm and the abstraction gets a face. A fish farmer operating in a shared bay or river can, if he chooses, crowd his pens tighter than is healthy for the fish, dose the water with antibiotics to control the resulting disease, and let the runoff of waste and drug residue flow downstream into water everyone else's farm also draws from. He gets more fish per acre of water than a competitor who spaces his pens properly and skips the drugs. His downstream neighbors, meanwhile, absorb a fouled river they had no say in fouling, and if enough farmers upstream make the same individually profitable choice, the whole river eventually can't support healthy fish at any density, at which point everyone loses, including the farmer who moved first. He captured the early gain. The bill arrived later, addressed to everybody, including him.

The second case Alexander runs comes from closer to home for most of his American readers, and it borrows its name from the legal scholar Elizabeth Warren's research into family bankruptcy: the two-income trap. A single generation ago, in many American cities, one working parent's income was enough to buy a house in a school district good enough to educate the children reasonably well. Then some families started sending both parents into the workforce, and with two incomes bidding on the same finite stock of houses zoned into the same good school districts, prices in those districts rose to absorb the second income, the way any fixed supply absorbs new demand. A family with a single earner competing for the same house is now simply priced out of it, so the choice to send the second parent to work, made freely by one family after another for reasons that sound entirely sensible taken alone, has rewritten, one household at a time, the entry price for the whole competition. Every family that opts in gains, relative to families that don't, exactly nothing, because the good school district was never actually for sale at a fixed price. It was for sale at whatever price the collective decisions of every other family bidding on it happened to set that year, and every family that joins the bidding raises the price for the family behind it.

Chapter Six: The Trap That Ate a Way of Life

The largest-scale version Alexander offers goes back roughly ten thousand years, to the invention of agriculture, and it borrows an argument researchers including Jared Diamond have made from skeletal and demographic evidence: hunter-gatherer populations, by several measures anthropologists can actually check in the archaeological record, height, bone density, number of hours worked per week, appear to have been healthier and less overworked than the farming populations that eventually displaced them almost everywhere on earth. Agriculture did not spread because it made the individual farmer's life better than the forager's life it replaced. It spread because a fixed area of farmed land can feed, and eventually arm, many times more people than the same area could support under foraging, and a numerous, settled, organized population beats a smaller, healthier, more mobile one in the one contest that actually determines whose way of life survives to the next century: who has more people and more spears. Two populations in competition for the same territory don't get to vote on which lifestyle wins. The lifestyle that produces more competitors wins, whether or not it produces happier ones, and everyone alive today is descended from the side of that contest that chose the trap.

Run the same shape forward into the twentieth century and it explains an arms race without needing a single villain anywhere in the chain. Two nations that both suspect a nuclear weapon might be useful someday face a mirror version of the dollar auction: build the weapon and you're safer relative to a rival who doesn't, so long as you have it and they don't, but the instant they build one too, both nations are back to the relative safety they started with, now carrying the absolute cost and risk of a weapon that produces no lasting advantage for either side. Rational choices made in sequence by two governments that each want, above all else, not to be the side without the weapon, produce a world stocked with tens of thousands of warheads that nobody involved actually wanted to build for their own sake and that both sides would happily trade away in a verified, simultaneous disarmament neither side can fully trust the other to honor.

Chapter Seven: What Finally Stopped the Farms

Return to the antibiotics with the trap's full architecture in view and the sixty-three-year delay stops looking like negligence and starts looking like exactly what the model predicts. No farmer could unilaterally quit the practice without simply losing market share to farmers who kept using it, so restraint had to arrive from somewhere outside the competition itself, an actor not competing in the same race and therefore not punished by the same logic for imposing a cost that applied evenly to every competitor at once. A regulatory ban is, mechanically, a change to what each competitor stands to gain, made illegal for every player at the same moment, so that nobody loses relative position by giving up the winning move. The rule didn't ask any single rancher to be a better person than his neighbor. It removed the neighbor's advantage by removing everyone's access to it at once, the only kind of intervention a genuine multipolar trap actually responds to.

Be honest about how partial that fix has turned out to be. Growth-promotion sales did fall to nothing, but overall antibiotic sales for livestock in the United States have since drifted back upward in several of the years the Food and Drug Administration has reported since, now justified under the looser label of disease prevention rather than growth promotion, a reclassification that a skeptical reading would call the same trap finding a new door once the old one got locked. The mechanism that produced the original sixty-three-year delay hasn't been defeated. It's been out-maneuvered exactly once, on exactly one specific use of the drug, and the underlying incentive to find some other angle on the same competitive advantage is still sitting there, unregulated, waiting for the next door to be tried.

Chapter Eight: Elua, the God Still Standing

Everything so far describes what Moloch does. Alexander spends a shorter stretch of the essay on the question of what, if anything, stands against him, and reaches for a source considerably stranger than Ginsberg: the novelist Jacqueline Carey's Kushiel series, whose invented god Elua represents flowers, art, philosophy, and what Alexander's essay calls free love and all soft and fragile things. Set beside a god of child sacrifice, a god of flowers sounds close to a joke, and Alexander seems to know it, choosing the frailest possible image on purpose. Elua has no armies and enforces no rule of competition on anyone. He represents, in the essay's account, everything a person or a civilization values that a multipolar trap doesn't reward and will happily discard the instant discarding it improves the score: leisure, beauty, unhurried love, art made for no other reason than that someone wanted to make it. None of that wins a war, a market, or a dollar auction. All of it survives anyway, in the pockets and moments a trap hasn't yet colonized, and that survival is the only kind of victory this particular god has ever claimed.

This lands almost exactly where last episode left off. Max Weber, describing a disenchanted world in which the old religious authority that used to rank human values against each other has vacated the field, said the old gods climb back out of their graves and resume, in his words, their eternal struggle with one another, no longer wearing names or temples but no less real for that. Alexander's Moloch and Elua are two of those gods, stripped of temples exactly as Weber said they would be, fighting for the same unclaimed territory Weber's lecture described a century earlier from an entirely different angle: not disenchantment this time, but competition, and the question of which values survive contact with a system built to punish anyone who keeps them.

Chapter Nine: The Frames This Shelf Already Had

None of this mechanism is actually new to a listener who has stayed with this feed. Robert Jackall's corporate managers, four episodes into this show's whole run, protected themselves from blame by passing it downward and sideways rather than by doing better work, because a manager who accepted blame honestly lost standing relative to managers who didn't, and a manager who unilaterally stopped playing blame games in a building full of managers who hadn't simply got outmaneuvered by the ones who kept playing. That's a multipolar trap with careers as the currency. Charles Perrow's account of tightly coupled, interactively complex systems described a different mechanism entirely, accidents built into the physics and wiring of a plant rather than into anyone's incentives, but the England health service Bevan and Hood studied nine episodes ago ran on a trap of exactly Alexander's shape: a hospital manager who refused to game the eight-minute ambulance target watched his star rating fall behind managers who did game it, and the honest manager had no way to unilaterally opt out of a system that judged every trust by the same published number regardless of how any one trust chose to hit it.

Andy Grove's own career supplies the clearest case of an entire industry accepting Moloch's terms and calling it strategy. Grove ran Intel through the nineteen eighties memory chip business at the exact moment Japanese manufacturers, backed by cheaper capital and government support, started selling memory chips at prices no American firm could match and still turn a profit. Every year Intel stayed in that market to defend its position cost the company money it wasn't making back, and every year a competitor exited the race for good, ceding ground permanently to whoever stayed. Grove and his co-founder Gordon Moore eventually asked each other, in the story Grove tells in his own memoir, what a new management team walking in fresh would do with the company, and answered their own question by doing it themselves: they left the memory business entirely and bet everything on processors instead, the one move available to a company willing to stop competing in a race it recognized as a trap rather than a fight it could win by trying harder. Most competitors in most multipolar traps never get to make that choice, because exit only works if there's somewhere else to go. Intel had one. A fish farmer boxed into a single bay usually doesn't.

Chapter Ten: The God Is Losing Some Ground

The strongest reply to the essay doesn't come from an economist arguing the model is wrong. It comes from a writer well inside Alexander's own intellectual circle, Zvi Mowshowitz, who published a piece years later called "Moloch Hasn't Won" making a simple empirical observation: if multipolar traps really did grind every value that doesn't help you win down to nothing, human civilization would already look a great deal worse than it does. Art still gets made by people who will never recoup the hours they spent on it. Parents still take vacations instead of banking every spare hour toward the college fund arms race. Mowshowitz's explanation is that real-world competition is almost never as perfectly unforgiving as the dollar auction or the fish farm model assumes: most markets carry enough slack, friction, and outright irrationality that an actor can decline to feed Moloch and merely do somewhat worse, rather than get eliminated outright, and a great deal of what looks like a trap only tightens to a lethal degree once some additional social or signaling pressure gets layered on top of the ordinary competitive one.

This show has already met one of the clearest documented cases of a group escaping exactly this kind of trap on its own, without needing a Grove-style exit or an outside regulator. Elinor Ostrom spent decades cataloguing common-pool resources, alpine pastures in Switzerland, irrigation systems in Nepal, that should, by a naive multipolar-trap reading, have been strip-mined by whichever villager grazed the most sheep or drew the most water first. Most of them weren't, for centuries at a stretch, because the communities using them built local monitoring, graduated penalties, and locally accepted rules that made cheating cost something without needing a national regulator anywhere in sight. Ostrom's tribunals and Alexander's fish farm are the same trap described from opposite ends: one shows the mechanism grinding a shared resource down because nobody built an exit, the other shows a group that built one.

Chapter Eleven: The Toolbox

Alexander's own intellectual collaborator Eliezer Yudkowsky picked up the essay's argument three years later and tried to convert it from a diagnosis into something closer to an instruction manual, first in a book called "Inadequate Equilibria" and then in a companion pair of essays under the title "Moloch's Toolbox." Yudkowsky's contribution is a short list of the specific conditions that let a bad equilibrium survive contact with people who can plainly see it's bad: the people making a given decision are often not the people who bear its costs, so an executive who benefits personally from a corner-cutting choice has no built-in reason to weigh the cost to a customer who will never trace the harm back to him; the information needed to catch the corner-cutting is often available to nobody positioned to act on it, split between parties who each hold only half of what would be needed to prove the problem; and the whole arrangement is frequently propped up by the same coordination failure Shubik's auction demonstrates, where any single actor who tried to fix it alone would simply be replaced by an actor willing to keep exploiting it.

What naming these three conditions buys you is a way to sort what kind of fix might actually work. Misaligned incentives need a change to who pays for what. Scattered information needs a mechanism that lets the scattered pieces meet, an audit, a whistleblower channel, a second, independent measurement of the same thing the primary system reports. Pure coordination failure, where everyone privately agrees the current arrangement is bad and would happily switch to a better one if everyone else switched at the same moment, is the one kind a regulator, a treaty, or a widely trusted convener can sometimes dissolve overnight simply by giving every actor simultaneous permission to stop, the same mechanism behind the growth-promotion ban that finally got antibiotics off the farms that couldn't stop themselves.

Chapter Twelve: The Charge That the Villain Went Missing on Purpose

The hardest and most specific objection to the whole essay comes from a writer named Mike Travers, responding under a piece he called "Meditations on Meditations on Moloch," and his complaint isn't that the mechanism is wrong. It's that the mechanism, described the way Alexander describes it, conveniently erases every actor who benefits from a bad arrangement and has every reason to keep it running. Travers points out that treating a race to the bottom as a leaderless, agentless force of mathematics lets a factory owner who lobbies against a safety regulation, or a landlord who profits from an artificially tight housing market, disappear into the abstraction along with everyone else, when in fact some of the people inside these systems are not hapless competitors trapped by a structure beyond their control. They are the structure's active beneficiaries, spending real money and real political effort to keep the trap exactly as it is, because the trap that costs a fish farmer's downstream neighbors a clean river is also the trap that makes the fish farmer richer than his more careful neighbor, every single season the trap keeps running.

Travers's second charge goes after the essay's own literary borrowing. Ginsberg's Moloch section names "Moloch whose name is the Mind" as one of the demon's own faces, indicting rationality and calculation themselves as part of the sickness. Alexander inverts that specific line without acknowledging the inversion: his essay's whole proposed remedy is more and better rational calculation, a smarter, more coordinated application of the very faculty Ginsberg's poem blamed for the disaster in the first place. Whether that inversion is a flaw or simply an update depends on which decade's evidence you trust more, nineteen fifty-four's fear of the bomb the rational planners built, or twenty fourteen's fear of a coordination problem that better information and better institutions might actually be able to solve. Both writers were responding to the tools available to civilization in their own moment, and neither had the other's decades of additional data to work with.

Chapter Thirteen: The Gardener

Alexander's own proposed exit is the essay's most ambitious claim, and it should be stated plainly rather than softened. Every escape catalogued so far, Grove's exit into a new market, Ostrom's local monitoring, the federal ban on growth-promotion antibiotics, works only within a bounded competition that some larger actor can still stand outside of and regulate. Alexander's own worry is what happens once a trap operates at the scale of the entire global economy or the entire species, where no larger actor exists to stand outside it the way a national government stands outside a single industry. His answer, offered with more confidence than the argument up to that point has actually earned, is that a sufficiently capable and well-aligned artificial intelligence, acting as what he calls a gardener rather than a ruler, could occupy exactly that outside position for the whole of human civilization at once, coordinating away the traps that no merely human institution has the reach to dissolve.

That step is what it is: a speculative, unproven leap from a documented historical mechanism, real fish farms, real dollar auctions, real antibiotic sales figures, to a technology that in twenty fourteen existed nowhere outside a research paper and still, more than a decade later, has never been demonstrated to do anything resembling what the essay asks of it. The diagnosis rests on evidence a skeptic can check against actual farm data and actual auction results. The proposed cure rests on a hope about a technology whose safety and capability nobody, including its own most serious builders, currently knows how to guarantee. An essay this careful about grounding every other claim in a checkable case earns the benefit of the doubt on its diagnosis. Its prescription is a different kind of writing entirely, closer to a wager than a finding, and the two halves of the essay read as two different genres rather than one continuous argument.

Chapter Fourteen: The Ratchet Inside the Counselling Programme

A small, human-scale version of this same trap runs inside the maternal mental health counselling programme this show's listener runs at a distance, across several districts, staffed by lay counsellors supervised by supervisors who are in turn supervised by a small central team. The programme measures itself partly on enrollment, the count of women who start counselling in a given month, because enrollment is visible, comparable across districts, and easy to put on a dashboard. A supervisor whose district's enrollment count looks weak against every other district's, for reasons that might have nothing to do with the quality of care her counsellors are actually providing, feels the same pressure Alexander's fish farmer feels: push counsellors toward faster intake and lighter early sessions to keep the number competitive with the districts she's compared against, even where a slower, more careful intake would serve the individual woman better. She didn't invent that pressure, and she can't unilaterally opt out of it without her own numbers falling behind everyone else's, a multipolar trap running at the scale of one organization's internal dashboard rather than a market or a species. The exit, when it works at all, looks like Grove's paired indicators from three episodes back: measuring something enrollment can't fake, like counsellor-reported session quality or a woman's own follow-up report weeks later, so that gaming one number stops being free.

Chapter Fifteen: The Dose

Put Alexander's essay through the same dosage test this show applies to every cynical framework it takes seriously, and the results split cleanly along the same line that separated the essay's diagnosis from its prescription. Used diagnostically, on the antibiotics, the dollar auctions, the ambulance targets, the multipolar trap is one of the most useful single ideas this feed has covered, because it answers a question that blame alone never resolves: not who behaved badly, but what would have to change about the structure itself before behaving well stopped being a losing move. Used as an identity, treating every unfair outcome in your own life as Moloch's work and therefore nobody's fault and nothing anyone chose, the same framework becomes exactly what Travers warned against: a way of describing exploitation that happens to let the people extracting real, chosen advantage from it off the hook, because a trap with no operator is a trap nobody ever has to answer for. The honest reading holds both things at once. Some bad arrangements really do have no villain, only a structure that needs changing from outside. Others have a villain standing inside the structure, benefiting from it, and calling it Moloch instead of calling it by his name is its own kind of shortcut.

Chapter Sixteen: What You're Feeding

The question to hold onto is a specific one: name a competition you're currently inside, at work, in your field, in your household's own choices about money or schooling, where you already suspect the winning move costs something you'd rather keep, and ask honestly whether you could unilaterally stop making that move without simply losing ground to everyone still making it. If the answer is no, you've found a trap, not a personal failing, and the next question is who or what stands outside that competition with enough reach to change what it rewards, since that's the only kind of actor a multipolar trap has ever actually answered to.

Next time, this shelf stays with the same writer for a very different kind of essay: Scott Alexander's "Considerations on Cost Disease," an honest accounting of why healthcare, education, and construction now cost several times what they did in nineteen seventy for no improvement anyone can clearly point to, and a rare case of a writer this confident admitting, in print, that he doesn't actually know the answer.
