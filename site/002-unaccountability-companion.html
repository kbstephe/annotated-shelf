Dan Davies primer: Lying for Money, accountability sinks, and the Gervais Principle

An analysis of Dan Davies's body of work — Lying for Money, The Unaccountability Machine, and the Back of Mind newsletter — synthesized with Rao's Gervais Principle: sinks as industrialized HIWTYL, the clueless middle as the sink's human interface, and Powertalk as the unattenuated channel.

24 August 2026

Chapter 1: Who Dan Davies is

Dan Davies's The Unaccountability Machine is the natural third leg of the stool this series has been building, alongside Rao and Jackall. So this episode is a tour of Davies's whole body of work, ending in a synthesis with the Gervais Principle. First, who is he — and a disambiguation, since there are two British writers named Dan Davies, and the other one wrote a biography of Jimmy Savile. Our Davies is a Welsh former Bank of England regulatory economist turned investment-bank analyst, and before he was an author he was one of the great bloggers of the early internet, writing as D-squared Digest through the two thousands. That blog matters for understanding everything after, because it established his method and his most quoted idea: what he called the one-minute MBA, whose first and most famous lesson was that good ideas do not need lots of lies told about them in order to gain public acceptance — written about the case for the Iraq war, and holding up remarkably well since. His signature register was set there too: whimsical, contrarian, allergic to moralizing, and convinced that the most interesting truths about institutions live in their plumbing rather than their pronouncements. He now writes a Substack called Back of Mind, subtitled a newsletter of quiet contrarianism, slow analysis and ambient ideas, which is the living laboratory where his books get thought out in public. The corpus, then: Lying for Money in twenty eighteen, a theory of fraud. The Unaccountability Machine in twenty twenty-four, a theory of organizational decision-making built on the cybernetics of Stafford Beer. A book on the Brompton folding bicycle company, about manufacturing and process knowledge. And the newsletter stitching it together. The through-line, which I'll try to convince you of by the end: Davies has spent twenty-five years writing one long book about the same subject — how trust, information, and blame move through systems that have grown too big for anyone to hold in their head.

Chapter 2: Lying for Money — fraud as an information problem

Start with the fraud book, because it contains the seed of everything. Lying for Money is nominally a tour of great financial frauds — pyramid schemes, counterfeits, cartels, control frauds — organized into a working taxonomy. But its real subject is trust, treated as an economic quantity. Davies's central move is to point out that checking things is expensive. A modern economy works because we mostly don't verify — we trust certificates, audits, invoices, titles, and each other — and this non-checking is not laziness but the whole source of the productivity. Fraud, in his definition, is an attack on that trust infrastructure: the fraudster finds a place where society has decided checking isn't worth the cost, and moves in. Which yields the book's most famous and most clinical conclusion: the optimal level of fraud is not zero. A society with zero fraud would be a society that verified everything, and it would be poor, slow, and miserable — a low-trust world. Fraud is, quite literally, the tax we pay on the efficiency of trusting. He illustrates with what he calls the Canadian paradox: why does laid-back, high-trust Canada have such a lively history of securities fraud, while low-trust societies have less? Because you can only be defrauded where trust exists. Fraud is a disease of advanced trust economies the way certain infections are diseases of hospitals. Notice the shape of this argument, because it recurs: take a phenomenon everyone treats as a moral failing of individuals, and redescribe it as a structural property of information flows — with an equilibrium level, trade-offs, and no satisfying villain. And notice something else, relevant to our synthesis: the fraudster in Lying for Money is recognizably Rao's sociopath in the wild. He is precisely the person who has noticed which parts of the collective performance are load-bearing and unguarded. Davies just declines to be scandalized about it.

Chapter 3: The Unaccountability Machine — the sink

Now the main event. The Unaccountability Machine, from twenty twenty-four, opens with an image that has already entered the language: the accountability sink. Davies's canonical illustration is an airline story from the nineteen nineties, in which hundreds of squirrels arriving as cargo without proper paperwork were destroyed — fed into a shredder — by ground staff following the applicable procedure, to public horror. The question the book hangs on that horror: who, exactly, decided to do that? The staff followed policy. The policy authors never met a squirrel. The managers enforced compliance with a rulebook written for good reasons. Everyone did their job, the outcome was monstrous, and there is genuinely no one to blame. That is an accountability sink: a structure in which decision-making has been delegated to a process, and the crucial link — the feedback link between the person affected by a decision and anyone capable of changing it — has been severed. You have met the sink. It is the phone tree that disconnects you, the insurance denial that no human at the company can override, the hospital discharge decided by an algorithm no one in the building can explain. Davies's sharpest observation is that sinks are not accidents and not exactly evil: they are how large organizations metabolize more complexity than any individual can process. Delegating decisions to a rulebook is what lets a system scale. But he also states what he calls the principle of diminishing accountability: unless conscious steps are taken to prevent it, any organization in a modern society will restructure itself to reduce the personal responsibility attributable to its actions — and this tendency continues until crisis. Sinks accumulate, in other words, because they are individually rational and collectively corrosive. The diagnostic test he offers is beautifully simple. Ask of any bad outcome: was there anyone with the authority to override the policy? If yes, you have accountability. If the answer is the policy is the policy, you are standing in the sink.

Chapter 4: Stafford Beer and the cybernetic spine

What elevates the book beyond a catalogue of bureaucratic horror is its second half, which is a rescue mission for a nearly forgotten intellectual tradition: the management cybernetics of Stafford Beer, the flamboyant British consultant best known for building Project Cybersyn, a real-time economic control room for Allende's Chile that died with the nineteen seventy-three coup. Davies extracts three ideas from Beer that you should file permanently. First, POSIWID: the purpose of a system is what it does. Not what its mission statement says, not what its leaders intend — if a system reliably produces an outcome, treat that outcome as its purpose and analyze accordingly. A hospital billing department that reliably generates surprise bills is, functionally, a surprise-bill-generating system, whatever its values slide says. It's the most clarifying four-letter acronym in organizational thought. Second, Ashby's law of requisite variety: a controller must have at least as much variety — as many possible states — as the thing it's controlling. When it doesn't, it must attenuate: compress reality into categories, dashboards, and forms. Every organization is therefore an engine for throwing away information, and the design question is only ever which information gets discarded. The squirrels died in an attenuation: whatever was individual about that situation had been compressed to a paperwork category with one procedure attached. Third, and most striking, Beer's claim — which Davies updates for the AI age — that organizations should be understood as artificial intelligences: genuinely decision-making entities whose goals are distinct from the intentions of any member, running on people the way software runs on chips. We spent the twenty-tens fretting about misaligned AI, Davies observes, while already living inside misaligned AIs called corporations and ministries. The book's villain, incidentally, is the economics that blessed all this — particularly the Friedman-era doctrine that a firm's only responsibility is shareholder value, which Davies reads as the ideological license to install one master sink at the top of every corporation.

Chapter 5: The blog and the Brompton — process knowledge

Before synthesizing, two quicker stops that round out the picture. The Brompton book — about the London folding-bicycle manufacturer — sounds like a departure and isn't. It's a study in process knowledge: the tacit, accumulated, embodied know-how that lives in a workshop's hands and habits and can't be written down, offshored, or acquired in a merger. It is, in cybernetic terms, a book about the information that must not be attenuated — the variety a viable system has to keep in-house. If The Unaccountability Machine is about what organizations destroy by compressing information, The Brompton is the affirmative case: what it looks like when an organization protects its unattenuated knowledge, even at the cost of staying small and slightly eccentric. James C. Scott's metis — the local knowledge that legibility schemes destroy — is the same idea wearing different clothes, which tells you Davies and Rao are, in places, mining the same vein. Then there's Back of Mind, the Substack, which I'd genuinely recommend as one of the best ongoing feeds of institutional thinking anywhere. It's where the books breathe: essays applying the toolkit to whatever's in the news — regulatory disasters, opinion polling, judicial review, why certification regimes fail when everyone obeys rules to the letter with no margin, an idea he calls stacked tolerances that deserves wider currency. Recent posts have even wandered directly onto Rao's turf, including one titled middle management is what the other guy does. The register throughout is the opposite of the systems-collapse doomer genre: cheerful, unscandalized, fixated on mechanism. Davies writes about institutional dysfunction the way a good clinician writes about disease — as a professional who finds pathology interesting and believes in treatment.

Chapter 6: The synthesis — Rao's people, Davies's plumbing

Now the payoff — laying Davies over the Gervais Principle, because the overlap you sensed is real, and the disagreement is even more interesting. Both writers are staring at the same phenomenon: organizations systematically route blame away from power. But they tell opposite stories about the mechanism. Rao's account is agent-centric: sociopaths deliberately construct heads-I-win-tails-you-lose structures, and the clueless middle exists to absorb liability. Blame, for Rao, flows downhill into a person. Davies's account is structure-centric: blame flows into a process, and vanishes. Nobody absorbs it, because the sink's whole function is that there is no body. Jackall's managers said blame flows down; Davies says the mature machine improves on that — blame flows nowhere. Here's my proposed synthesis, and I think it's stronger than either book alone. The accountability sink is the industrialized, scaled-up successor of Rao's HIWTYL scheme. A heads-I-win-tails-you-lose arrangement is artisanal — it must be hand-built for each situation, and it still needs a designated loser, which is inefficient and leaves evidence. A sink automates it: build the deniability into procedure once, and it runs forever, protecting everyone above it, with no individual scapegoat required and therefore no one with a grievance. And this suggests where the layers of Rao's pyramid actually stand relative to the sink. The sociopath layer are the sink's architects — the people who commission rulebooks, choose the metrics, decide which variety gets attenuated, and, crucially, retain for themselves the override authority that Davies says defines real accountability. The clueless middle is the sink's human interface — the sincere enforcers who say the policy is the policy and mean it, converting reality into the categories the system can digest. Manufacturing legibility, as we said last time; operating the attenuator, as Beer would say. And Powertalk? Powertalk is the unattenuated channel. It's how the architects exchange the full-variety information — who's really exposed, what the numbers really mean — that the official channels were designed to compress away. The formal org chart runs on attenuated information; the sociopath network runs on the raw feed. That, I'd argue, is the deepest single insight the two frameworks produce together: power in an organization is exactly the question of who gets the unattenuated information, and who possesses override authority over the sinks. Find those two things and you've found the real org chart.

Chapter 7: Verdict — which theory to reach for when

So how should you hold these two bodies of work together? They correct each other's characteristic error, almost perfectly. Rao's failure mode is paranoia: everything is somebody's scheme, every structure hides an author, and the reader is trained to hunt sociopaths — which over-attributes agency, and in real institutions leads you to accuse people of designing dysfunctions that nobody designed. Davies's failure mode is the opposite — call it exoneration: the system did it, everyone followed procedure, responsibility dissolves into cybernetics, and the reader half-forgets that sinks have beneficiaries, that somewhere there were architects who chose this attenuation over that one, and that the principle of diminishing accountability advances because specific people, with names, profit from each increment. Even one of the strongest critiques of Davies's book lands here: it's diagnostically brilliant and thin on remedy. The combined lens avoids both errors. When something goes wrong in your institution, run Davies first: map the sink, find where the feedback link was cut, ask who holds override authority — because most dysfunction really is structural, and hunting for villains in a structural failure wastes your outrage and your credibility. But then run Rao second: ask who benefits from this sink's existence, who built it, who is exempt from it — because sinks are not weather; they are architecture, and architecture has clients. In Beer's terms: the purpose of a system is what it does, and what it reliably does for the people at the top is worth particular attention. As for the reading order, if this episode sends you to the shelf: The Unaccountability Machine first — it's the most complete and the most relevant to this decade, AI included. Lying for Money second, because it's the most purely enjoyable and the trust framework generalizes everywhere, from finance to peer review. Then subscribe to Back of Mind and let the toolkit compound weekly. Davies is, I think, the rare writer who took everything true in the cynical tradition we've been touring — Rao's sharpness, Jackall's blame hydraulics — and rebuilt it as engineering rather than demonology. Which makes him not just an overlap with the Gervais Principle, but arguably its adult supervision. Thanks for listening.
