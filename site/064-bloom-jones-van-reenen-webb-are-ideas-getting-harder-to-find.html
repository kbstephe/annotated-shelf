Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find? (Nicholas Bloom, Charles Jones, John Van Reenen and Michael Webb): the 2020 accounting of what a research dollar buys, set against Collison and Nielsen's survey of Nobel prizes and the disruption index of Park, Leahey and Funk, with the two published corrections to that index

The American Economic Review paper that found research productivity falling in chips, crops, cancer and firms, tested against the Atlantic essay that asked scientists to rank discoveries and the Nature study of declining disruptiveness, and what the replies to that study leave standing.

14 October 2026

Chapter One: The Treadmill

In 1971 an Intel chip carried about two thousand three hundred transistors. The most recent chip on the chart in the paper this episode is about carries two billion six hundred million. The chart is drawn on a logarithmic scale, so the history of the intervening forty years comes out as one straight line, and the line says that the count on a chip doubled about every second year, for as long as anyone was counting.

That doubling time works out to growth of thirty-five percent annually, and the paper treats it that way. Sustained for four decades, the rate is the output. The authors, Nicholas Bloom, Charles Jones, John Van Reenen and Michael Webb, then asked the question an economist asks of any output, which is what went in. They assembled the research spending of Intel, Fairchild, National Semiconductor, Motorola, Texas Instruments, Samsung and more than two dozen other chip and equipment makers, and they converted the dollars into people by dividing by the wage of a highly skilled worker. The figure that results is an effective number of researchers, a headcount that no firm ever literally employed, and it is the unit the whole paper runs on.

That headcount rose by a factor of eighteen between 1971 and 2014. The growth rate of chip density did not rise at all. So the same thirty-five percent a year took eighteen times as many researchers to produce at the end as at the start. Divide the output by the input and you get what the authors call research productivity, which fell by the same factor of eighteen, an average of six point eight percent a year. They tried the narrower definitions of which spending counts, and the most conservative one still gave a fall by a factor of eight.

The sentence the authors reach for is from Lewis Carroll. In the Red Queen's country, it takes all the running you can do to stay in the same place. A constant exponential, on this account, is a treadmill whose belt speeds up, and the thing that keeps the chart a straight line is a research workforce that keeps growing. If the number of researchers had stayed at the 1971 level, the line would have bent.

That is the claim at full strength, and the question this episode follows is older than the paper. When something that used to be cheap to produce gets expensive, is the cause in the thing, in us, or in the ruler? Three famous studies say that ideas are getting harder to find, and they use three different rulers. The first sets dollars against growth, the second sets them against the opinion of working scientists, and the third ignores money and looks at what later papers do with their citations. Two of those three have since been told, by named critics, that their ruler was bent, and the answer the series cares about, whether to believe the headline, depends on which.

Start with the best-built one, because it is also the one that has been attacked least on its own ground, an economist's habit of defining the terms before running anything.

Chapter Two: The Puzzle in the Fastest Sector

Economists since the 1990s have used a simple equation for growth from ideas. Growth in the stock of ideas equals the number of researchers multiplied by how productive each researcher is. Everyone agreed on the first half. The argument was about whether the second half, productivity, stays constant, and a whole generation of growth models assumed that it does, partly because it makes the mathematics tractable. If it does, a fixed number of researchers produces a fixed percentage of improvement every year, forever.

The paper's contribution is to go and look. Chips are the showpiece, but the authors deliberately spread across scales, because there is an obvious escape route for the constant-productivity view. Perhaps the productivity of research on any one product line declines, as the easy improvements to corn or to transistor density get used up, while growth continues because researchers keep inventing new product lines. Steam power, then electricity, then the engine, then semiconductors. A paper that only looked at chips would never see that.

So the paper tests the escape route from both ends. At the top, it takes the whole American economy. Output per person has grown near two percent annually, the number of researchers has risen enormously, and the division gives a fall in research productivity by a factor of forty-one since the 1930s, a little over five percent a year. The half-life comes out near thirteen years, which becomes a sentence in the conclusion: to keep the growth rate of the economy constant, the country has to double its research effort every thirteen years.

At the bottom, it takes narrow cases where both sides of the ledger could be measured, and the authors say plainly that this requirement chose the cases. Crop yields come first. Yield per acre for corn, soybeans, cotton and wheat has grown at about one and a half percent a year since 1960, while the effective number of researchers aimed at each crop, public and private spending deflated by the skilled wage, climbed by large multiples. For agriculture as a whole, effective research roughly doubled between 1970 and 2007 and productivity fell by a factor of nearly four. A farmer's field is a fair test of the idea, because nobody can call a bushel an accounting artefact.

Medicine is the third set, and it is the one the listener of this series is likely to read most closely. The first test counts new molecular entities, the novel compounds that new drugs are built on. The yearly count approved by the Food and Drug Administration has shown little trend, while pharmaceutical research spending grew at six percent a year in real terms. The paper's calculation has research effort up by a factor of nine and research productivity down by a factor of five by 2014, having fallen by eleven at the low point in 2007 before recovering somewhat. The authors flag at once that counting molecules treats a statin and a me-too antacid as the same unit.

So they turn to mortality, and here the construction runs differently, because the same machinery that gave a clean answer for chips has to be rebuilt for diseases. Life expectancy does not grow exponentially. In the United States it has risen at about one point eight years per decade since 1950, a straight line, so the output they use is years of life saved by the fall in a disease's death rate, which they derive with a model of competing risks. The input is a count of publications in PubMed carrying the disease's subject heading, or the subset flagged as clinical trials. For all cancers, years of life saved per thousand people peaks near 1990 at more than a hundred and then falls toward sixty. Over the same years publications on cancer rose by a factor of three and a half. Research productivity for breast cancer trials declines at about ten percent a year, and for heart disease trials at seven.

Two caveats belong here, and the authors make both. Publications are a very crude proxy for effort: a count does not know whether a paper reported a trial of ten thousand patients or a letter. And the years of life saved by a given cancer drug accrue long after the trial, so any attempt to align a year of input with a year of output smears the result. They also report that the heart disease death rate has fallen partly because people stopped getting the disease, which the research counts do not distinguish.

The pattern runs on. In the Compustat database of public firms, research productivity falls at roughly ten percent a year by the measure that uses sales, and in the Census of Manufacturing at about eight. The summary table of the paper has a column for the half-life, and the numbers run from five years at the fast end to over a hundred for the one broad cancer measure based on publications. The authors summarise it as a substantial decline in virtually every place they looked.

The paper then raises a puzzle. By their measure, chips show a steep decline in research productivity, six point eight percent a year. Chips are also the sector with the fastest idea growth anywhere, thirty-five percent annually. A reader who believes research productivity falls because the easy ideas are used up would expect the sector with the fastest decline to be the sector that has hit the hardest wall. The paper says the opposite. They fit a parameter, written as the Greek letter beta, that measures how fast the difficulty of new ideas rises as the stock of ideas grows, and it comes out at about two tenths for semiconductors, the mildest diminishing returns of any case, against more than three for the economy as a whole. Seven divided by thirty-five is a fifth.

The explanation they offer is demand. Computer chips are a general-purpose input to everything, so the world is willing to pay for a fast-growing research workforce there, and productivity per researcher falls fastest where the workforce grows fastest, not because chips are uniquely hard. The sector with the most research per idea is the sector where research pays best.

That reading changes what a falling research productivity means. It is not necessarily a sign of exhaustion. A sector can be paying more per idea because the ideas are worth more.

Chapter Three: What an Idea Weighs

The steelman of the paper is that the authors have chosen one accounting identity and applied it everywhere with the same discipline. Their robustness argument is a good one. The research input in their cases grows at five to seven percent a year or more, doubling every ten to fifteen years, while the output growth rates are flat or falling. To overturn the result, a different output measure would have to show a rate that itself doubles every decade or so. A constant mismeasurement of growth rates, say that seed yields are really a point higher than the data say, leaves the finding unchanged, because the finding comes from the difference in trends. Only measurement error that worsens systematically, by a lot, would rescue it, and the authors know of no evidence for that.

That argument is persuasive about measurement error and silent about something else. The identity says that growth equals researchers times productivity, and it labels the remainder productivity. A remainder is whatever the other terms leave. In the paper's own words, the exercise measures a Solow residual of the idea production function, and anything omitted from the input side falls into it. They list some of the omitted things: improvements by workers on a factory floor, the efforts of failed start-ups, research in other industries that spills over. Their conclusion is that unmeasured inputs which grow more slowly than measured effort would overstate the fall, and ones that grow faster would understate it.

The most developed objection I found sits on the other side of the identity, and it comes from an economist, Karthik Tadepalli, writing in Asterisk in October 2025. His point concerns what an idea is. In the paper, an idea is whatever raises productivity growth, and the research production function turns the output into ideas by assumption. But a better seed leaves yields where they were until farmers buy it, and a better process lifts an economy's productivity only once the firms that have it take market share from those that do not. Tadepalli's argument is that economy-wide productivity is a market-share weighted average across firms, so it can stall while innovation proceeds, if the best firms are not gaining share. If that is what has happened, the research productivity in the paper falls partly because the ledger counts allocation as a research failure. He cites work showing that less productive firms gained share against more productive ones and that breakthrough patents per research dollar have risen since the late 1970s. He is also careful to say that patents are a proxy and that the causes of poorer allocation are unresolved.

That objection does not fit every case in the paper equally. It has force for the aggregate and the Compustat numbers, where output is a firm's measured productivity. It has less force for the chips, where the thing measured is a physical count of transistors per chip, and it is mostly irrelevant for years of life saved, where the output is a death rate. I take that to mean the paper's cleanest cases, chips and cancer, are the ones least exposed to Tadepalli, and they are also the ones where the authors' choice of units is most visible. Moore's law counts doublings, and a doubling of a density that is now close to atomic limits may be a bigger thing than a doubling in 1971. If so, a constant growth rate does not mean a constant idea, and a fall in research per doubling may only report that doublings got bigger. The paper chooses growth rates as the unit of an idea deliberately, and the choice is the argument. An economist who prefers another unit gets another answer.

I also came across, through summaries only, a critique from the agricultural economists Alston and Pardey of the conceptual model and the measure of research productivity, and a longer treatment by Guzey and Rischel, objecting to the use of total factor productivity as a measure of innovation. I did not read either, and I name them as the places to look for the opposing case rather than as findings of this episode.

What survives all this is narrower than the headline and more interesting. Spending a fixed share of a growing economy on research does not produce a fixed rate of improvement in chips, crops or cancer survival. That is a statement about dollars per unit of a chosen measure of progress. Whether the world is out of easy ideas or has stopped converting good ideas into goods is a separate question, and the accounting by itself cannot say.

Chapter Four: A Round Robin of Nobel Prizes

In November 2018 Patrick Collison and Michael Nielsen published an essay in The Atlantic with a title that states its conclusion: science is getting less bang for its buck. Collison is a founder of the payments company Stripe and the reader whose shelf this series spent eight episodes on, and Nielsen is a physicist turned writer on how science works. I could not open the Atlantic page from where this script was prepared, so what follows comes from the summaries of it and the passages quoted in them, and the essay should be read directly.

Their method answers the problem the economists' paper had, which is that productivity needs an output, and the output of science is hard to count. Collison and Nielsen asked working scientists. They took the Nobel Prize winning discoveries in physics, chemistry and physiology or medicine, grouped them by decade, and had scientists in each field compare discoveries from different decades in a round-robin of pairwise judgments, which of these two mattered more. For physics, one summary of the survey says they asked ninety-three physicists from leading departments. They left out the 1990s onward because too few prizes had been awarded to rank them reliably.

The result in physics is a hump. Discoveries from the 1910s to the 1930s, the decades of quantum mechanics, rank highest, and rankings fall afterward with a small rise in the 1960s. Chemistry and medicine are closer to flat. Together, the authors say, scientists judge that the biggest breakthroughs are arriving at a near-constant rate, while the time and money invested has vastly increased. On a per-dollar or per-person basis, they conclude, science is becoming far less efficient.

They offer three explanations, again as summarized by those who read the essay. The average age at which Nobel winners made their prize-winning discovery rose from thirty-seven to forty-seven, according to the work of Benjamin Jones and Bruce Weinberg that the essay cites, which suggests longer training before a person can contribute at the frontier. Teams have grown, from Rutherford publishing alone in 1911 to the thousand authors on each of the two 2012 Higgs papers. And science may have remained too focused on established fields. The authors also complain that there has been little institutional response, and suggest that scientists see acknowledging diminishing returns as against their collective interest.

I will give the essay credit for a thing the economists' paper cannot do. It asks the people best placed to judge what a discovery is worth, which sidesteps the unit problem of Chapter Three. A physicist deciding between the neutron and the cosmic microwave background is deciding which of two ideas is bigger, and there is no growth rate in the question.

The objections arrive quickly, though, and they are structural. First, a Nobel Prize is awarded decades after the work, so the sample of prizes from recent decades is thin and different in kind. Park and colleagues, in the paper discussed below, observe that the gap between a discovery and its prize has itself grown, which means the recent decades are under-sampled for the best work of the period. Second, the people ranking are living scientists, and what they have had time to see matter is older work. A discovery taught for eighty years looks bigger than one applied for twenty, whether or not it is. Third, the 1920s and 1930s were the decades of quantum mechanics, an unusual period for physics, and at least one commentator in the coverage I saw made the same objection to the baseline, though I could not trace it to a name. If the peak is a one-off, the downward slope behind it is partly a return to normal. Fourth, the Nobel committee's three fields and its habit of honoring discoveries rather than methods leave out most of what Bloom and colleagues count: chips are not in it, nor crop genetics, nor the sequencing machines.

The last objection cuts the other way for the listener who wants the evidence to be wrong. A flat output line at a near-constant rate is exactly what you would see if the ruler were saturated. Experts comparing prizes across eras are using their own sense of importance, which was formed in a world where the biggest discoveries are the ones that built the field they were trained in. That would produce a flat or gently falling profile even if real output were rising. The survey does not show that this is happening, and the authors do not claim to have excluded it.

Set the two side by side and something odd appears. The economists find that research effort per unit of progress has fallen enormously, and they leave open whether the output side is shrinking. The physicists' survey asks that other half and finds the output roughly flat, using the weakest ruler in the set. The two together read like a pincer: more effort, no more output. Each, though, depends on its own idea of what is being produced, and neither ruler touches the other's.

Chapter Five: A Citation Network with a Single Number

The third study is the one that tried to produce a ruler for the whole of science with no economist's assumptions and no panel of experts. Michael Park, Erin Leahey and Russell Funk published it in Nature in January 2023, and its title is as direct as the others: papers and patents are becoming less disruptive over time.

The measure it uses, the CD index, comes from earlier work by Funk and Owen-Smith, and its logic is a clean piece of reasoning about what a citation network can say. Pick one paper and collect the later papers that cite it. Some of them also cite the works it built on. Those authors still needed the predecessors, so the paper looks like a refinement that consolidated what was there. Others cite the paper and skip its predecessors, as though it had replaced them, and those suggest disruption. The index is the difference between the two counts divided by everything, running from minus one, a consolidating paper, up to plus one, a disruptive one. A third group, later papers that cite the predecessors and ignore the paper itself, counts in the denominator only.

A constructed example, for the ear. A paper cites three earlier papers. Twelve later papers cite it or its predecessors. Seven cite the paper alone, three cite it together with its predecessors, and two cite just the predecessors. Seven minus three is four, divided by twelve, a third. The numbers are invented to show the arithmetic, and the real calculation runs over millions of papers.

The authors give two real anchors. The 1953 paper of Watson and Crick on the structure of DNA scores sixty-two hundredths five years out, a disruptive paper, because later work cited it without reaching back for Pauling's triple helix. The 1965 paper of Kohn and Sham on electron structure, which also earned a Nobel Prize, scores minus twenty-two hundredths, a consolidating paper that cemented the theorems it used. The two papers were both important, as the authors say, and different in what they did to the literature around them.

The finding is a collapse. Across forty-five million papers and, in the published version, three point nine million patents, the average index falls toward zero in every field. In the Web of Science papers, the social sciences fall from fifty-two hundredths in 1945 to four hundredths in 2010, a drop of nearly ninety-two percent, and the physical sciences from thirty-six hundredths to zero. For patents, between 1980 and 2010, the fall runs from about seventy-nine percent in computers and communications to about ninety-one percent in drugs and medicine. The authors stress that the decline is similar across fields, which they take as evidence against the low-hanging fruit story, since fields would be unlikely to use up their fruit on the same schedule. They also report that the decline shows up in the language of titles, with a falling ratio of distinct words to all words and a shift from verbs of creation and discovery toward verbs of improvement and application.

Two features of the result make it unusual. One is that the absolute number of highly disruptive papers and patents stays nearly constant while the volume of publication grows. In their reading, breakthroughs of the old kind keep arriving at the old rate, and the average falls because the volume of other work is growing around them. The other is a mechanism. Papers are citing a narrower slice of the literature: less variety in what gets cited, a rising share of citations going to the one percent of papers cited most, more self-citation, and an older average age of the references. When the range of work cited narrows, they find, disruptiveness falls.

Readers of this series will recognise the last of those as an old friend. A rising share of citations to the already most cited is the Matthew effect of episode sixty-two, measured at the scale of a literature. A reader who has to choose what to read from an expanding pile may rationally choose what everyone else is reading. The consequence is a literature in which newcomers cite the stars and not each other's predecessors, and, in the paper's own phrasing, scientists may be putting too much effort into writing and not enough into reading and thinking. Their closing line is a reply to Vannevar Bush, the engineer whose Endless Frontier report opened this part of the series in episode fifty-seven. Science, they write, does not necessarily seem to have reached the end of the endless frontier.

I should say that I read the preprint of Park, Leahey and Funk, in its fifth version, and not the Nature text, which sits behind a subscription. The abstract and the figure list match, and I have used the preprint only for numbers that the Nature abstract also supports, and the two differ on the patent count, three point five million in the preprint against three point nine million in Nature.

Chapter Six: Two Replies

A paper this widely cited gets audited, and this one was audited twice, by teams who found different bends in the ruler. I read the versions of both that were available.

The first is by the economists Jeffrey Macher, Christian Rutzer and Rolf Weder, and it concerns the patent half of the result. The patent database Park and colleagues used covers patents granted from 1976. The reply says that their analysis dropped all backward citations to patents granted before 1976. A patent from 1980 that cites thirteen patents from 1958 to 1974 therefore looks, in the analysis, as if it cited nothing. A patent with no references scores as disruptive by construction, because no later patent can cite its predecessors.

That error has a direction. The older the sample, the more of a patent's references fall before 1976, so the early patents in the series look the most disruptive, and the later ones, whose references are mostly in the window, look less so. Macher and his colleagues found that thirty-eight percent of patents in 1980 had no backward citations under the truncated method, against under two percent when all citations are counted, and that the average index in 1980 was thirty-nine hundredths in the truncated version and nine hundredths with all citations. They say that the truncation accounts for eighty-eight percent of the decline in average disruptiveness that the Nature paper reports for patents. When they extended the series to 2016 and corrected a second problem, the treatment of citations to applications, the preferred series dips slightly and is roughly stable since about 2005, and highly disruptive patents have more than doubled since 1980, with a near twentyfold rise in information technology. Their paper appeared, as I understand from library listings, in Research Policy in 2024, and their claim is explicitly about patents. It says nothing on the paper half.

The second reply, from Vincent Holst, Andres Algaba, Floriano Tori, Sylvia Wenmackers and Vincent Ginis of Brussels, goes after the papers, and its story is stranger. A histogram in the Nature paper's extended data, of how the index is distributed, showed no papers at the maximum value of one. Holst and colleagues traced that to a bug in the plotting library the authors used, which silently dropped the largest values. The papers at one were there in the analysis, nearly a million of them in Web of Science, along with a hundred and forty-two thousand patents. When Holst's team took those out, the decline in papers vanished, and the decline in patents shrank.

The reason is a discontinuity. A paper with zero recorded references scores exactly one if anyone cites it, since nobody can cite its predecessors, and such papers were more common, as a share of the records, in the earlier decades. In their sample from an open citation database, zero references were listed for ninety-seven percent of the papers at one, and for seventy-eight percent of the patents. They drew a hundred of each at random and opened the originals. Ninety-three of the papers and ninety-eight of the patents did list references. The zero was the database's, not the author's. Their conclusion is that the curve shows how the metadata improved, not how the science changed.

They also say why the authors' own robustness checks missed it. A regression adjusting for the number of references did not model the jump at zero, and when the team added a dummy variable for zero references, the share of variance explained rose from fifteen to ninety-five percent for papers. The random-rewiring simulation, which was meant to show that the observed decline exceeds what chance would produce, preserved each paper's reference count, so it preserved the zeros and reproduced the same decline. The mechanism is internally consistent and the checks are specific. It is also a preprint, as I read it, and I looked for a published answer from Park, Leahey and Funk and did not find one. A disagreement between two sides, one of which I could not hear, should be left open.

Where that leaves the third study is clear enough. For patents, the decline is mostly an artefact, on the account of the one team that did the correction. For papers, the decline has a serious challenger and no visible reply. The narrow-citation findings, the rise in self-citation and in citation of the already cited, are different measurements and the zero-reference problem does not obviously touch them, though I have not seen them re-checked. And the finding that the absolute count of highly disruptive works holds steady is untouched by both replies, and it is a reasonably Bush-compatible result: a frontier with the same number of breakthroughs a year, in a literature that has grown many times larger.

Chapter Seven: Three Rulers, One Treadmill

Put the three together and ask what each one measured.

Bloom and colleagues measured the cost of holding a growth rate constant. That is a fact about a treadmill, and it survives the objections I could find, because an exponential that stays on trend while its inputs grow exponentially is arithmetic. What the fact means is open. The world may be running out of easy ideas. Or, as the chips case suggests, society may be happy to pay more for each doubling because a doubling is worth more. Or the economy may be converting ideas into goods less well. The identity cannot separate these.

Collison and Nielsen measured expert opinion about rank, and it gives a flat line from the weakest ruler of the three: the thing it measures is the judgment of people who read the prizes in hindsight.

Park and colleagues measured how later work cites earlier work, and the main number turned out to be partly a property of the database.

So the pincer I described earlier has a different shape. The parts that hold are the economists' arithmetic, and the Nature paper's finding on constant numbers of breakthroughs and narrowing citation. The parts that may not are the experts' downward slope and the average disruption curve. A reader who wants this literature to confirm that science is hitting a wall finds that the confirmation sits in the weaker instruments, and the strong one does not say it.

I came to this episode expecting to find that the studies agreed with each other more than their authors admitted, and that the replies to the third would be quibbles. The finding is the reverse. The three do not corroborate one another, since they measure three different things, and the one that looked like independent confirmation is the one whose ruler has been shown to bend. What still favours the accounting is a denominator, the thing a clinician-researcher asks for first. The paper supplies one: dollars, converted into skilled-wage people, for each unit of a named output. The expert survey has a denominator of money and a numerator of opinion. The citation index has no denominator at all, only a fraction of its own network.

There remains the explanation question, nature or institutions, and here the evidence is thinner than the arguments. Park and colleagues said that similar declines across fields argue against low-hanging fruit, and with that finding now contested, the argument is weaker. What is left is the statement in Bloom's paper that beta, the rate at which ideas get harder, differs enormously across sectors, from two tenths in chips to more than three in the aggregate. A single physical story about nature running dry would be hard pressed to produce a spread that wide. A story about allocation, demand and who gets to work on what could produce such variety. That is an inference from one table, and the authors do not draw it.

One finding from the previous episode applies here. Azoulay and colleagues found that fields where a star died drew in outsiders, whose work was then cited. If the Matthew effect narrows what everyone cites, then a star's shadow that keeps outsiders away would raise the cost of finding an idea in the crowded places people look, and a literature that cites a narrower range of work is what that account would predict. No one in these three studies tested that, and I offer it as a conjecture to read against the data, not as a finding.

Chapter Eight: Reading Prescription

The Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb paper runs forty pages in the American Economic Review. Read the introduction, the section on Moore's law, and the summary table with its half-lives and its beta column, which together take about an hour. Skip the medical section's derivation of years of life saved unless the methodology of mortality is your own subject; for anyone who works with disease burden it is a clever measure and takes one slow pass. I would take the cancer curve, which rises to a peak near 1990 and falls, as the example to keep.

The Collison and Nielsen essay is short, and the useful part is how it chose its ruler. I could not check whether it addresses the Nobel lag or the choice of baseline, so read it with both in mind.

For the Park paper, read the abstract and the figure of the two anchor papers, and then read the Holst reply alongside it, as the pair makes a better lesson in checking a robustness analysis than either alone. The reply is a preprint, and the Macher article is peer reviewed.

For the record, here is how this episode was grounded. I read the American Economic Review version of Bloom and colleagues in full, from the publisher's file, and the numbers are theirs. The Nature article is behind a paywall, so I read its abstract, reference list and extended data legends from the journal page, and the main text from the fifth version of the preprint. I read the Holst preprint in full, and the Macher, Rutzer and Weder paper through a fetch tool that summarised it, so those figures are as that summary reported them. The Atlantic essay I did not reach: I relied on a blog post that excerpted it, on search summaries of the survey, and I quote nothing from it beyond a phrase. Tadepalli's essay I read in summary form. Alston and Pardey and Guzey and Rischel I know only by search description. I found no published reply from the authors of the Nature paper to either critique.

A research group that wants to know whether its own line is on a treadmill has a simple version of the paper's test. Take the lab's best measurable output of the last year, and ask what it took in people and money to get that, and what the same output took ten years ago. If the output is flat and the input has grown, the next question, which the three studies never settle for the world, is whether the flat line is a plateau in what can be found or a rate set by what the group chooses to look for.
