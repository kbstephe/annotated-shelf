Can Nutritional Epidemiology Be Trusted? Ioannidis's 2018 JAMA essay against the replies of Willett and Hu, the hazelnut arithmetic, and what feeding trials can and cannot fix

John Ioannidis argued in JAMA in 2018 that nutritional epidemiology needs radical reform, and Walter Willett and Frank Hu answered that the field has earned its conclusions; this episode tests both sides against trans fat, beta carotene, the Women's Health Initiative and the inpatient feeding trial.

02 October 2026

Chapter One: A Year of Life per Hazelnut

Eat twelve hazelnuts a day, one ounce, and you will live twelve years longer. That is one year per nut. Drink three cups of coffee a day and you gain about the same twelve years. Eat a single mandarin orange, eighty grams, and the ledger shows five. In the other direction, one egg a day takes six years off your life, and two slices of bacon, thirty grams, take off a decade, which is worse than smoking.

None of these sentences comes from a diet book or a wellness podcast. They come from John Ioannidis, a Stanford physician and statistician, in a short opinion piece published in the Journal of the American Medical Association in August 2018, and he built them from published meta-analyses of prospective cohort studies. He took the relative risks that the meta-analyses reported, assumed for the sake of argument that they described causal effects across a whole lifetime, and multiplied them out against a baseline life expectancy of eighty years. The arithmetic is plain, the inputs are real, and the output is absurd. A nut a year is not a thing that happens to people.

His next step is the one that stings. Defenders of the field often say that the true associations must be even larger than the reported ones, because the self-reported diets that go into these studies are measured with error, and error tends to shrink an effect toward nothing. Fine, he says. Then eating twelve hazelnuts a day would add twenty to thirty years, not twelve. The defence makes the absurdity worse.

This is the last episode of the nutrition series, and it is a duel, so the shape needs saying early. On one side is Ioannidis, who has spent two decades arguing that most published research findings in many fields are false, and who in 2018 aimed that argument at the part of medicine that reaches the public most directly, the part that tells you what to put in your mouth. On the other side are Walter Willett and Frank Hu of the Harvard School of Public Health, who between them have run or shaped the Nurses' Health Study, the Health Professionals Follow-up Study and a generation of dietary guidance, and who regard the Ioannidis critique as an exaggeration so large that it becomes irresponsible. The question underneath is older than either of them, and the whole series has been circling it: when a disease is slow and a diet is a thing every person eats every day, what kind of evidence could ever tell you which food does what?

The earlier episodes answered that question in a way that flatters the field. Lind put twelve sailors in six pairs and the lime won in six days. Goldberger fed prisoners a corn diet and gave them pellagra, then fed it to himself and did not. Those are experiments, and they worked because the effects were enormous: a disease that kills a third of a ship's crew is not subtle. Almost everything the series has covered since has been a retreat from that clarity, into tables of national fat intake, into archive boxes of baby weights, into thresholds on growth charts. The retreat was forced. You cannot randomise a man to forty years of bacon. So the field built a method for the effects that a trial cannot reach, and this episode asks whether the method works.

I will take the argument in four stages: what Ioannidis actually claims, once the hazelnuts are set aside; the best defence of the field, in the words of the people who built it; the two cases from history that the whole quarrel keeps turning on, one where the method won and one where it failed in public; and finally the experiments, which are what everybody agrees would settle it, and what they have in fact settled.

Chapter Two: What the Essay Says

The hazelnut passage is the hook, and the argument behind it is shorter than the hook suggests. I read the essay in full from the journal's own typeset copy, and it makes five claims that depend on one another.

The first is that the implausible sizes are the signature of bias, not of biology. If everything in the diet has an effect this large, the effects cannot be independent, because a life cannot be extended by twelve years by a nut, by a coffee and by a mandarin and still end somewhere finite. He says the estimates probably reflect almost exclusively the cumulative bias in this kind of research, which is residual confounding and selective reporting.

The second is a point about correlation among foods, and it is the one a clinician should find hardest to wave away. Almost all nutritional variables travel together. People who eat vegetables also tend to exercise, to see a doctor, to smoke less, to be richer. Adjusting for the measured companions, as every cohort study does, removes the part of that tangle you thought to measure. What remains after adjustment is an association contaminated by the part you did not. Ioannidis then adds a step that turns the problem from a nuisance into a law: with large enough datasets, if one dietary variable is truly causal for an outcome, many others correlated with it will also come out statistically significant. As cohorts grow, he writes, almost all nutritional variables will be associated with almost all outcomes. Big data does not rescue the method. It guarantees that the method finds something wherever it looks.

The third is the abundance of things you could look for. There are, he says, more than two hundred fifty thousand different foods, with three hundred thousand edible plants alone, and seemingly similar foods differ in their chemical signatures; a single food can contain more than five hundred distinct polyphenols. The literature, in his reading, silently assumes that the abundant substances, fats and carbohydrates, are the ones that matter, when a rare compound, a contaminant, or something produced only by a particular way of cooking may be doing the work. Here he reaches for an analogy from genetics that gives the essay its second memorable image. For decades, geneticists ran linkage scans with a few hundred markers across the human genome and tried to tie large stretches of chromosome to disease. Each stretch contained thousands of variants, and the scans produced many papers and little usable knowledge. Studying the effect of one food, he suggests, is like studying whether a large chromosomal region raises mortality: the unit is too coarse to carry the cause. A handful of questions about what you ate last year is a few hundred microsatellites aimed at a system that rivals the genome in complexity.

The fourth is the record of the single-nutrient studies, and it is the evidence that the worry is not theoretical. Here comes the example that I would put at the centre of the essay, if the essay had a centre. Meta-analyses of prospective cohorts report that people with the highest levels of beta carotene in their blood have a relative risk of death of 0.69 compared with the lowest, with an interval from 0.59 to 0.80. That is a stronger protective effect than any of the foods in the hazelnut list. And it was measured with an assay on blood plasma, not a questionnaire, so the usual excuse about self-report does not apply. Then multiple large randomised trials gave people beta carotene and found no benefit, with intervals that exclude even a small one. The association was real, the measurement was good, and the causal reading was wrong.

The fifth claim is a set of reforms, and they are modest compared with the rhetoric. Make existing cohort data available for reanalysis by independent investigators. Report results in their totality, for all the nutritional factors measured in a cohort, with standardised methods and a standard exploration of how sensitive the conclusion is to analytic choices. Avoid causal language and policy advocacy in papers that cannot support it. Reward the nutritional epidemiologists who take up this agenda with continued funding for large trials. He is not asking for the field to be abolished. He says the community has superb scientists, and that the best of them should take ownership of the reform.

Two supporting studies by Ioannidis himself bear on how the essay's arithmetic was reached. In 2013, with Jonathan Schoenfeld, he went through the Boston Cooking-School Cook Book, took the first fifty ingredients from randomly chosen recipes, and searched the medical literature for each one's association with cancer. For forty of the fifty ingredients there was at least one study, and for twenty there were at least ten. Of two hundred sixty-four single-study assessments, one hundred ninety-one, or seventy-two percent, concluded that the food was linked to a higher or lower risk. Seventy-five percent of the risk estimates were either weakly significant or not significant at all. The thirty-six meta-analyses then pulled the whole thing back toward the floor: their median relative risk was 0.96, a null with some noise. Individual studies shouted and the pooled evidence shrugged. That, more than hazelnuts, is the empirical backbone of his case, because it comes from a sample of ingredients nobody chose for their interest.

Chapter Three: The Defence Brief

Hold the verdict for a while and build the best case for the other side, as the series' charter requires, using their own words where I can verify them.

The most complete statement of the Harvard position is a 2015 review in the journal Advances in Nutrition, by Ambika Satija, Edward Yu, Walter Willett and Frank Hu, titled as an attempt to explain nutritional epidemiology and its role in policy. It predates the JAMA essay by three years, so it was written against the earlier wave of critics, among them a 2013 paper by Archer and colleagues that had declared the national survey data on calorie intake physiologically implausible. I could find no point-by-point exchange of letters in JAMA itself. The direct reply to Ioannidis came through the press. Willett told Medscape in September 2018 that the essay was unconvincing and misrepresented the literature, and the Association of Health Care Journalists summarised his stance as a belief that Ioannidis overstates and even distorts the problems to the point of irresponsibility. Hu's reported line was that the notion that we know nothing because the science is bad is bogus, and that a great deal is known about the basic elements of a healthy diet. The long argument is therefore the 2015 paper, which I read on the open-access archive, plus the interviews.

The 2015 paper makes four moves.

The first is about measurement. Food frequency questionnaires, the long checklists on which a participant reports how often they ate each of a list of foods, are validated against better reference measures, and the typical correlations for individual nutrients or foods run from about 0.4 to 0.7. Repeating the questionnaire over the years in a long cohort improves that, and statisticians can use the validity coefficients to correct the estimates for error. Biomarkers, where they exist, allow a triangulation. The instruments are blunt but calibrated, and the calibration is itself research. The authors also rebut the NHANES critique on its own ground: a single twenty-four-hour recall captures a day, which tends to catch extremes, and was never meant to estimate a person's usual intake; the survey added a second recall in 2002 for that reason. The point of that rebuttal is that critics have sometimes tested an instrument against a job it was never hired to do.

The second move is that the randomised trial, the gold standard Ioannidis wants more of, has structural problems for diet that the drug paradigm hides. Dietary trials cannot be blinded. Weight-loss trials routinely lose thirty to forty percent of participants within a year. Compliance fails, and the paper's best-known example is the Women's Health Initiative, whose eight-year low-fat arm never got close to its target of cutting fat intake by a fifth, and which became obsolete while it ran, because the scientific question shifted from total fat to the type of fat. A trial built to answer a 1990s question reported in 2006, after the question had changed.

The third move is the list of wins. This is the section a defender would hand a sceptic first, and its entries need a careful reading in the next chapter, since they are the real test. Trans fat, folate and neural tube defects, and sugar-sweetened beverages all appear, with the claim that the Bradford Hill considerations of strength, consistency, temporality and dose-response were met and that public-health action was justified by the evidence at the time.

The fourth move is a defence of the prospective cohort as a design: because exposure is recorded before disease, reverse causation is limited, lagged analyses remove the first years of follow-up when illness could be changing eating, and the long duration lets a study see what a two-year trial cannot. At its best, the authors say, a cohort can simulate a randomised trial. They also concede the failures. They note that a widely cited meta-analysis which found overweight people had lower mortality was distorted by reverse causation, because chronic disease causes weight loss years before death, and that a meta-analysis of fat and heart disease failed to specify what replaced the saturated fat in the diet. The field's own leaders are quick to name the errors made by meta-analysts, and slower to apply the same suspicion to a favourite cohort.

I do not want to caricature the other side's reply as complacent. Their strongest sentence, as far as I can reconstruct it from the interviews and the paper, is that Ioannidis is arguing about foods and they are arguing about patterns, and that its record of being right sits where the effect was large or the mechanism could be checked. That is a real argument, and it is also a concession, because it divides the field into a part to trust and a part not to. The remaining question is where the line falls.

Chapter Four: The Case Where the Method Won

Trans fat is the field's best story, and I will run it to its end, since both sides use it.

Partially hydrogenated vegetable oil, the margarine and shortening fat of the twentieth century, was introduced in part because it seemed healthier than butter. Its harm was first suggested by the cohort studies. As I recall from training, the Nurses' Health Study reported in the 1990s that women who ate more trans fat had higher rates of heart disease, with an effect larger than the one seen for saturated fat. By the numbers in the 2015 review, a meta-analysis in 2006 estimated that replacing 2 percent of energy from carbohydrate with the same energy from trans fat would raise the risk of ischaemic heart disease by twenty-nine percent, with an interval from eleven to forty-nine percent. Note the unit, a fiftieth of the day's energy, about forty calories on a two-thousand-calorie day. That is a small, concrete, bounded exposure, and the effect is moderate and plausible. Nobody doubts that you can lose a decade of life to something, but you could never get a decade from forty calories of an oil; the estimate lives in a range that biology can support.

Then came the second kind of evidence, which matters more than the first. Controlled feeding experiments, which put people on measured diets for weeks and checked their blood, showed that trans fat raised the harmful LDL cholesterol and lowered the protective HDL, a double hit that no other fat produced. That is from my training knowledge and not from a source verified this session, so I hold it at lower precision, but the finding is old and has been repeated many times. A cohort association, a large effect on a bounded exposure and a mechanism confirmed by feeding trials: the three together made a case that no one needed a thirty-year endpoint trial to believe. The policy followed. New York City restricted trans fat in restaurants, the review reports that the bans removed about seventy-two percent of trans fat from the American food supply, and heart disease rates fell faster in the municipalities that acted, with an estimate of between five hundred eighteen and one thousand thirty-seven deaths a year prevented in New York State.

Folate has a similar shape. Observational work and trials linked low folate in pregnancy to neural tube defects, the United States authorised fortification of grain in 1996 and made it mandatory by 1998, and the incidence of the defects fell by nineteen percent within five years. Here the final proof was a population-level natural experiment, close to the Goldberger pattern from the pellagra episode: you change the exposure for everyone and watch the count.

What do these wins have in common, and does it support the field or the critic? I think it supports both, and that split is the interesting result. They are all cases in which the cohort was one of three or four legs. In each there was a feeding or biochemical experiment that put a mechanism under the association, or an intervention at the scale of a population that tested it directly. And the exposures are specific, nameable and measurable: a particular fat, a particular vitamin, a particular drink. Ioannidis never says that nutrition science has produced no knowledge. He says that one kind of study, the association between a food and a lifespan, cannot be trusted alone. The history of trans fat is a demonstration of that sentence, not a rebuttal of it. The cohort pointed, and the feeding studies confirmed.

The defenders are right to bring up the cases, and the sceptic should not wave them away. But the cases do not license the next step, which is to treat a new cohort association about, say, a dietary pattern or a food group as if it had the standing of trans fat. The reason is the thing the series has been teaching since the first episode: whether an evidence chain has more than one leg.

Chapter Five: The Case Where It Failed

Now the other history, the one the field would rather not make the centre of the story.

In the 1980s and early 1990s, people who ate more fruit and vegetables, and people with more carotenoids in their blood, had less lung cancer and less heart disease in observational studies, repeatedly and in many populations. Beta carotene, the orange pigment in carrots, became the favoured candidate because it was a plausible antioxidant, easy to measure, and easy to put in a pill. The cohort evidence was strong in the ways that the 2015 review praises: prospective designs, large samples, consistent results across countries, a dose-response gradient. A reasonable reviewer in 1990 applying the Hill considerations would have found most of them satisfied.

Then the trials. As I recall from training, and this is the part I hold at hedged precision, two large randomised trials in the 1990s, one in Finnish male smokers and one in American smokers and asbestos workers, gave beta carotene to people at high risk of lung cancer, and found not a reduction in lung cancer but an increase, in the range of a fifth to a quarter. The trials were stopped early. Later trials of vitamin E and other antioxidants in other populations found nothing useful. The Ioannidis essay compresses all of this into one sentence about multiple large trials excluding even a small benefit, and it uses the beta carotene meta-analysis of blood levels, relative risk 0.69, as its exhibit.

The exhibit works because it removes the excuse that the field gives for the hazelnut results. Self-report error cannot explain a failure measured by a blood assay. What the assay measured was real beta carotene in real plasma. What the assay could not measure is why the people with high blood levels had them: because they ate a diet rich in vegetables, and fruit, and everything else that goes with a particular kind of life. Beta carotene was the marker, and the cause, if there was one, was elsewhere in the bundle. That is a confounding problem with a different anatomy from the usual one, because the confounder was the diet itself and not a lifestyle variable that someone forgot to adjust for. No amount of statistical adjustment on the data in hand could have found it, and the only tool that found it was an experiment.

There are two echoes of earlier episodes here, and the reader of the series will hear both. The first is the protein episode and its aflatoxin lesson: a diagnosis hardens around a cause, the cause proves a bystander, and the correction takes decades. The second is the Barker episode, where Huxley's critique showed that adjusting for current weight can manufacture an association out of a statistical artefact. In both, the failure was not dishonesty. It was a method working exactly as designed on a question it was not able to answer.

The defenders have a response to this, and it is a good one. Beta carotene was a one-molecule bet, the kind nutritional epidemiology has itself moved away from. Willett and Hu have argued for years for the study of foods and dietary patterns, on the ground that people eat meals and not molecules, and the beta carotene debacle is an argument for them. A Mediterranean pattern, a prudent pattern, a diet high in whole grains and low in refined carbohydrate and processed meat: these, they say, are the right unit of analysis. That is a fair defence. But it moves the question to the second ground of the Ioannidis critique, which is that patterns are more entangled with the rest of a life than single nutrients are. A person who eats a Mediterranean pattern is, on average, a different person from one who does not, in ways no questionnaire records. The move from molecules to meals sheds one confound and acquires another.

Chapter Six: The Trial That Settled Little

So bring in the experiment. Everyone, on both sides, says that randomised trials are the way out. Ioannidis says some additional large, long-term randomised trials may be useful, especially for assessing dietary patterns, and holds up the trial of the Mediterranean diet in Spain, PREDIMED, as the most promising large trial to date. The Harvard authors cite the same trial. Seven thousand four hundred forty-seven participants, at high cardiovascular risk, followed for about four and a half years, were assigned to one of two Mediterranean arms, one given extra-virgin olive oil and one given mixed nuts, or to advice to eat a low-fat diet. The diets beat the control on a composite of heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death.

Scoring the duel gets hard here, because both sides see their own point in the same trial. In 2018 the New England Journal of Medicine retracted and republished it. Investigators discovered that randomisation had been subverted for part of the sample, as I understand the journal's correction, in ways such as assigning whole households or clinics together. The reanalysis, excluding the affected groups and adjusting for the rest, gave results close to the original: the reduction in the composite endpoint was around thirty percent. The defenders say that the trial's finding survived a stress test. Ioannidis says, in the essay's phrase, that the study should no longer be considered a randomised trial, and he adds that the trial showed no survival benefit. Both are right. A result that survives a reanalysis is robust to that particular error, but a trial that was partly not randomised no longer enjoys the special protection against confounding that justified running it. And the endpoint was a composite, which pools events of very different weights, with no difference in total deaths.

PREDIMED shows what the trial can do and what it cannot. It can test a pattern, as a pattern, with hard events. It cannot do so without large costs, long follow-up, and a population that has to keep eating the way it was told for years. The Women's Health Initiative shows the other face. Its Dietary Modification Trial randomised 48,835 postmenopausal women, forty percent to a low-fat intervention and sixty percent to a comparison group, with a goal of cutting fat to twenty percent of energy and adding vegetables, fruit and grains. The difference in the percentage of energy from fat between the two groups was about ten points in year one and had narrowed to about eight by year six, and follow-up averaged eight years. The headline findings, as I remember them and hold at hedged precision, were a small and statistically uncertain reduction in breast cancer, with a hazard ratio near 0.91 and an interval that touched one, and no clear effect on heart disease. The trial cost hundreds of millions of dollars and settled little, and it did not settle the fat question because the groups did not differ by enough. The defenders read that as proof that a null result in a trial with weak adherence is uninformative; the sceptics read it as proof that when you test the cohort-derived hypothesis directly, the effect is small or absent. Both readings are legitimate, and the trial, by design, cannot choose between them.

There is a third kind of experiment, and it is the one I find most useful, because it is cheap, short, and does something the others cannot. In 2019 Kevin Hall and colleagues at the National Institutes of Health reported in Cell Metabolism a feeding trial in which twenty adults lived in a clinical research ward for a month. One fortnight was ultra-processed food and the other was unprocessed food, in random order, with meals designed to match for presented calories, energy density, sugar, sodium, fibre and macronutrients. They could eat as much as they wanted. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about five hundred eight calories a day more, mostly from carbohydrate and fat and almost none from protein, and they gained weight, while on the unprocessed diet they lost it.

Look at what that design buys. Nobody was free-living. Every plate was weighed. The confounding that sinks the cohort studies, with its wealth, exercise, education, and the thousand other things that travel with eating habits, was removed by the method of assignment and the locked door. The result is a causal one: in these twenty people, over two weeks, a food environment increased how much they ate. That is a finding of a size and kind that a cohort could never certify. It is also a finding about calories and weight, not about death. The trial cannot tell you what happens over thirty years, whether the effect fades, whether the participants would behave the same way at home, or whether weight change is the pathway to disease. Twenty adults living in a hospital for a month are a sample of a very particular kind, and the mechanism it tests is a short-horizon one.

This is the real division of labour that the whole argument points to, and the Ioannidis essay and the Harvard paper agree on more of it than their tone suggests. Feeding trials are strong on mechanism and weak on duration. Large pragmatic trials such as PREDIMED are strong on endpoints and weak on adherence and purity. Cohorts are strong on duration and weak on cause. No one design reaches all three, and the cases where nutrition science has been confidently right, trans fat and folate, combined at least two.

Chapter Seven: The Reforms, Tested

The essay's reforms are the part that I think the Harvard side underweights, and each one can be asked what it would actually change.

Data sharing first. If the Nurses' Health Study and the European cohort called EPIC, each of which has produced more than a thousand papers, made their data available, independent teams could ask whether a headline association survives a different set of covariates, a different coding of the questionnaire, a different lag. The field already knows this can be done. Ioannidis's 2013 paper in the BMJ on implausible results, cited in the essay, argues that one source of apparent signal is the freedom of the analyst to choose among many defensible models. A single published association out of a cohort of a hundred thousand, built from one set of choices, has a different meaning from the same association in a table that shows it under all of them.

Reporting in totality is the cookbook problem solved by design. When a cohort publishes the effect of one food on one outcome, the reader has no way to know how many other foods and outcomes were examined and left unreported. Schoenfeld and Ioannidis found that single studies reported a large proportion of significant results and the pooled analyses were close to null. A required table of all associations tested would turn each paper from an announcement into a dataset.

Dropping the causal language is the cheapest of the reforms, and the one with the largest effect on the public. The essay points out that a paper on coffee and mortality, from the European cohorts, was one of the twenty highest-scoring papers of 2017 in the attention metric called Altmetric, with its survival benefit given major coverage. Authors add caveats; the press ignores them. A cohort paper that says it found an association, and that the association is not an estimate of what would happen if you changed your coffee habits, cannot be shorn of that sentence by a headline writer without the headline being visibly wrong.

I would add a further test that does not come from either side, and it is the one the hazelnut arithmetic itself suggests. Before believing an association between a food and a life, convert it to years and divide by the dose. A year per hazelnut fails at once. A relative risk of 1.29 for forty calories of trans fat passes, because the unit is small and the effect is bounded, and because a second kind of evidence stands underneath. The Harvard authors might object that the conversion is unfair, because relative risks in a cohort are comparisons between the top and bottom of an intake distribution and not per-nut effects. That is true of the way the meta-analyses are built, but it does not rescue the result: whichever way the arithmetic is arranged, it is the same life being spent.

Chapter Eight: Scoring the Duel

Here is my own verdict, and it is, as in the last two episodes, a split.

Ioannidis is right about the class of claim he attacks: food-by-food, single-study, lifespan-scale associations in observational data, interpreted causally and handed to the public. The cookbook study shows that such claims are generated at industrial scale from weak evidence. The hazelnut arithmetic shows that the pooled estimates cannot all be causal. The beta carotene history shows that excellent measurement, a plausible mechanism and a large consistent association can all coexist with a wrong causal reading. If that were the whole of nutritional epidemiology, the verdict would be that it cannot be trusted alone, and that most of its output should be read as hypotheses.

He is wrong, or at least unhelpful, in letting the reader take that class to be the whole field. The cases in which the field has been right are as real as the cases in which it has been wrong, and they have a recognisable structure: specific exposures, moderate effects, a second kind of evidence under the first. His essay does mention that deficiencies, extreme overconsumption and obesity can raise mortality, and it grants that some trials agree with the cohorts. But the rhetoric of radical reform lumps trans fat with hazelnuts, and a reader who finished the essay with the first impression would not learn how to tell them apart. The Harvard authors are right about that, and their reply would have been stronger if it had said so in those terms, and had made the cut themselves.

The defenders, for their part, are wrong about the thing the field is least willing to hear. Their answer to the problem of error is a good answer to attenuation, to the worry that mismeasurement hides true effects. It is not an answer to differential error, or to confounding that no questionnaire records, and those were the worries Ioannidis raised. A correlation of 0.5 between a questionnaire and a reference measure says the instrument is usable. It does not say that the person who reports eating more vegetables is otherwise comparable to the person who does not. The beta carotene result is the evidence that the two come apart, and the 2015 paper, which names failures of meta-analysis in its own list, does not name that one.

So the practical rule I would take from the duel is a rule about reading, not about the field. When a nutritional claim arrives, ask what stands under the cohort. A feeding trial on the mechanism, a trial on the endpoint, a natural experiment at the scale of a country: if there is at least one such leg, the claim has earned a hearing. If the claim rests on the cohort alone, and the effect, converted into years, cannot be spent by a human life, treat it as a hypothesis with a press release attached.

As a reading prescription for the series, start with the Ioannidis essay itself, because it is three pages, with a hazelnut table to hang an argument on, and then read the 2015 Harvard review for the other side. Between them is everything the rest of the literature repeats at greater length. Skip the commentary that declares a winner without saying which kind of claim was being scored.

The series began with a lime and ends with a hazelnut, and the distance between them is the whole story. When the effect is enormous and the cause is a single missing substance, six pairs of sailors are enough. Where the effect is small and the cause is everything that travels with a way of eating, the method that worked on scurvy has nothing to say, and the field has to borrow strength from cohorts, from feeding wards, from the occasional enormous trial, and from the policy experiments that the world runs on its own. No one of them is a hero. Taken together they have been right oftener than the loudest critics allow, and wrong with a frequency the confident guidelines did not admit.

The generative question for the working week is for anyone who reads effect sizes. The next time a result tells you what a habit does, a drug, a diet, a management practice or a screening programme, convert it into a unit that a single life could check, years or dollars or patients per hundred, and divide by the dose. If the answer is a year per hazelnut, the study is describing its own biases. Then ask what kind of evidence, other than that study, would have to exist for the effect to be real, and whether anyone has looked.

Next time, the series moves from nutrition to science as an institution, beginning with Vannevar Bush's 1945 report, Science, the Endless Frontier, and the gap between what it said and what it is cited for. Thanks for listening.
