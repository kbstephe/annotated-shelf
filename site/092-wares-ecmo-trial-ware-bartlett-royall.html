Ware's ECMO Trial (Ware, Bartlett and Royall): whom does the next dying infant belong to?

Two American trials of lung bypass in newborns, one with a single control infant and one that stopped randomising at the fourth death, the statisticians who called them unethical from opposite sides, and the British trial of 185 infants that settled what they could not.

27 November 2026

Chapter One: One Ball of Each Colour

Between October 1982 and April 1984, fifty newborns heavier than two kilograms arrived in the intensive care unit at the University of Michigan in severe respiratory failure, intubated and breathing pure oxygen. Most were referred from other hospitals because Michigan could do something they could not. Thirty-six got better on the ventilator. Fourteen met a set of criteria the team had written in advance to pick out infants with at least an eighty percent chance of dying despite the best conventional care. Two of those had conditions that ruled out the machine, and both died. The other twelve were randomised.

The new treatment was extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, ECMO for short, a simplified heart-lung machine. A cannula in the jugular vein drains blood from the right atrium, a pump pushes it across a membrane where it takes up oxygen, and it goes back into the body, so that for days the lungs can be left almost alone to heal. Robert Bartlett's group had been doing this for years in infants judged to be dying. In their uncontrolled series of fifty-five moribund patients, more than half survived. They wanted proof of a kind that series could not give, and they did not want to withhold the machine from alternate babies to get it.

So they used an urn. The design, which Marvin Zelen had proposed in 1969 and L. J. Wei and S. Durham had worked out in 1978, is called randomised play-the-winner. Picture an urn holding one ball per treatment. Each new patient gets a draw, which is noted and put back. A survival on a treatment adds a ball of that treatment's colour; a death adds one of the other colour. A treatment that keeps winning crowds the urn, and the next patient is more likely to get it. The department of biostatistics kept the assignments in sealed envelopes labelled only A and B, and the statistician did not know which letter meant ECMO.

The first infant drew ECMO and survived. The second drew conventional care. He had entered at five days old, older than any of the other eleven, through the category the protocol called acute deterioration, and despite everything the unit could do he died on his thirteenth day; at autopsy his lungs were scarred with bronchopulmonary dysplasia and fibrosis. After two patients, the urn held three ECMO balls and one conventional ball. The third infant drew ECMO and lived. So did the fourth, and the fifth, and every infant after, as the conventional ball's share of the urn shrank from a quarter to a fifth to a sixth. The stopping rule ended randomisation when ten balls of one colour had been added. Two more infants who met the criteria were given ECMO directly. All eleven ECMO infants survived to leave hospital. Bartlett's paper in Pediatrics in October 1985 said the study proved that ECMO improves survival.

It had compared eleven infants with one. Nobody, then or since, has thought one infant was a control group, and the paper drew two commentaries in the same journal, one by Nigel Paneth and Sylvan Wallenstein and one by James Ware and Michael Epstein, both uneasy. Ware and Epstein's verdict was that the results were not completely convincing, primarily because only one patient had received the standard therapy.

What followed was a second trial at Harvard, designed by Ware, a biostatistician at the Harvard School of Public Health, and then a published argument among statisticians over whether either trial should have been run at all. Ware's paper in Statistical Science in 1989 ran with its discussions and a rejoinder to some forty pages, much of the field's best talent disagreeing in public about dying infants. They disagreed about everything, and the disagreement is the subject of this episode. The question underneath it is older than ECMO: when the evidence inside a trial starts to favour one arm, whom does the next patient belong to? To herself, to the patients who will come after her, or to the people who must be persuaded?

Chapter Two: What the Urn Bought and What It Cost

Start with the case for the urn, which is stronger than its single control makes it look.

Bartlett gave three reasons for the design. The outcome of each case was known within days, so the urn could be updated before the next infant arrived. The team expected most ECMO infants to live and most controls to die, so a modest number of patients might settle it. And it met what they called the scientific and ethical dilemma halfway: they felt compelled to run a prospective randomised study and reluctant to withhold a lifesaving treatment from alternate patients merely to satisfy conventional allocation. The design they chose sounds like a compromise, and in one sense it was a principled one. Every assignment was truly random, and no clinician, parent or investigator could steer an infant into one arm. What the urn changed was the odds, and it changed them only in response to outcomes.

The sample size was set on what the authors called a selection paradigm. The question was not whether a test would reject a null hypothesis but whether, at the end, the trial would pick the better treatment. With a difference in survival of at least forty points, ten patients gave at least a ninety-five percent chance of choosing correctly, and with the survival rates the team believed, ninety percent on ECMO and ten percent on conventional care, the chance was essentially one.

Now run the urn forward under those beliefs. I simulated it, two hundred thousand times, which is my arithmetic and not anything in the papers: ten infants, one ball of each colour to start, ECMO saving nine in ten and the ventilator one in ten. About a quarter of the time, the ten infants include one control or none. The lone control infant was not a freak. He was what the design produces, often, when the new treatment is as good as its inventors hoped. And the same simulation shows why the lopsidedness is itself evidence. If the two treatments had been equal, each saving half, an allocation that lopsided toward ECMO would turn up about three times in a hundred, and in either direction about six. A trial that ends eleven to one is telling you something about the treatments through the allocation alone. Wei later computed an exact one-sided p-value for the Michigan data that took the design into account, and got 0.051.

The cost of the design is in the same paper.

Begin with the selection paradigm. Ware and Epstein pointed out, in their 1985 commentary, that if the two treatments were equally effective, a trial built to pick a winner would declare the innovation superior in half of all trials. The design has no null hypothesis to fail to reject; it always crowns someone. Donald Berry, then at the University of Minnesota, answered in 1989 that this complaint averages over data that did not occur, and that the eleven-to-one data were not equivocal however the procedure might behave in other worlds. That is a real disagreement about what inference is, and it returns later.

The second cost cannot be argued away by any school of inference. With one control, everything the trial can say about conventional care rests on one infant, and that infant was unusual. Bartlett's own table records the control infant's age at randomisation as a hundred and forty hours. The ECMO infants entered at ages from ten hours to a hundred and thirty-seven, most of them within the first three days. His blood was the most acid of the twelve. The commentators noted that the one control was the most severely ill patient in the study. Nobody chose that; the urn did. But the trial's answer to the question of how infants like these fare without ECMO is the fate of one older, sicker infant, and the eighty percent mortality the criteria were meant to guarantee was asserted from the unit's past experience rather than shown in the paper.

The series met this in Senn's red die, four episodes ago. Randomisation does not promise balance in any one trial. What it promises is an honest error and an allocator's mind kept out of the comparison. Michigan's urn kept the mind out perfectly, and the error it made honest was enormous, because there was almost nothing on one side of the comparison to be wrong about. Bartlett conceded the design point himself: in retrospect, he wrote, it would have been better to start the urn with two or three pairs of balls, which would probably have produced more than one control.

One more fact in the table changes what "all survived" means. The eleven ECMO infants survived to discharge. Two of them carried conditions nobody had diagnosed before the machine went on: one infant had an anomaly of the pulmonary veins that was repaired and months later proved fatal, and another had a congenital muscle disease that eventually killed the child. Neither death belongs to ECMO, and the paper is candid about both. But it shows how narrow the outcome was. Survival to discharge from a neonatal unit measured whether the lungs had been rested long enough to work, which was the question, and nothing else. A third infant on ECMO had a brain haemorrhage with severe neurological deficit, and that was the worry the sceptics kept raising: that a machine requiring full anticoagulation in a newborn might trade death for disability.

So in 1985 the evidence was a dramatic uncontrolled series, one randomised trial with one control who was the sickest child in it, and an unanswered question about brains. The Harvard group looked at that and decided it was not enough.

Chapter Three: Four Deaths

Ware and his medical colleagues at Boston's Children's Hospital and the Brigham and Women's Hospital reviewed the Michigan trial and the ECMO registries in 1985 and concluded, in his words, that the data did not justify routine use without further study. They were uneasy about a new and potentially dangerous technology spreading on thin randomised evidence. They also shared Michigan's fear: that if early results in a new trial again suggested dramatic benefit, continuing to randomise would become ethically intolerable.

Their own past made the fear concrete. A chart review found thirty-nine infants with persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn treated at the two hospitals in 1982 and 1983. Thirteen met a definition of severe disease, persistently low oxygen in the aorta between twelve and seventy-two hours after birth on maximal medical therapy, and eleven of those thirteen died. All had received conventional care. That definition became the trial's eligibility rule.

Ware discussed designs with Zelen and settled on one that is easy to describe. Randomise in blocks of four, two to each arm in random order. When one arm reaches a fixed count of deaths, stop randomising and give every later patient the other treatment, until either that arm reaches the same count or enough of its patients have survived to establish its superiority. He set the number at four. Planning on twenty percent survival with conventional care against eighty percent with ECMO, a four-death design had power of 0.77 at a one-sided five percent level; three deaths would have given 0.55, and five, 0.84. Consent was handled by Zelen's randomised-consent design: only families whose infants drew ECMO were approached. Parents whose babies drew conventional care were not told there was a trial, or a machine.

The trial began on the sixth of February 1986. Nineteen infants were randomised. Ten received conventional therapy, the last of them patient nineteen, and four of the ten died. Nine received ECMO, and all nine lived. At the fourth conventional death, randomisation stopped. The doctors caring for the infants were not told at once, but the treatment could not be blinded, and it soon became obvious that every new patient was going on the machine. The second phase was to run until the twenty-eighth ECMO survivor or the fourth ECMO death. Twenty more infants were enrolled before the trial ended on the first of July 1988. Nineteen survived.

Take the randomised phase alone, because that is what the argument is about. Four deaths in ten against none in nine gives a one-sided p-value from Fisher's exact test of 0.054. Ware calls what comes next ironic. A conventional trial testing the same hypotheses with five percent and twenty percent error rates would have needed only ten infants per arm. The first nineteen came within one patient of completing it, and because of the blocking, the twentieth would have been assigned to ECMO, the treatment that infant in fact received. Count that infant, and four deaths in ten against none in ten gives a p-value of 0.043. Harvard ran a design built to avoid the conventional trial and arrived, nearly to the patient, at the conventional trial's sample size.

Ware then did something unusual for a frequentist in 1989. He computed posterior probabilities. With a prior that put a third of its weight on the treatments being equal and split the rest evenly between ECMO better and ECMO worse, the probability that ECMO was inferior, given the nineteen randomised infants, came to 0.01. With a prior built from the thirteen historical infants, it fell to 0.0004. Over a wide range of priors, he wrote, the chance that the machine had the lower mortality was at least ninety percent, and given that analysis it was difficult to defend further randomisation ethically.

There was a dissent inside the trial, and Ware reported it in his rejoinder. After ten infants had entered the second phase, the investigators asked four colleagues, two neonatologists and two experienced biostatisticians, to review the study. The four advised resuming randomisation: the long-term morbidity was unknown and the mortality data were not definitive. The investigators said they understood that view and declined.

Two last details from Boston. During the trial, five eligible infants at the two hospitals were never enrolled: in three cases no physician or technician was available to run ECMO, and in two every machine was in use. Two of those five died and two survived on conventional care; the fifth got ECMO elsewhere and lived. And the confidence interval Ware reported was, as D. Y. Lin and Wei showed in their discussion, too generous for so small and adaptive a design; recomputed exactly, it barely cleared significance at five percent.

A trial designed in fear of a dramatic result had produced one, and the result was 0.054.

Chapter Four: Disturbing From Both Sides

The discussion that followed in Statistical Science is the reason this trial is still taught. It ran from one wall to the other.

Richard Royall, at Johns Hopkins, opened his comment plainly: this study is deeply disturbing to me. He believed no infant should have been randomised to conventional therapy at all, and that given that some were, hiding the trial from their parents was a grave error. His argument against starting the trial uses Ware's own tools. Take Ware's two priors and apply them not to Harvard's nineteen infants but to Michigan's ten randomised ones, the data available before Harvard enrolled anybody. The posterior probability that ECMO was inferior comes out at 0.01 with the first prior and 0.00003 with the second. Those are the numbers Ware used to say that further randomisation was difficult to defend. Applied before the trial, Royall wrote, the same reasoning makes "further randomisation" mean the first baby entered at Harvard.

Ware's reply was that Michigan's ten patients were an important piece of evidence but that treatment strategies for hundreds if not thousands of future patients should not be set by such scanty evidence. Royall had anticipated the move and followed it out. Suppose Michigan's results could not be trusted at Harvard, because patients, staff and procedures differed. Then Harvard's findings would be no use at Johns Hopkins, which would need its own trial, and so on through every hospital in the land; and since populations and practices also change over time, last year's results at Harvard would not settle tomorrow's decisions there either. An argument that refuses to extrapolate from Michigan ends by making every randomised trial a matter of local, temporary interest, including the one it is being used to justify.

On consent, Royall did not accept Ware's defence. Ware had argued that approaching distraught parents for consent to an invasive procedure that might then not be given would be cruel. That might be true, Royall replied, but it does not explain why the parents of infants assigned to conventional care were not told afterwards about the study, the alternative and their right to decline. For an infant for whom conventional therapy held little hope, the parents were never told that a highly promising alternative existed.

Berry went further. He titled a section with the emperor's new clothes. His case against randomisation in general began with a commute. Drive route A for a week and it takes about thirty minutes every day; route B, the next week, takes roughly twenty. Now admit you flipped a coin each day and happened to get five As and then five Bs. Should that change what anyone concludes? If the driver discloses everything he knew, Berry argued, the data should be analysed the same way whether or not a coin was involved. In his view the historical evidence for ECMO already outweighed anything Harvard could add, the Harvard study should not have been conducted, and the parents should have been given all available information and allowed to choose. Most or all, he thought, would have chosen ECMO. On Ware's attempt to balance ethical and scientific concerns, he allowed no compromise: ethical concerns win. He closed with ten numbered conclusions, the second of which was that randomised trials are inherently unethical and inappropriate for life-threatening conditions, and the last of which proposed replacing them with comprehensive national patient registries.

From the other wall came Colin Begg, at Memorial Sloan-Kettering, who praised the study as careful and thoughtful and then said the randomised phase had been stopped too soon. His reasoning began with what a randomised trial is for. New treatments are discovered in uncontrolled series; the trial's job is to confirm or refute, and to be conclusive in its own right, and this looked like the only legitimate trial of neonatal ECMO that would ever be run. Was it conclusive? Ware's 0.054 was one-sided; the two-sided Fisher test gives 0.09. Then Begg ran a test anyone can run. Change one infant's outcome. If seven of the ten conventional infants had survived instead of six, the p-value becomes 0.21. If one ECMO infant had died, eight of nine against six of ten, it becomes 0.31. A conclusion that one infant can overturn is not a conclusion. As for the Bayesian analysis, a prior built from the historical patients is reasonable only if historical and randomised data are equally reliable, and if you believe that, he wrote, there is no point in a randomised trial in the first place.

Begg then turned the question on himself. Asked which treatment he would choose for a child of his own, he said ECMO, without hesitation, and he would have chosen it even if the data had been seven of nine against six of ten, because when your own neck is on the line, you always choose what appears best. If everyone did that, he went on, the resulting anarchy would destroy the evidence medicine runs on. He predicted that stopping early would leave ECMO used by its devotees while its wider acceptance stalled.

Between them sat Peter Armitage, at Oxford, writing with a research student, Stephen Coad. They found the evidence for ECMO patchy but fairly convincing, and they found it so partly by luck. The infants in the second phase were apparently at higher risk than those in the first: the eligibility rule had been tightened, and more were outborn, which in this trial went with higher mortality. Had that not been so, they would have been only moderately impressed, because nothing guaranteed that the two phases were comparable, and a drift in the other direction would have flattered ECMO with no way to tell. They had simulated adaptive rules under gradual trends and found the damage modest; a sudden change in allocation, as between Harvard's phases, they called potentially catastrophic. And they made an observation that matters more as the stakes rise. The harder a design pushes allocation toward the apparently better arm, the less precise its estimate of the difference, and an imprecise estimate may move the medical community more slowly than a balanced trial would.

Set the four positions side by side. Royall and Berry say the trial took too much from the infants in it. Begg says it took too little, and so wasted what it did take. Armitage and Coad say its answer depended on an assumption nobody could check. Ware, in his rejoinder, said the obvious thing: one's view of the ethics of the study depends directly on one's assessment of the evidence that was available. That is true, and it is also not an answer, because whoever settled the evidence had already settled the ethics.

Chapter Five: The Patient Horizon

I said the disagreement ran wall to wall. I think it ran along one axis, which one discussant named and the others circled. Janis Hardwick, at Michigan, wrote that what any attempt to put numbers on a trial's ethics needs first is the patient horizon: everyone, inside the trial and after it, whose treatment its result will affect. Weight the current patients against the future ones and the problem becomes, in principle, arithmetic.

Lay the positions along that line. Berry's ten conclusions give almost all the weight to the infants in the trial, and then try to rescue the future by registries. Royall gives the infant in front of the physician a claim the future cannot override, a duty of care owed to that one patient. Ware's design spent a few control infants and stopped, protecting the trial's later patients at the price of an answer that did not quite convince. Begg gives the future most of the weight, but his future is not abstract. He writes that the global practice of medicine does not work by a trial convincing its own investigators and everyone thereafter receiving the better treatment, and that individual trials change practice only indirectly, through the many practitioners and the leaders among them who must be persuaded.

Every design in this story moves deaths from one place to another. An urn or a four-death rule reduces deaths inside the trial. Whether that is a net saving depends on what the trial's result then does outside it, and that depends not on the probability that ECMO is better, which was high by 1986 on almost any reckoning, but on whether the people who treat the next thousand infants believe it. Berry was right that the evidence favoured ECMO. Royall was right that Harvard's own numbers, applied honestly, favoured it before the trial began. Neither point answers the question the horizon asks, which is about the audience. A one-in-a-hundred chance that ECMO was worse, which convinces Royall and does not convince the neonatologists of a country is, for the infants of that country, worth what the neonatologists do with it.

Begg's prediction can be checked, because the trial he wanted was eventually run, in another country.

Between 1993 and 1995, a British collaborative group randomised a hundred and eighty-five mature newborns with severe respiratory failure, from fifty-five hospitals, to referral to one of five specialist centres for consideration of ECMO, or to continued intensive conventional care where they were. The eligibility threshold was an oxygenation index of forty or more, and the diagnoses were broader than Boston's: persistent pulmonary hypertension from meconium aspiration, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, sepsis and others. It was a trial of a referral policy, not of the machine alone; eighty-four percent of infants allocated to ECMO actually received it. It was fixed and balanced, and it was watched by an independent data-monitoring committee, on whose advice the steering committee stopped recruitment early, in November 1995, because the accumulating data showed a clear advantage.

Thirty of the ninety-three infants allocated to ECMO died. Fifty-four of the ninety-two allocated to conventional care died. The relative risk was 0.55, with a ninety-five percent interval from 0.39 to 0.77. In the authors' plainer terms, one extra infant survived for every three to four allocated to ECMO, and the difference held across primary diagnosis, severity and type of referral centre. Among the children old enough to be assessed at a year, one in each arm had severe disability; milder impairments numbered ten on ECMO and six on conventional care, so the sceptics' worry about the brain was not baseless, only smaller than the deaths.

So the British trial confirmed what Michigan and Harvard had seen and what Berry and Royall had believed. And the price of the confirmation was paid in Britain, a decade later: fifty-four deaths on the conventional arm of a trial whose answer several of the 1989 discussants thought was already known. Some of those infants might have died on ECMO too; the comparison says about twenty-four of them would not have, on average, if the arms had been the same size and the effect as estimated. That is the patient horizon run to its end. The deaths an adaptive design spares inside a trial can reappear outside it, in a trial somebody else must run because the first one did not persuade.

The frame from two episodes back applies with a twist. The significance filter says that a small study's passing results overstate the truth, and Boston's design stopped randomising at the moment the conventional arm reached its fourth death, a moment chosen by that arm's run of bad luck. What the filter predicts is that people trained to distrust small, data-dependent stops would discount Harvard, and many did. That discount was not irrational. It was the filter working as designed, on a result that happened to be true.

Chapter Six: The Case Against the Horizon

The thesis I have been building says that a trial's ethics must be judged across the whole horizon, and that the small adaptive trials of the 1980s spared a few infants at the cost of persuasion. Berry is the place to begin testing it.

The currency of persuasion is not a law of nature. It is a convention, and statisticians helped mint it. If neonatologists in 1986 had been trained to read eleven survivals against one death, or Royall's posterior of 0.01, as strong evidence, then Michigan would have persuaded, and no British infant would have needed to be randomised. To treat the audience's scepticism as a fixed cost and charge it to the designs is to let the discipline that created the scepticism off the hook. Berry would say that the fifty-four British deaths are not the price of Ware's caution; they are the price of a culture that demands randomised confirmation of a treatment whose effect is large and visible, and the cure is to change the culture. That argument is not crazy. Some effects are large enough that the uncontrolled evidence is overwhelming, and nobody runs a randomised trial of parachutes.

My answer is that ECMO in 1985 was not a parachute. The British trial lost thirty-two percent of its ECMO infants. Harvard's conventional arm, at sixty percent survival, did three times as well as the historical figure on which every Bayesian prior in the discussion leaned, and Ware called that survival unexpectedly high. Berry's own analysis, using a prognostic model from seven hundred and fifteen registry cases, found the conventional infants at Harvard had somewhat worse average prognoses than the ECMO infants, though not enough to change the conclusion. A treatment whose benefit was this sensitive to who was enrolled and how the controls were managed is the kind on which history has fooled medicine before. Ware's rejoinder quoted a talk Charles Moertel gave at the Society for Clinical Trials in 1980, titled How to Succeed in Clinical Trials Without Really Trying. The method, in Moertel's joke, was nonrandomised studies. The audience's scepticism was learned from cases like that, and it was earned.

Royall's objection is harder, and I do not think the horizon answers it. The physician's duty of personal care is not a weight to be summed against future patients; it is a constraint. If a doctor believes, on good evidence, that one treatment is better for the infant in front of her, she may not enrol that infant in a trial that might assign the other, however many future infants would gain. On this view, the right response to 1986 was not a better-designed trial at Harvard but no trial there, and the evidence would have had to come from elsewhere: from historical controls, from hospitals without ECMO, from the five eligible Harvard infants who missed the machine because it was busy or unstaffed. Royall suggested exactly that design. Two years later he made the physician's duty of personal care the centre of a second paper in Statistical Science, with Bartlett and Zelen among its discussants; I could not obtain that paper's text and know it only by its description, so the argument here comes from the 1989 comment, where it is already plain. This is the series' streptomycin episode inverted. The Medical Research Council could randomise in 1947 because Britain did not have enough of the drug, and a lottery was the fairest way to divide a shortage. Royall's point was that Harvard had enough machines most of the time, and the shortage it needed for an ethical control group was one it would have had to manufacture. Where real scarcity existed, as in those five infants, he wanted it used.

Royall's constraint is coherent, and it is one reason an ethics committee should hear from someone who holds it. But note what it requires: that the physician's belief be well founded. Ware's whole defence was that his colleagues, with full access to the data and the clinical expertise, did not believe ECMO was better in 1986. Equipoise was not a fiction there; four colleagues looked at Harvard's results partway through the second phase and still thought randomisation should resume. The personal-care principle protects the patient from a doctor who knows better and randomises anyway. It says nothing about a doctor who does not know.

A last objection concerns my use of the British trial. It answered a different question: a referral policy, across fifty-five hospitals, in a broader population, a decade later, with five specialist centres and a transport system. Those differences are exactly what Royall warned about. If Michigan could not speak for Harvard, Harvard could hardly speak for a British network of referring hospitals, and the British trial may have been needed whatever the Americans had done. That is fair, and it weakens the arithmetic of the last chapter: I cannot say how many British deaths a persuasive American trial would have prevented, because the British may have needed their own answer anyway. What it does not weaken is the shape. The British design, fixed allocation with an independent committee empowered to stop, protected the infants inside the trial by stopping the whole trial rather than by unbalancing its arms. It stopped at a hundred and eighty-five, with an interval nobody could explain away.

That is also, in substance, the modern verdict on outcome-adaptive allocation. Peter Thall and colleagues at the MD Anderson Cancer Center, simulating adaptive schemes in 2015, found they often unbalanced allocation in the wrong direction and overstated the difference, and concluded that for confirmatory comparisons fixed randomisation with group sequential stopping is preferable, scientifically and ethically. David Robertson and colleagues, in a review published in Statistical Science in 2023, replied that the ECMO trials had left adaptive randomisation almost unused for twenty years on the strength of one example, and that gentler schemes, the original urn among them, rarely misallocate in the way Thall's most aggressive one did.

Robertson is right that the ECMO trials have been used to condemn a whole family for the faults of one member, and that the drift problem, which Armitage and Coad flagged in 1989, is a matter of degree. But the defence does not reach the case this episode is about. A two-arm confirmatory trial of a life-or-death treatment, in a single centre, with an audience that has to be persuaded, gains little from unbalancing its arms and loses the one thing it exists to produce.

Chapter Seven: Verdict

My verdict, then.

The Michigan and Harvard trials were both serious attempts to solve a problem that has no clean solution, and the people who ran them were neither reckless nor naive. Michigan's urn was a correct design for the question its authors asked, which was which treatment to choose, and wrong for the question everyone else asked, which was how much better the choice was and whether to believe it. Its single control was the predictable output of a design that worked as intended on a large effect, and the trial still left the comparison resting on one sick infant. Harvard's design was cleverer and more honest about inference, and its randomised phase stopped at a moment its own colleagues thought premature, with a result one infant could overturn. Both trials spared infants inside them. Neither persuaded, and the persuasion was eventually bought in Britain with a balanced trial that let its monitoring committee stop the whole thing.

No single discussant won the exchange. What it shows is that "ethical" in a trial is always relative to a horizon, and that the people in that 1989 exchange were drawing the horizon in different places without saying so. Berry drew it around the patients in the trial. Royall put it at the patient in front of the physician. Begg's enclosed the profession that has to change its practice. Ware tried to take in all three at once and ended with a design that satisfied none of them. The arithmetic of the British trial, about one extra survivor for every three or four infants referred, says that the stakes were real on every side of the line.

For an analysis plan, the section this bears on is the interim-monitoring paragraph, and the ECMO story asks it to say something most such paragraphs leave out. Every stopping rule spends some outcomes now to buy belief later; whose belief is it buying? A rule that halts when the investigators are convinced has been designed for an audience of the investigators. One that ends the trial when an independent committee sees evidence that would survive Begg's test, the flip of one patient's outcome, is designed for the reader who was not in the room. Harvard's table, at the moment it stopped randomising, fails that test: move one control infant from the dead column to the living one and the two-sided p-value goes from 0.09 to 0.21.

The object to look at first is Bartlett's patient table in Pediatrics, October 1985, twelve rows long, where the one control infant's age and blood gases sit beside the eleven who lived. Then Ware's paper in Statistical Science in 1989, which is short, and its discussions in order rather than only the famous ones: Berry against randomisation, Royall against starting, Begg against stopping, Armitage and Coad on combining phases, and Ware's rejoinder, which is where the four colleagues who told him to resume appear. The British trial's 1996 report in the Lancet is the ending. Thall's 2015 paper in the Annals of Oncology and Robertson's review carry the argument into the present, with Robertson as the reply.

Ware's last sentence in the original paper is about money, not inference. Since the data had been reported to the medical staff, he wrote, demand for ECMO had grown rapidly at the participating hospitals, and it would raise difficult questions about managing a demand that exceeds what the system can provide. The trial was meant to settle whether to use the machine. By the time it was published, the hospitals had decided, and the question had become who would get one.

Next time, Popper and Mayo on what a null result licenses: a trial that finds nothing, run through the severity test, to see which effects its silence actually rules out. Thanks for listening.
