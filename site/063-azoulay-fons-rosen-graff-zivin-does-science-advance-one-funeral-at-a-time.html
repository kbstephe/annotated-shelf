Does Science Advance One Funeral at a Time? (Pierre Azoulay, Christian Fons-Rosen and Joshua Graff Zivin): Planck's sentence, the 2019 study of 452 dead life-science stars, and what happens to a field when the person everyone was watching is gone

The American Economic Review study of what happens to a research field after an eminent life scientist dies, read against Planck's original remark, the earlier test on Darwin's British audience, and the question of what a grant panel can do about a star's shadow.

13 October 2026

Chapter One: A Sentence in an Old Man's Memoir

Max Planck wrote the sentence in the last years of his life, and it came out in the shape most people know only after another man had cut it down. In his scientific autobiography, in the English of the 1949 translation, he says that a new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents. It wins because the opponents eventually die and a generation grows up that is familiar with it from the start. The sentence has an earlier outing in a German article of October 1946, a memoir of old times in a physics journal, and Planck died in 1947. Nobody should read it as a research finding. It is an eighty-eight-year-old man, whose own best-known idea had once been a hard sell, looking back.

The short form came later. In June 1975 the economist Paul Samuelson wrote a column in Newsweek about the economist Alvin Hansen and said that science makes progress funeral by funeral, crediting Planck. That compression travelled much further than the original, because it is a sentence a person can say to a colleague in a corridor. It also changed the claim. Planck described a mechanism by which generations replace each other. Samuelson's version sounds like a cost-benefit statement about gatekeepers: the field is held back, in a number of funerals, by whoever is still alive.

Those are two different claims, and the study this episode covers tests the second. To see why, it helps to know that the first has been tested already, and mostly came out badly for Planck. The best-known test appeared in Science in 1978, by the philosopher David Hull and two colleagues, Peter Tessner and Arthur Diamond. They took the people who might have accepted or rejected Darwin's central claim, that species evolve, among British scientists in the decade after the Origin appeared, and asked whether the older ones held out longer. As summarized in the secondary accounts I could reach, and not checked against the paper itself, age in 1859 did not predict how fast most of them came around, and a small minority of holdouts after 1869 were on average about ten years older than the early converts. Episode fifty-nine met this result in passing, in the chapter on Kuhn and the Planck remark, and gave Hull's sample as roughly seventy-eight scientists. That figure also came from a summary, and I have not seen the paper.

Other attempts, as reported in the same kind of summary, point the same way or further. A study of plate tectonics in the 1960s found, according to the account quoted by a commenter on a rationalist forum, that the earth scientists who adopted the new picture early were on average older in career years than those who held out. A reader should treat that as a rumour about a paper, which is what it is for the purposes of this episode. The finding that matters is the general one. When researchers go looking for the old man who cannot change his mind, they find some, but not enough to explain how a field moves.

So the standard verdict on Planck is that people mostly do change their minds, at a rate that does not track their age. If that were the whole story, the 2019 paper would be a footnote. Its authors started from a different place. They suspected that the funeral does its work on something other than belief. A dead star leaves behind a field, and a field is a market in which some people have stayed out.

The question that predates the paper, and will carry the rest of this episode, is what a famous person does to the room by being in it. Episode sixty-two ended with Merton's forty-first chair, the idea that a system built on distinction manufactures the difference it then rewards. That episode asked what happens when credit flows to the credited. This one asks what happens to everyone else when the credited person stops existing, and a good deal of what follows depends on one design choice: nobody chooses when an eminent biologist dies.

Chapter Two: Seventy-Two Neighbours for a Yeast Paper

Natural experiments in the science of science have a recognizable shape by now. Episode sixty-two followed one in which investigators at the Howard Hughes Medical Institute were compared with near-misses. This one swaps the prize for a death, and the method asks two things of the researchers. They must find the people who died while still working, and they must say which part of the literature each of them left behind.

The first part is a sampling problem. The authors began with a list of 12,935 elite academic life scientists, about five percent of the relevant labor market, qualifying through some combination of heavy funding, heavy citation, patents, membership in the National Academies, Howard Hughes appointments, and early-career prizes. From that list they took the 452 who died between 1975 and 2003, while still active enough to be publishing and holding grants. The median age at death was sixty-one. Fifty-one percent died after a long illness and forty-one percent suddenly, and for the remaining eight percent the authors could not establish the circumstances. About ten percent were women. Forty percent held a medical degree. On average each of them had brought in about sixteen and a half million dollars from the National Institutes of Health over a career, published 138 papers, and accumulated about eight thousand citations.

The second part is where the paper earns its reputation. A field, here, has no editorial board and no society behind it. The authors needed a way to draw its boundary without using the social facts they wanted to measure, since defining a field by who cites whom would build the answer into the question. They used the PubMed Related Citations Algorithm, which groups papers by the words in their titles and abstracts and by the controlled subject headings that professional indexers assign at the National Library of Medicine. No citation links and no co-authorship enter the calculation.

Their illustration is a yeast paper. Ira Herskowitz, a geneticist at the University of California, San Francisco, published in Science in 1998 a description of the transcriptional program of sporulation in budding yeast, and he died in 2003 of pancreatic cancer. Fed that one paper, the algorithm returned seventy-two related articles. The second most similar was a study of a meiosis-specific transcription factor called Ndt80, with a relatedness score of ninety-four percent. The most distant of the seventy-two scored sixty-two. Some of those neighbours were written by Herskowitz's collaborators. Others were written by people who had never met him and might not have known his name, and the algorithm has no way to tell the difference. That is what the design needs.

The unit of analysis is the neighbourhood of one paper. Herskowitz had twelve last-author papers in the five years before he died, and each became its own subfield. Across the sample, 452 dead stars generated 3,076 subfields, and the authors report that these are compact. Half of all pairs of subfields from the same star had no articles in common at all.

Then comes the control group, built with some care. For each treated paper the authors looked at papers in the same journal and year whose last author was a star who was still alive, and matched on the number of authors, the age of the star, and several other features. That produced 31,142 control subfields attached to 5,809 living scientists, each given a counterfactual date of death copied from its treated partner. The comparison asks, in effect, how a neighbourhood of Herskowitz's yeast paper changed after 2003 relative to the neighbourhood of a similar paper whose famous author happened to stay alive.

The last piece of machinery is the label on each publication. Anyone who had co-authored a paper with the star before the death counted as a collaborator, and a paper with at least one collaborator on its author list went in the collaborator bin. Everything else was a non-collaborator paper. The authors could identify authors only if they were on the roster of American medical school faculty or on an NIH grant, so people at foreign universities and in industry are invisible to the design. Collaborators wrote about eleven percent of the output in the average subfield at baseline, a number that matters later, because an effect on the other eighty-nine percent is where any large change must come from.

Before looking at results, the authors checked the thing a skeptic would check. If neighbourhoods of stars who were about to die were already different from their controls, the whole comparison would be contaminated. Their raw plots show treated and control neighbourhoods rising together, peaking about two years before the death, and declining together. After the death, the lines separate.

Chapter Three: What Moved, and by How Much

Start with the collaborators, because the result there is the least surprising and has been seen before. After a star dies, papers by people who had published with the star fall. In this study the drop is large: the coefficient implies a decline of about twenty-one percent. An earlier paper by Azoulay and Graff Zivin with Jialan Wang had found the same pattern, that coauthors of a dying superstar lose output, and the effect was strongest for the coauthors who worked in the star's own territory. Nobody needed a new paper to be told that when a lab head dies, the lab suffers.

The second number is what the paper is about. Papers by non-collaborators, counted in the same neighbourhoods, rise by about eight and a half percent. The coefficient is 0.082, which converts to eight point six percent, and it is statistically significant at the one percent level. Funding moves along with it: the number of distinct NIH awards acknowledged by papers in the neighbourhood rises by about eleven and a half percent for the non-collaborators and drops by roughly a quarter for the collaborators, twenty-three percent in the paper's figure.

Take the three figures together and the arithmetic is plain. Overall publication activity in the neighbourhood goes up by about five percent, and the estimate is imprecise, so the authors do not lean on it. Of every nine papers at baseline, the star's circle wrote one, and that output falls by a fifth. The other eight came from people outside the circle, and they gain a twelfth. After five years the event-study plot shows the gain among outsiders large enough to cancel the collaborators' loss. The neighbourhood does not shrink and does not grow much. What changes is who is writing in it.

One correction belongs here. Episode fifty-nine said that subfields grew by about seven percent, a figure drawn from a search summary that I flagged as unchecked. The paper says eight point six percent for the non-collaborators, and a smaller, imprecise five percent for everyone. The earlier number was wrong, and the direction was not.

The next result is the one that makes the paper something more than a story about turnover. The authors sorted the non-collaborator papers by how often they were eventually cited, relative to every other paper published in the same year, drawn from a universe of more than seventeen million articles. If the new arrivals were filling the neighbourhood with ordinary work, the extra papers would sit in the middle. They do not sit there. Among papers in the bottom quartile of citations the change is slightly negative, and not statistically different from zero. Through the middle quartiles it stays flat. Among papers between the seventy-fifth and ninety-fifth percentile it is about thirteen percent. The next band, up to the ninety-ninth, shows twenty-six percent, and the top one percent shows thirty-eight.

Hold on a moment to what that pattern means. Take a hundred extra papers in the neighbourhood after the death. The effect is not distributed across them like a tide. It is a surge at the high end, and nothing, or a slight fall, at the bottom. The outsiders who come in are not filling space with whatever they had on the shelf. They are bringing the sort of paper that gets cited.

Now the question every reader should ask of a result like this: did the outsiders come in from the edge of the field, or from outside it? The authors check this three ways. First, they measure how much of each non-collaborating author's earlier output fell inside the star's neighbourhood. Slightly more than half of the related articles in the whole data set have authors with exactly zero overlap, and the growth after a death comes from authors with little or none. These people did not publish in this territory while the star lived. Second, they ask whether the new papers are intellectually close to the star's source paper. They are. The extra papers close to the source grow by about ten percent and the distant ones do not grow in a way the data can distinguish from nothing, so the outsiders are working on the field's central questions, not on its margins. Third, they look at what the new papers cite. Papers that cite nothing from inside the neighbourhood expand by about fourteen percent, while the ones with an internal reference show no growth at all. Citing the star's own bibliography matters too: the papers that skip it rise sixteen percent, and those that include it, eight. The additions draw on newer references and newer combinations of subject headings.

Put the three together and the outsider arrives from a neighbouring literature carrying tools the neighbourhood does not have, and applies them to the neighbourhood's old questions. The authors call it intellectual arbitrage. A man with a better microscope walks into a room where everyone was using the same slides, and he does not wait for the room to ask.

Chapter Four: A Revolution Would Have Looked Different

Anyone who has followed this series since the Kuhn episodes will recognize what is on the table and what is not. Planck's sentence, in Kuhn's hands, was a claim about paradigms: a field's shared assumptions are so thoroughly absorbed by the people who grew up in them that only their replacement does the job. Episode fifty-nine followed that claim through the oxygen case and concluded that the holdouts in the real stories were rarer than the quote suggests.

The paper makes no claim about paradigms and says so in its introduction. Its authors write that paradigm shifts are rare, and that once one emerges it is very costly to resist or ignore it, so the empirical tests of Planck's remark on those cases have mostly come out against it. They are looking at what Kuhn would have called normal science, the ordinary work of a settled field, which they suspect is much more open to what they call political jousting. That is the key move of the paper, and it takes Planck's principle out of the history of great revolutions and puts it in the life of an ordinary laboratory.

The authors also check whether the surge is the kind of event a revolution would produce. They use a measure from two other researchers, Russell Funk and Jason Owen-Smith, which asks whether later papers that cite a given paper also cite its predecessors. A paper that supersedes its ancestors is called disruptive, and one that is cited alongside its ancestors consolidates them. By this measure the new papers are not disruptive. The authors describe the result as a myriad of small permanent revolutions, in which new ideas come to the fore without eclipsing the older approaches.

That sentence changes how the whole finding should be read. Nothing in the data says the field was overturned. What the data say is that the field had a doorman, and the doorman left.

The doorman is a figure of speech. Whether a doorman existed at all is the paper's real question, and the authors are careful about it. When they look for the usual ways a senior scientist could block entry, they find almost nothing. In the five years before death, only three of the 452 stars sat on a National Institutes of Health study section, the panels that decide which grant applications are funded. Another three were journal editors. Those six cannot produce a result that appears across the sample, and the authors confirm that dropping them does not move it. If the stars were not blocking entry by controlling money and journals, something else was.

Their answer is the title of a section: Goliath's shadow. They split the sample at the median on several measures of the star's eminence, and the pattern holds across all of them. For stars with above-median citations the post-death rise among non-collaborators is about thirteen percent, and for those below it the estimate is about four percent and indistinguishable from zero. Split by funding, the figures are about eighteen percent against one and a half. Split by the star's share of the papers in the neighbourhood, thirteen percent against six. The more eminent the person who died, the more room opens. The authors read that as deterrence by presence: a sensible outsider does not apply a better method to a question another person owns, because that person will be the referee, the invited speaker, and the name that program officers recall.

Merton comes back at this point. Episode sixty-two found that when two papers are equal, the famous name collects the credit. Add the deterrent here and the same mechanism has a second, quieter effect on the work that does not get written. The outsider who stays away is making a calculation Merton would have understood, about the expected reward of a contribution to a field in which the largest share of any increment will go to someone else. The star's death rewrites the odds, and the outsiders respond within a few years.

Chapter Five: The Invisible College After the Funeral

If the shadow were the whole story, every field that lost a star would open up in proportion to the star's eminence. They do not. Among the fields of the most eminent half of the stars, the authors find that the response varies a great deal, and they spend the last third of the paper on why.

They test three ideas. The first is intellectual coherence: tight clustering around the same questions and methods may look unwelcoming to an outsider. The second is social coherence, measured by the share of closed triangles in the coauthorship network, which marks a field as a club if its authors tend to publish with one another. Resource control: the collaborators the star leaves behind may sit on the panels and editorial boards the star himself did not.

The patterns are consistent in direction and uneven in strength. In the less coherent fields the post-death rise was about twenty-two percent on one measure against seven in the more coherent, and eighteen against ten on the other. The less clubby fields gained about fourteen percent and the clubbiest about six. On resource control, fields in which the star's collaborators had written fewer editorials, as a rough proxy for holding an editorship, gained about sixteen percent, against nine. With no collaborators on study sections the gain was fourteen percent, and with some the estimate went slightly negative. Where collaborators held a large share of the neighbourhood's NIH money the gain was nine percent, and where they held little it was nineteen.

The authors say plainly that the differences between the halves of each split do not reach conventional statistical significance, and that they are conditional correlations, since nothing about the fields themselves was randomly assigned. What persuades them is that every cut points the same way. A reader should be a little more cautious than the authors are in their prose, and a little less than the p-values would suggest. Six cuts that agree are not six independent confirmations, because intellectual coherence, clubbiness and resource control probably move together across fields. They are, taken together, a story with a consistent shape.

The shape is an invisible college, the term the authors take from the mid-sixties sociology of science. The dead star's collaborators survive him and keep the field the way he kept it, whether from loyalty, from habit, or because they hold most of the funding. They do not need to read anyone's grant application with hostility. The outsider reading the field from outside sees the same cluster of names at the same journals and concludes, correctly, that the entry price is high.

The phrase in the paper that stays is influence from beyond the grave. The stars exert it without having tried to. Think about what that implies for the tools by which a funder thinks about gatekeeping. A conflict-of-interest policy, a rule about recusal from a study section, a limit on terms for journal editors: all of these address control exercised on purpose by someone with a seat. This paper says that in the life sciences the control was mostly exercised by presence, with six of 452 stars holding the seats at all.

Episode sixty-one met something like this from the other side. Polanyi's republic of science is held together by overlapping neighbourhoods in which each scientist watches the pieces the nearest others have just placed, and the system works because everyone is watching someone. The Azoulay paper asks what happens to the watching when one person is watched much more than the rest. Polanyi's picture has no place for an asymmetry of that size, and the data show that it matters.

Chapter Six: The Case for the Star, and the Welfare Bill

Before the case against the paper, the best case for the thing it is measuring, because the authors themselves refuse to treat the finding as an indictment. A fair defender of eminent scientists could say this. Without an organizing figure there may be no shared questions at all. Progress in the sciences is cumulative, and cumulative work needs people to agree for a while on what counts as a result and how to get one. A star who sets those terms is performing a service, and the price of the service is that outsiders stay away for a time. The authors write, in nearly these words, that gatekeeping might help when a field is young, and that the ability to steer a domain is in itself a prize that may encourage risk-taking at the start.

The welfare section of the paper is the most careful part and I would give it a long look. The first thing the authors observe is that the additions by outsiders are offset in the count. The collaborators lose output as the outsiders gain it, so the neighbourhood's publication total barely changes. The second is a check at the level of the person. When the authors follow the outsiders themselves, their total output does not increase after the death. Their grant counts stay flat as well. They write the new papers in place of papers they would otherwise have written somewhere else. The aggregate result is a reallocation of attention from one neighbourhood to another, and the gain, if there is one, is in the quality of the paper and not in the quantity.

The third finding adds a twist. The fields that grew most were those in which the star had been presiding over an empire already losing momentum in the years before his death. A neighbourhood that was running down, and was lightly defended by the star's coauthors, drew the biggest response. One reading is that outsiders are good judges of where an incumbent is weak. Another is that stars are the best people in the world at a stage of a field's life and the worst people at a later one, and that the market for ideas has a rough way of noticing.

Beyond these checks the paper reasons carefully about what it does not know. What the outsiders would have done in their original neighbourhoods is unknowable from the data, and so is whether the star's presence also improved the quality of the work in the field while he lived. It states that the stars' lofty accomplishments suggest that their net contribution is positive, and that today's journeymen become tomorrow's stars in their turn. In the closing section the authors decline to draw policy conclusions at all.

This restraint is to the paper's credit, and it leaves a gap that is easy to fill with a wrong inference. A reader who finishes with the sense that stars are obstacles, and that science would go faster with fewer of them, has gone further than the evidence allows. The evidence is that in one set of fields, about a decade after a premature death, the arrival of outsiders was higher by a measured amount, and that the outsiders' work was cited more. It supports the claim that there is a price to concentration of attention. It says nothing about what a field would have done without any star at all, and cannot, because every field in the data had one.

Chapter Seven: What the Design Cannot See

Reception first. I searched for published critiques and replications of this paper and found none that I could reach. The working paper circulated from 2015, the American Economic Review version appeared in January 2019, and the closest things I found were the follow-up literature on collaborators of dead scientists that the paper itself cites, by Oettl, by Jaravel, Petkova and Bell, and by Mohnen. For a paper in a top journal that is some information, and it is not strong information. A lack of critiques I could find says as much about my search as about the paper. What follows is therefore the narrator's own case against, and I mark it as such.

The sample is the first limit and the authors name it themselves. The study covers academic life scientists in the United States, with deaths between 1975 and 2003 and data through 2006. The authors expect different patterns in physics, where equipment concentrates power in institutions and not in individuals, and in fields where people mostly work alone. Whether the pattern holds in an era of very large laboratories is a question the data predate.

The second limit lies in the word premature. The authors required that the stars be visibly active in the years before they died, and the median age at death was sixty-one. When they cut the sample by age, the effect comes mainly from the stars who died younger, and for the older ones it is small and imprecise. That matters for the title. Planck's funeral is the funeral of an old man who has spent decades defending an old idea. The paper's funerals are mostly those of people in their fifties and early sixties, with grants and students, whose neighbourhoods were still growing, and the paper's own evidence says the old man's death matters less. It is a test of what an untimely loss does to a field, which is a related question, and a different one.

The third limit is the design's reliance on the cause of death. Fifty-one percent of the deaths followed a long illness. A scientist who knows he is dying can pass a field to chosen successors, and the authors call this intellectual estate planning. They separate sudden from anticipated deaths and find the same direction in both, with less precision in each. That is the answer a skeptic should hope for, and it still leaves the anticipated deaths as the weaker half of the identification.

The fourth is the definition of an outsider. The roster covers American medical schools and NIH grantees, so a collaborator working in industry or overseas is classified as a non-collaborator. If those people are a meaningful part of a star's circle, some of the outsider surge is the circle, appearing in the data under the wrong label. The authors acknowledge that they cannot see them. They argue that the mislabelling should affect treated and control neighbourhoods alike, which is a reasonable argument, and one that leaves the size of the effect uncertain.

The fifth is the field boundaries. The related-citations algorithm uses words that authors choose, and authors choose words to describe where they hope to be placed. The authors argue that this should affect treated and control neighbourhoods symmetrically, and for the level of the effect I agree. For the question of who counts as an outsider I am less sure, since an author who wants to be read as part of a neighbourhood, once its star is gone, has an incentive to adopt its vocabulary.

None of these limits breaks the main finding. The pre-trends are flat, the effect rises over time, the result survives the authors' alternative definitions, and the dose-response by eminence is what a causal story predicts. What the limits do is shrink the scope of the claim. The paper shows that in one era of American biomedical research, a field that lost a prominent member early was entered by outsiders whose work was disproportionately cited. It does not show that science advances one funeral at a time, and it does not say Planck was right. It says something a little more useful, which is where to look for the places in a field that are closed without anyone having closed them.

Chapter Eight: A Constructed Field and a Question for the Panel

Here is a constructed case, invented for this episode and not drawn from any real field, to put the findings to work. A subfield studies a particular signalling pathway in heart failure. For twenty years one laboratory has defined the field's questions, trained a large fraction of its postdoctoral fellows, and given the invited lecture at most meetings. Its head is in the middle of a productive decade. Four labs in related areas have thought about moving in. Each has a method the pathway's laboratories do not use. Each has considered the likely first reviewers of their paper, and each has decided that the odds are better elsewhere.

That is the Azoulay picture of a field in its shadow. The result of the study is that the question is not whether anyone is blocking the four labs. Nobody is, and the head of the laboratory would welcome them. The question is what a panel could do about a deterrent that no one is exercising on purpose. The paper gives three levers, each with a different cost, and I would not lean on the first more than the data allow.

The first is to treat the absence of applicants as information. A study section sees only the applications that arrive. If the best method in a neighbouring field is not being applied to the pathway's questions, no application shows that, and the panel has nothing to score. The question a program officer can ask is the one the paper's design makes visible: which neighbourhood has a dominant name and conspicuously few outsiders, and what has that name's presence been doing to the applications? Episode sixty's panel had to decide among the proposals it received. This one is about the ones it did not.

The second lever is the one the narrator would add, and it is the narrator's own. When a panel reads an outsider's application to a field that has a star, the usual question is whether the applicant has a history in that field. A history is what the shadow kept the outsider from building. A better question asks about the applicant's record with the method, and whether the field's own reviewers have a reason to resist the idea apart from the fact that it is new. Reviewing for that distinction costs nothing and shifts the panel's prior in the right direction.

The third lever is slower and sits with the star's institution. The paper's heterogeneity results suggest that fields reopen faster when a star's close collaborators do not hold the editorships and the panel seats. The corresponding duty for the living stars is plain enough: be the person who invites the outsider to the meeting. The paper cannot say whether that works, because the stars in its sample were not experimenting with it, and no one should write a policy from a speculation about what could be done.

The same applies to the collaborators themselves, who lost a fifth of their output in the data, and whom a funder might reasonably want to protect. A death of a star is an event that a program can see coming a year ahead, in the cases where it is foreseen, and a bridge fund for the collaborators who are about to find themselves without their anchor is cheaper than the field they would otherwise leave. That is the narrator's suggestion, and the paper does not test it.

There remains the matter of Patrick Collison's question from the first episode of the Collison shelf: how to ensure an adequate replacement rate in systems that have no natural way to die. Collison's example was institutions, and he pointed at bankruptcy as what clears out failing firms. This paper offers an uncomfortable answer for science. What did the clearing in the life sciences, at least in this period, was death, and the replacement it produced came from outside. A system that could reproduce the opening without the funeral would need some other means of telling outsiders that the cost of entry had dropped. Nobody has designed one, and the study does not say that anyone could.

On whether to read the paper, I would. It runs about thirty pages, the argument is carried by a handful of tables, and its design section, with the Herskowitz yeast paper walked through from source to controls, is a clear example of how to define a field without a committee. Planck's sentence takes a minute to read and is not worth more. The tables on citation quantiles and on eminence reward a slow look. The appendices I did not read, and they carry the robustness work.

For the record, here is how this was grounded. I read the American Economic Review version in full, main text and tables, from the publisher's file, and the numbers in this episode are the paper's, with the percentages converted from its Poisson coefficients, which is how its tables define them. The appendices, which include the death-by-cause splits, the age cutoffs and the author-level output tables, were not available to me, so everything said here about them is from the main text's own description. Planck's wording and dates come from a quotation-history page, not from the book or the article. Hull and colleagues, and the plate tectonics result, reached me through secondary summaries and a forum post that quoted a review. I found no published critique of the funeral paper.

A grant panel has a stack of applications from people in the field it knows. The question to carry into the next review cycle is who in this field would change what gets written if that person stopped answering email, and what the outsiders who are not applying would send if they thought the odds had changed.
