Robert Taylor, The Dream Machine, Part Two: the lab that built tomorrow's computer and watched almost everyone else sell it

Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center built the mouse, the windowed screen, the office network, and the laser printer inside one decade, then shipped a product nobody could afford while a dozen of its own engineers carried the rest of it out the door to other companies.

17 September 2026

Chapter One: A Printer Nobody Asked For

In nineteen seventy-one, an optical engineer named Gary Starkweather walked into a manager's office at Xerox's research campus in Webster, New York, and asked permission to point a laser at a photocopier drum instead of a lens full of ordinary light. He had spent four years trying to convince the copier division that a laser could paint an image onto that drum one line at a time, direct from a computer file, rather than photographing a paper original the way every Xerox machine since the nineteen fifties had done. His own managers told him, more than once, that the idea threatened the product line paying his salary, and that he should stop pursuing it on company time. Starkweather asked for a transfer instead, to a brand-new outpost the company had just opened in California with no copier assembly line anywhere near it, and got one. Nine months after he arrived, he had a working prototype standing in a lab that had never manufactured anything. He called it SLOT, for Scanned Laser Output Terminal, an unglamorous name for a machine that would eventually put several billion dollars a year into a company that had spent four years trying to talk him out of building it.

Starkweather's own laser printer is not the invention this hour is best remembered for, and that gap is the more useful place to start than the famous machines. The lab he transferred into, the Xerox Palo Alto Research Center, would spend the next decade building the first computer designed for one person to own, the first network built to let those computers talk to each other and to a printer in the next room, and the graphical screen full of windows and icons that every computer sold today still borrows its basic vocabulary from. Almost none of it shipped as a Xerox product while the lab that built it was still building. The printer did. The rest of this hour asks why that gap opened, who it swallowed, and what it means to call a decade of research a success or a failure depending on which ledger gets read.

Chapter Two: A Network's Alumni, Rehired Wholesale

The man who opened that lab's computing half in nineteen seventy had spent the previous four years inside the federal office this shelf spent the last hour inside as well. Robert Taylor ran the Advanced Research Projects Agency's Information Processing Techniques Office from nineteen sixty-five to nineteen sixty-nine, the same chair Licklider had first occupied and renamed, funding the same scattered university labs, watching three mismatched terminals on his own Pentagon desk turn into the irritation that became the ARPANET. When Xerox's chief scientist, Jack Goldman, went looking in nineteen sixty-nine for someone to build a computing research group at a new lab he was planning three thousand miles from company headquarters, Taylor was an obvious name to call, and he said yes on one condition. He wanted to build the staff the same way he had built ARPA's funded network, by finding people he already trusted rather than posting a job and reading résumés from strangers.

He got an unusually direct opportunity to do that. A Berkeley spinoff called the Berkeley Computer Company, itself descended from an ARPA-funded timesharing project, folded in nineteen seventy after running out of money, scattering a small group of engineers who had spent years building one of the more ambitious operating systems anywhere in the country and had nowhere left to build it. Taylor hired nearly the whole group at once: Chuck Thacker, Butler Lampson, Peter Deutsch, Jim Mitchell, Charles Simonyi, engineers whose names would soon sit on half the patents this hour describes, walking as a unit from a bankrupt Berkeley startup into a fully funded Xerox lab inside the same year. It was the identical mechanism Licklider had used at ARPA a decade earlier, one trusted person vouching for another across an existing network rather than a stranger's résumé earning a cold evaluation, except this time the network doing the vouching was itself the direct residue of the ARPA money that had funded the Berkeley project in the first place.

That residue had a second, less flattering source, and the timing explains why so much of it arrived at once. In nineteen sixty-nine Congress passed the Mansfield Amendment, requiring every dollar of military research money to show a direct, demonstrable link to a specific military need, closing off the kind of open-ended basic research Licklider's own renamed office had spent the decade funding on the strength of a hunch rather than a mission statement. University computing labs that had lived comfortably on ARPA contracts for years suddenly found their funding tied to justifications few of them could honestly write, and a wave of researchers who had built careers on that earlier freedom went looking for a new sponsor willing to pay for curiosity rather than deliverables. Xerox, flush with photocopier profits and eager to look past its own core business, was a sponsor with money and no such law to answer to, and Taylor, already carrying the address book of everyone that freedom had produced, was standing at the open door when the wave arrived. The tension Licklider's own nineteen sixty-three memo had admitted and moved past, that the whole enterprise existed to serve a military need whether its director found that mission interesting or not, is the same tension that pushed a decade's worth of talent out of federal science and into a corporate lab six years later, once Washington finally enforced it.

Chapter Three: The Manager Nobody Would Call a Manager

Xerox's first director for the new lab's overall research program, a physicist named George Pake, took one look at Taylor's résumé and decided he was not fit to run anything formally. Taylor had no doctorate, no years inside a university department building the kind of academic standing Pake's own hiring instincts were built to recognize, only four years of funding other people's research at ARPA and a track record nobody in Pake's own field had a framework for crediting. Pake installed a different scientist, Jerome Elkind, as the Computer Science Laboratory's titular manager in nineteen seventy-one and left Taylor running its actual daily operations underneath that title, an arrangement that lasted for years before Taylor's own name finally reached the org chart he had already been running from the start.

What Taylor built underneath that borrowed title had almost no formal hierarchy in it at all. He organized weekly gatherings the group nicknamed Dealer meetings, after a book on card-counting one staff member happened to be reading, where researchers sat on beanbag chairs and argued a colleague's half-finished idea apart in public. The purpose, Alan Kay said afterward, was to make progress without anyone in the room having to lean on a formal manager's authority to make people do anything. A junior engineer's objection carried as much force in that room as a senior one's, provided the objection survived being argued with out loud, and Taylor's own rule for himself was to talk last, if at all, so that the room settled a question on its own evidence rather than on whoever technically signed the paychecks. A man barred from an official manager's title had gone on to build the least hierarchical research culture in American computing, almost as if the ban had freed him from any rank left to protect, and the irony returns, twelve years later, in circumstances considerably less kind to him.

Chapter Four: Borrowed Light

Not every hire Taylor made this decade drew from a fresh cohort of talent, and the most complicated one drew from a lab this shelf has already visited. Bill English, who had built the wooden shell of the first computer mouse alongside Douglas Engelbart at the Stanford Research Institute and staged the nineteen sixty-eight demonstration that introduced it to the world, moved to Xerox PARC in nineteen seventy-one and rebuilt the device there with a rolling ball in place of the original's two perpendicular wheels, the design every mouse since has descended from. English's own departure was one signal among several that Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center, the same lab Licklider had funded on the strength of one report a decade earlier, was losing the very people who had made it the liveliest computing address in the country, as the Mansfield-era funding squeeze tightened around a project with no near-term military use to point to.

Engelbart himself watched much of what his own group had spent the nineteen sixties building, the mouse, the pointing and clicking, the screen full of linked documents, get refined, renamed, and eventually credited to a newer lab a few miles down the road, one better funded and considerably better at getting its work in front of a paying customer. He never fully forgave the industry for treating his own augmentation vision, a whole philosophy about extending human intellect that the mouse was only ever one small piece of, as a parts bin PARC's engineers were free to raid for the pieces that were easiest to ship. The complaint complicates the tidy story of one lab's genius succeeding a first lab's failure. What Xerox actually assembled was not a clean invention built from nothing, but a second-generation harvest of a first program's own people and ideas, arriving at PARC's door for reasons that had more to do with a funding law passed in Washington than with any shortfall in Engelbart's own vision. Michael Hiltzik's institutional history of the lab, "Dealers of Lightning," traces that same parts-bin argument through payroll records and internal memos rather than through Engelbart's own grievance alone, and lands closer to Engelbart's reading than to Taylor's: the mouse, the network, and the windowed screen each had a traceable author inside an earlier, less-funded program before a single Xerox researcher touched them.

Chapter Five: A Computer for Children of All Ages

A different pole of the lab's ambition arrived from an entirely different direction than either the Berkeley hires or the Engelbart alumni. Alan Kay, who had spent his own graduate years imagining a machine small enough for a child to carry and rich enough to let that child compose music, draw, and write programs on the same screen, joined the lab in nineteen seventy and wrote up the idea formally two years later under a title that reads, decades on, like a dare: "A Personal Computer for Children of All Ages." He called the imagined machine the Dynabook, a book-sized slate no engineer at Xerox could actually build with nineteen seventy-two's electronics, and instead of shelving the idea until the hardware caught up, Kay organized a research group to build the software such a machine would eventually need, a programming language called Smalltalk designed so that a child, not a professional engineer, could teach the machine new tricks by pointing and typing rather than writing formal code.

Kay's own mantra for the group, delivered after one too many executives asked him to forecast which way computing would trend next, put the lab's whole operating theory into a single sentence: the best way to predict the future is to invent it. It is a tidy line, quoted since in contexts that have nothing to do with computers at all, and its tidiness can obscure how strange the actual bet underneath it was. Kay's group was not forecasting a market. It was building a machine years before any customer had asked for one, funded by a copier company whose entire existing business model depended on selling toner and service contracts to people who already owned a Xerox machine, on the theory that inventing the thing first was a more reliable way to know what people would eventually want than asking them.

Chapter Six: The Machine Two Thousand People Almost Owned

What the lab actually built to house that theory arrived in nineteen seventy-three, smaller than Kay's imagined slate but closer to a working reality than anyone else in computing had come. The Alto, designed principally by Chuck Thacker with Butler Lampson's software architecture underneath it, gave one person exclusive use of a machine roughly the size of a small refrigerator, with a screen that displayed each pixel individually rather than drawing crude letters from a fixed character set, a mouse descended from English's rebuilt version of Engelbart's original, and enough memory to let a document on screen look, for the first time on any machine anywhere, close to how it would appear once printed. Charles Simonyi built an editor called Bravo on top of it that let a writer see bold text as bold and a different typeface as visibly different while typing, rather than reading a string of formatting codes only a printer could interpret, a feature since given the acronym WYSIWYG, what you see is what you get, a phrase invented to describe exactly what every word processor on earth now does without anyone remarking on it.

Wiring enough of those machines together produced its own invention inside the same building. In nineteen seventy-three Bob Metcalfe and David Boggs built a shared cable any machine on it could speak onto, each one listening first and backing off if two signals collided, a scheme Metcalfe named Ethernet and patented four years later alongside Lampson and Thacker as co-inventors. Starkweather's printer, refined under that same roof and renamed the Dover for its internal use, plugged into the identical cable, so that any Alto on the network could send a document to be printed at laser resolution down the hall without a single sheet of paper changing hands along the way. Its commercial descendant, the Xerox 9700, reached the market in nineteen seventy-seven as the first laser printer built to run off a computer file rather than a paper original, and it became, within a few years, one of the most profitable machines Xerox had ever sold, spawning a printing business that still generates billions of dollars a year for the company decades later. Each Alto cost Xerox something on the order of twelve thousand dollars in parts alone to assemble, by Thacker's own later estimate, a figure nobody at the lab ever had to justify to a customer because the lab built roughly two thousand of them over the following years, scattered across the research lab itself, a handful of universities, and eventually a wing of the White House staff, and sold not one as a commercial product. Of everything this chapter has just described, the printer is the one piece that made it out of the research lab and onto a factory floor before the decade was over.

Chapter Seven: An Office Already Living in the Future

By the middle of the decade, PARC's own hallways had become the only place on earth where the future Kay kept promising was simply how work got done. Researchers wrote memos on Bravo, sent them across the building on Ethernet through a mail system called Grapevine, one of the first networked email services anywhere, and printed the results on a laser printer that produced a page indistinguishable from professional typesetting, all before nine in the morning, at a moment when most American offices outside the lab still ran on carbon paper and a typing pool. A researcher who left the building for a conference and came back a week later returned to a stack of mail no one else on the trip had, because almost no one anywhere else on the planet yet had an account to send it from. The screens themselves carried the same lead: because the Alto drew each pixel on its own rather than assembling letters from a fixed set, a researcher could open several overlapping windows at once, drag one on top of another the way a hand moves a sheet of paper across a desk, and switch between a memo, a hand-drawn diagram, and a chess-board layout without ever touching a different machine, an arrangement so far outside anything a nineteen-seventies office had a word for that Kay's own team simply borrowed the word desktop from the furniture it was replacing.

That gap between the lab's own daily experience and the rest of the country's is where the argument of this hour actually starts, rather than where the famous demonstration usually places it. The researchers building this equipment were not guessing at what an office computer might someday be worth to a stranger. They were living inside the answer, years ahead of a market that did not yet exist to buy it, a small daily rehearsal of the exact bet Kay's own mantra about inventing the future was describing, repeated every time someone sent a Grapevine message instead of walking a memo down the hall. What none of that daily fluency solved was the harder problem of turning a research building's own working habits into a product a company with no experience selling computers could price, manufacture, and support for a customer who had never seen a mouse.

Chapter Eight: A Trade Worth a Million Dollars

By December of nineteen seventy-nine, the Alto and the graphical interface built on top of it had been running inside the lab for six years, seen by almost nobody outside it, when Apple arranged the second of two visits by its own engineers under terms that had nothing to do with generosity on either side. Jef Raskin, an Apple engineer already running his own project to build a simpler computer, had brought a first small group through earlier that year hoping to show Steve Jobs that other serious companies took the same kind of graphical, mouse-driven interface seriously; Jobs came away from that first look unconvinced enough that Raskin arranged a second, fuller session and made sure Jobs attended it himself. Xerox's venture-capital arm wanted an early stake in Apple ahead of an initial public offering already drawing enormous attention, and Jobs offered a trade: Xerox could buy a hundred thousand shares of pre-IPO Apple stock at ten dollars each, a bet worth a million dollars if the offering went well, in exchange for letting a handful of Apple's own engineers spend real time inside the research lab watching what its computers actually did.

One of the researchers asked to run the demonstration, Adele Goldberg, argued against showing Jobs's team the software at all, telling Xerox's own executives they were about to give away, in her words, the kitchen sink. The executives who had already agreed to the stock trade overruled her, and Goldberg and a colleague, Larry Tesler, walked Jobs and his engineers through Smalltalk's windows, its pointing device, its icons standing in for files and folders, work Kay's group had been refining for most of the decade already gone. Jobs later described the effect in a single sentence, worn smooth by how often it has been repeated since: it was obvious to him that every computer would eventually work this way. What made the moment land was not that Jobs had seen something nobody had described before him. Engelbart had demonstrated a mouse in public a decade earlier, and Kay had published the Dynabook proposal seven years before Jobs ever walked through the door. What Jobs saw, six years after the Alto first ran, was a company that had already paid to build a working answer and left it sitting in a research building instead of shipping it.

Chapter Nine: The Price Tag and the Diaspora

Xerox did eventually try to ship it. In nineteen eighty-one the company released the Xerox Star, a commercial version of the Alto's screen, mouse, and windowed interface built to run in ordinary offices, complete with small pictures standing in for the objects an office worker already handled by hand: a folder for a group of documents, a printer for the machine down the hall, an actual trash can to drag an unwanted file into and empty. Nothing sold in an American office before it had ever tried to look this much like the desk it was replacing. The Star reached the market priced at sixteen thousand five hundred ninety-five dollars for a single machine before adding the network of servers and printers a working office installation actually required, a bill that could run past a hundred thousand dollars for even a modest department. Corporate customers who had never heard of a mouse and had no category in their own budgets for a machine that expensive largely declined to buy one; across its full four years on the market the Star sold something on the order of twenty-five thousand units total, a rounding error against the personal-computer market about to open underneath it.

What happened next scattered the lab's own people faster than the Star ever scattered its technology. Larry Tesler, who had helped run the demonstration for Jobs, left a couple of years later to join Apple directly, carrying the interface ideas he had helped refine into the machine that would become the Macintosh. Charles Simonyi left for a young software company in Washington state called Microsoft, where the WYSIWYG editing he had built for Bravo became the seed of Word. Bob Metcalfe left to found a company called Three Com, selling Ethernet hardware to every computer maker willing to license the technology he had helped invent under Xerox's own roof, collecting a share of a market Xerox itself never entered. Two researchers who had built Interpress, the lab's own page-description language for driving Starkweather's printers, John Warnock and Chuck Geschke, grew frustrated enough with Xerox's refusal to release that work commercially that they left together the following year and founded Adobe, rebuilding the same idea under the name PostScript, the format that would define desktop publishing for the following two decades. Apple's own Lisa reached the market a year after that, its Macintosh the year after the Lisa, both openly indebted to what their engineers had watched running on an Alto screen, and both, unlike the Star, priced and marketed in a way that found a customer.

Chapter Ten: Three Objections to the Story Just Told

That sequence, one expensive flop followed by a decade of departures each carrying a piece of the lab's own work to a rival, has calcified into a parable about corporate blindness, and each of its central claims holds up less firmly than the parable assumes. Start with the assumption sitting underneath everything this hour has described so far, that a single administrator's foresight explains what the lab built. Taylor himself never coded a line of the Alto's software or drew a schematic of its hardware, and the lab's most consequential technical bets, from Kay's Dynabook program to the Berkeley hires who built the actual machine to English's rebuilt mouse carried over from Engelbart's own group, all arrived through channels Taylor arranged rather than ideas he personally originated. Pake's own refusal to grant him a formal title captured something real about where his actual skill sat: recognizing which people to trust and then staying out of their way while they worked, an editor's gift rather than an inventor's. Crediting him as the visionary behind the Alto risks the same mistake Janet Abbate's history flagged one episode back, crediting a single administrator's foresight for work a much larger, multi-actor effort actually carried out, and Engelbart's own bitterness over a mouse built on his own group's discarded work is the plainest reminder that PARC's achievement rested on at least two earlier programs' worth of borrowed ideas, not a single lab's isolated brilliance.

Taylor's own account of where his judgment came from complicates the shelf's succession story further. Asked years later who deserved the credit for the vision behind everything the lab eventually built, he answered with no hedge in it at all: nobody who held his old job at ARPA after Licklider, in his own assessment, ever matched Licklider's original vision, and most of the field's later advances, his own included, amounted to extrapolations of ideas Licklider had already named first. Licklider chose two successors before he left ARPA, and this hour has just spent eight chapters describing what one of those two successors built with a company's checkbook instead of a federal budget line. Read as a straight continuation, the story looks like proof that Licklider's mechanism kept working for a second generation, a program officer's trust in one protégé outliving the office where he first placed it. Read more skeptically, Taylor's own admission that he was extrapolating rather than inventing raises a harder question about how much of what this shelf calls succession is one administrator's judgment reproducing itself, and how much is one good idea simply getting funded a second time by someone with the standing to fund it.

A third objection targets the parable's own ending rather than its beginning. Malcolm Gladwell's widely read reassessment of the whole story argues that judging the lab purely against the personal-computer business it failed to enter misreads the actual ledger Xerox was keeping. Starkweather's laser printer alone produced a printing business worth billions of dollars a year, a return that by itself paid the lab's entire research budget back many times over before anyone had finished arguing about who should be credited for the mouse. Xerox's stake in Apple, even sold earlier than it should have been, still turned a profit on the original million-dollar bet. Measured as one research portfolio placed across many uncertain bets rather than as a single missed chance to become the world's first personal-computer company, the lab looks less like a company that let a fortune walk out the door and more like an investor whose biggest winner nobody remembers to mention, because a printer humming down the hall never made as good a story as a mouse pointing at an icon.

Chapter Eleven: The Ledger That Actually Mattered

None of those three objections erases what the parable gets right. Xerox built the graphical personal computer years before anyone else, watched a room full of its own researchers show visiting engineers exactly how it should be sold, and let Apple, Adobe, Microsoft, and Three Com each carry out a separate piece of the same idea while its own product, the Star, sat mostly unsold at a price built for nobody's actual budget. What the objections narrow is the reason that gap opened, from a story about corporate stupidity to a harder question about which ledger an organization reads when it decides whether an investment in strange, unproven work paid off.

Compare Taylor's version of that question to the two this shelf has already tested. Vannevar Bush embedded his trust in a university department's existing peer structure, durable past his own tenure because it never depended on one man's continued presence. Braben trusted one executive's shelter at British Petroleum, and the whole program evaporated the week that executive walked out the door, because nothing besides his personal protection had ever been built to survive without him. Taylor's own lab collapsed the way Braben's did, not the way Licklider's office had. In nineteen eighty-three, a new director named William Spencer challenged Taylor's authority in a meeting that turned on the same objection Pake had raised twelve years before, that Taylor held no doctorate and no formal claim to run anything, and Taylor answered with two words, I quit, and walked out. Nearly his entire research staff left with him inside the following weeks, most of them landing together at a new lab Digital Equipment Corporation built for them from scratch. Licklider had handed his own office to two named successors years before he needed to, and the mechanism outlived him because someone specific had been chosen to keep it running. Nobody at Xerox ever named a successor to keep Taylor's version of that mechanism alive inside the company that owned it, and when he left, the apparatus left with him, evidence that naming an heir in advance, not simply building a productive research culture, was the ingredient that let Licklider's arrangement survive its founder's exit while Taylor's did not.

What that leaves behind is a dilemma closer to a research director's own problem at closing time than to a funder's problem writing the first check. Braben's book raised the question of how much unstructured time to hand one promising researcher. Licklider's office raised the harder question of who gets trusted to keep making that call once its architect is gone. Xerox's own decade raises a third, plainer question neither man's story quite reaches: once the work is finished and sitting on a shelf, which measure of success gets applied to judge it, the flashy demonstration a visiting engineer remembers for the rest of his career, or the unglamorous machine down the hall paying for the whole operation without anyone bothering to write its story down. A monitoring system built to track a scattered program's real output runs into a small version of the identical trap. The number that draws every reader's eye is rarely the one that was actually paying for the whole operation, and a reviewer who only reads the dramatic line can miss the modest figure carrying the budget the entire time, a form filed correctly in a district nobody visits while the pilot everyone photographs never reaches the scale that would make it real.

Xerox's own leadership had both entries in front of it for years and mostly remembered the wrong one. Next time, this shelf leaves the computer behind entirely for a different argument about why authority itself stopped working the way it used to: Martin Gurri's "The Revolt of the Public," on what happens to an institution once the public it answers to can see everything it does.
