The Design of Experiments (Fisher): tea, maize, and the argument he lost over cigarettes

Fisher's 1935 book turned a lady's claim about tea into the logic of the modern trial: fix the questions in advance and let dice, not judgment, assign the treatments. Here is why that works, Darwin's maize as the worked case, and where Fisher's own logic failed him on smoking.

20 October 2026

Chapter One: Eight Cups

On a summer afternoon in Cambridge in the late 1920s, a group of university dons, their wives and some guests sat around a table having tea. One of the women said that tea tastes different depending on whether the tea is poured into the milk or the milk into the tea. The scientific men present scoffed. They could not think of any chemical difference between the two mixtures that could survive the pouring. A small, thin man with thick glasses and a graying beard said, with some excitement, let us test the proposition, and within a few minutes the dons were pouring cups out of her sight and bringing them to her one at a time.

David Salsburg tells the story in his history of statistics, The Lady Tasting Tea, and he says he had it in the late 1960s from a man who was at the table, a statistician named Hugh Fairfield Smith. The man with the beard was Ronald Fisher. According to Smith, the lady got every cup right. Fisher's own account, in the second chapter of The Design of Experiments, says nothing about her results and nothing about Cambridge. Other tellings, which I know only second-hand and have not checked, give the woman a name and move the table to Rothamsted, the agricultural station where Fisher worked. The book names no one. It uses the lady as an example, and the example is built so carefully that it has outlived the dispute over where the table stood.

Here is the version in the book. Eight cups of tea are mixed, four with the milk added first and four with the tea added first. The lady is told this in advance: eight cups, four of each kind, presented in random order. Her task is to sort them into two sets of four. Fisher then does what he does throughout the book, which is to count before anything happens. How many ways are there of choosing four cups from eight? Choose them in succession and you have eight choices, then seven, then six, then five, which comes to sixteen hundred and eighty. But that counts every set in every order, and four cups can be ordered in twenty-four ways, so divide, and the answer is seventy. A lady with no ability to tell the cups apart is guessing which four are which, and one guess in seventy lands on the right set.

Now suppose she gets three of one kind right and the fourth wrong. Because she knows there are four of each, a mistake in one set forces a matching mistake in the other. Sixteen of the seventy possible answers have that shape. Fisher adds a refinement that gets used for the rest of the century. You do not ask how rare the observed result is. You ask how often chance would produce a result at least as good. Sixteen answers score three right, one answer scores four, so seventeen of the seventy are as good or better than hers, nearly one in four. That cannot count as evidence of a real ability. Reverse the counts, so that four right turned up sixteen times and three right only once. Nobody would call the rarer outcome significant merely for being rare.

Before a single cup is poured, then, the whole table of outcomes exists. Every possible result has been listed, and each has been assigned a meaning. Fisher is explicit that this is the discipline: forecast all the possible results, decide without ambiguity what each will be taken to mean, and know the argument that sustains the reading. A trialist will recognise the job of a statistical analysis plan, argued for well before the name existed.

The numbers shift with the size of the experiment. With six cups, three of each kind, there are only twenty arrangements, so perfect success comes by luck five times in a hundred, and Fisher says a man who refuses to be moved by a chance as high as one in twenty gains nothing from three cups of each. With twelve cups, six of each kind, there are nine hundred and twenty-four arrangements. A lady who sorts ten of the twelve correctly, with one pair swapped, produces a result that chance gives thirty-seven times in that total, under a twentieth. She may make a mistake and still be believed. Enlarge the experiment and a lady with merely good taste can pass. The claim "I can tell" has become a quantity, the share of cups she gets right, and the size of the experiment decides how small a share above one half can be detected.

Fisher uses the example to introduce the convention that would later harden into a ritual. It is usual and convenient, he says, to take five percent as the standard level and ignore results that fall short of it. In the same paragraph he undercuts it. No isolated experiment, however significant, can establish a natural phenomenon, because the one chance in a million will turn up with exactly its appropriate frequency. What does establish a phenomenon is, in his words, not an isolated record, but a reliable method of procedure, which means you know how to run an experiment that rarely fails to reach significance. I will come back to that sentence. Fisher forgot it twenty years later, and it would have saved him.

There is a hole in the arithmetic. The one-in-seventy figure assumes that a lady with no discrimination would choose each of the seventy sets equally often. Why should she?

Chapter Two: The Sugar

Fisher begins his answer with a way the assumption could fail. Suppose the experimenter, without thinking, puts sugar in every cup made with milk first and none in the others. A lady who cannot feel the order of pouring will taste the sugar anyway. She sorts the cups into sweet and unsweet, and she is all right or all wrong. The chance of naming every cup correctly is then not one in seventy but thirty-five in seventy, because a single difference in flavour decides everything. The test is wholly vitiated, he says, and errors equivalent in principle to this one are frequently built into otherwise well-designed experiments.

The obvious remedy is to make the cups alike. Fisher says that cannot be done, and the reason is not carelessness. The china will differ in thickness, the milk will not be measured to the drop, the infusion will weaken or strengthen between the first cup and the last, and the tea will cool as the tasting goes on. Any one of these can be named and, with labour and expense, largely removed. But the uncontrolled causes, in his phrase, are strictly innumerable. An experiment is carried out with limited resources, and part of the subject is deciding which disturbances to take care over and which to ignore on purpose.

His solution is a single physical act. Imagine every disturbing cause already fixed for each cup: the strength of the infusion, the quantity of milk, the temperature at tasting, whatever you like. If she cannot discriminate, these are the only things that move her answers, so the chance that she names any particular four cups as milk-first has been settled before you do anything. It might be a lopsided chance. One set of four might be very likely to be called milk-first because it happens to be the warm cups. Now assign the treatments, four cups to milk-first and four to tea-first, by a random process. Whatever set she was going to name, the chance that it coincides with the set you drew is seventy to one against, exactly. The disturbances are no smaller than before. They simply cannot line up with the treatment, except by the very coincidence the one-in-seventy figure already prices in. This is the accident of Vienna's two clinics and London's two water companies made on purpose, with the calendar and the pipe-laying contract replaced by a shuffled pack.

Randomisation does not make the groups equal, and it does not try to. The cups still differ in a thousand ways, and in any one experiment the milk-first cups may by bad luck turn out warmer. What randomisation guarantees is that the probability statement is true. The one chance in seventy is a fact about the procedure, and it holds whatever the cups are like. Fisher keeps validity apart from sensitiveness. Care in preparing the cups, with the same tea and the same milk and no sugar, makes it likelier that a real ability will show. If the experimenter mixes Indian and China tea, a subtle effect of pouring order may be buried under the larger differences between leaves. That costs sensitiveness, and the test remains valid. Randomisation protects validity. Refinement serves sensitiveness. Fisher adds, with the dryness of a man who had watched colleagues spend money, that sensitiveness can usually be bought more cheaply by enlarging the experiment, so the experimenter should take whichever route is easiest and ignore the ingenious precautions that predecessors called essential.

He is also exact about what the dice are for. The random choice guarantees the test. It is not a courtesy to the lady or a way of being fair to a suspicious reader. The phrase random order, he says, is shorthand for a physical process, cards or dice or a published table of random numbers, and not an order that merely occurs to the experimenter. A list a person writes down in a haphazard-seeming order is not random in this sense, because the person's habits are one more uncontrolled cause, and that cause may line up with something.

It is easy to hear all this as a point about cups of tea. The question that follows is whether the trick works when the cups are fields, and the man who asked it for his daily work was employed on a farm.

Chapter Three: The Muck Heap

In the spring of 1919, Ronald Fisher was twenty-nine and, by Salsburg's account, could be counted a failure by many measures. He had been sickly and nearly blind as a child. Sir John Russell, who ran the agricultural station at Rothamsted north of London, offered him a year's work at a thousand pounds, with no promise that it would last beyond the year. The station had been paid for by profits from artificial fertiliser and had spent ninety years running what it called experiments. The records filled leather-bound notebooks: daily rainfall and temperature, weekly dressings, annual harvests. Most of the experiments had never produced consistent results. Russell wanted someone to look at the pile, and Fisher later called the job raking over the muck heap.

The pile taught him two things. The first is a story Salsburg tells with relish, and it shows what past records can and cannot say. Fisher took the yields of a wheat field called Broadbalk and allowed for rainfall and for the fertilisers. Something remained, a slow drift in the yield with a break in 1876, worsening after 1880, improving from 1894 to 1901 and falling again after that. The weeds in the same field showed the same drift reversed. Before 1876, small boys had been hired to pull weeds. The Education Act of that year made schooling compulsory, a second act in 1880 added penalties for keeping children out, and the boys left the fields. The weeds flourished. In 1894, as Salsburg tells it, a schoolmaster at a girls' school nearby, who believed in vigorous outdoor exercise, began bringing his pupils out to the wheat on Saturdays and evenings to pull weeds, and the weeds receded. When he died in 1901 the girls returned to the classroom and the weeds came back. It is detection of a cause nobody had recorded because nobody had thought it was a cause. If an old record of fertiliser yields held an unrecorded child-labour law, what else did it hold?

The second thing was the design. The station's experiments had typically put one fertiliser on one field and another on another, or compared one year with another. That confounds the treatment with the soil, if the fields differ, or with the weather, if the years differ. The natural remedy was to lay the treatments side by side in small plots, on the same ground in the same year. But in which order? Salsburg imagines the meeting at Rothamsted. Someone proposes that the extra phosphorus always go on the northern row. Someone objects that fertility may run north to south, so alternate the rows. Someone points out that a gradient running from the northwest would help the treated row in every block, and another that a gradient from the northeast would favour the control. Which is it? Nobody knows how the fertility runs, and it probably runs in no simple direction at all. Fisher, in Salsburg's telling, takes the pipe out of his mouth and says: randomise.

The book's version of the farm experiment is in the fourth chapter, and it is concrete enough to follow without a picture. Five varieties of a crop are to be compared. The field is divided into eight compact blocks, and each block is cut into five plots lying side by side, forty plots in all. Within each block, one plot goes to each variety, and the pairing of plot to variety is decided at random. He says how. Shuffle a pack of cards numbered one to a hundred, divide each number by five, and let the remainder name the variety, skipping any variety already placed in that block. Because five divides a hundred, each variety holds twenty cards. Failing cards, he points to the first published table of random sampling numbers, compiled by Tippett, and says that with such a table a chart of the whole field can be drawn as fast as if the varieties had been set down in tidy order.

The tidy order is what he argues against. Suppose a clever agronomist knows his field and lays out the plots in a systematic pattern that evens out the fertility better than a random layout would. Fisher asks you to picture that plan carried out on a field where every plot gets the same treatment, so the yields are unaffected by who is assigned where. The total variation is fixed, and so is the variation between blocks. If the agronomist's cleverness has made the varieties more alike in fertility, the variation attributed to varieties has dropped, and since the total is fixed, the variation left over for the estimate of error has risen by the same amount. The comparisons really are more precise, and the estimate says they are less. If the cleverness backfires and the pattern lines up with the fertility, the real error rises while the estimate falls, and the experiment reports a confidence it has not earned. The estimate of error is the casualty in both directions, and it is all the test of significance has to stand on.

That is why replication does two jobs in his scheme, and the second is the one nobody had priced. Repeating the treatments on many plots lowers the error, as every farmer knew. It also supplies the measure of that error from the same plots. An experiment with one plot per treatment could not say whether the best treatment was best or merely sat on better soil. The experimenters could only argue that so big a difference was beyond anything they had seen neighbouring plots do, which borrows an estimate from past experience. Fisher notes that you would need about twenty years of records from those same plots under uniform treatment to judge, even roughly, what difference arises by chance once in twenty trials, and that assumes the weather and the soil never changed. The experiment has to carry its own yardstick. Plots treated alike make the yardstick, and randomisation guarantees that it measures the right thing.

So far we have a tea party and a field with cards. Neither shows what the method does to a result that someone has already published and thought they understood. For that Fisher goes to Darwin.

Chapter Four: Darwin's Maize

In the third chapter, Fisher takes up an experiment Charles Darwin ran for eleven years and reported in 1876, on cross-fertilisation and self-fertilisation in plants. Darwin raised a crossed plant and a self-fertilised plant together in the same pot, at the same age and under the same conditions, and gave the measurement of every individual plant. He wanted readers to see, in the passage Fisher reprints, that the superiority of the crossed plants did not rest on two or three extra fine specimens. Then he did something unusual for his time. He asked Francis Galton to look at the tables.

Galton took the smallest set, the maize, fifteen pairs. His method was to line up the crossed plants from tallest to shortest, do the same for the self-fertilised plants, and compare the tallest of one rank with the tallest of the other, the second with the second, and so on. Ranked that way, the crossed side led almost everywhere, and Galton declared that a crossed series would always exceed a self-fertilised one. The regularity looked impressive. Fisher's complaint is that Galton had manufactured it. The tallest plant in either group is tallest through a run of favourable accidents, among them the accidents that made some pots better than others. Matching the tallest with the tallest throws away the very variation the experiment must be judged against. A statistician who rearranges data into an artificial regularity, Fisher says, is not elucidating the facts but falsifying them.

He then does the sum properly. Darwin's design compared plants within pots, so the unit is the difference between the two plants in a pot. Fisher gives the fifteen differences, in eighths of an inch. Thirteen favour the crossed plant and two do not, one by sixty-seven eighths and one by forty-eight. The total is three hundred and fourteen eighths. The mean is a shade under twenty-one eighths, a little over two and a half inches in favour of the crossed plants. The scatter of the differences about that mean gives a standard error of 9.746, and the ratio of the mean to its standard error, the statistic that Student, the pen name of a research chemist, had worked out in 1908, comes to 2.148.

Fifteen pairs leave fourteen independent comparisons, and the table says that with that many a value of 2.145 is exceeded by chance in exactly five trials in a hundred. Darwin's experiment, analysed properly, scores 2.148. It clears the line by three thousandths. Fisher says it falls on the border between results that can stand alone and results whose value lies in agreeing with other experiments.

Now run Galton's rearranged columns through the same arithmetic. They give 5.171, a value chance would produce about once or twice in ten thousand trials. Galton had taken an experiment sitting on the five percent line and made it look decisive. The honest figure, Fisher says, leaves so much room that a repeat of the experiment would often give a mean half as big again, or half as small. He is as unsparing about the attempt to correct the averages with estimated heights for plants that died before measurement. That, he says, goes beyond the limits of justifiable inference, and shows the logic of statistical induction was in its infancy even when the technique of experiment was already advanced.

There is one more step. The ratio just computed is Student's t, and the t test is exact for one hypothesis: that the two sets of measurements are samples from the same normal population. Fisher asks what you can say without assuming a normal curve, and answers by returning to the lady. If the treatment made no difference, it was a matter of chance in each pot which plant took which position, so each of the fifteen differences was as likely to be positive as negative. Fifteen differences with two possible signs each give two to the fifteenth power, roughly thirty-three thousand assignments of sign. Count how many give a total as large as the observed three hundred and fourteen, and his table gives eight hundred and sixty-three, with the same number going as far in the opposite direction. Together they are 5.267 percent of the possibilities. The t test said five percent, and counting said 5.267, with no curve anywhere in the counting. The probability distribution was supplied by the act of randomisation, as it was in the tea.

Here Fisher says something in a dry paragraph and moves on. Darwin did not randomise. The one flaw in his procedure, Fisher says, was the lack of it. No coin decided which side of the pot each plant took. The counting exercise therefore describes what the data would have said had Darwin tossed a coin for each pot, and by Fisher's own standard of validity, the 2.148 is not licensed, because the act that would have made the probability true was never performed. It is what you get if you pretend. Fisher says so and still publishes the number as the best reading of the evidence, which I think is the right instinct, though the book does not ask where the pretending should stop. If it is acceptable to pretend that Darwin tossed a coin, what were the dice for? I give my answer in Chapter Six.

First there is the other half of the book, which Fisher thought mattered more to working scientists.

Chapter Five: Four Questions at Once

The common wisdom in 1935, and still in many laboratories, is to change one thing at a time. Hold everything else steady, vary one factor, and watch. Fisher calls this an ideal doctrine, closer to elementary physics teaching than to any laboratory practice. His objection is practical. In genuine research nobody knows which of countless factors will turn out to matter, or whether any factor acts independently of the rest. Factors get chosen because they can be controlled and measured, and nature has no obligation to be simple in those terms. An investigator who confines attention to one factor is either the victim of a doctrinaire theory of experiment or short of time and material. If factors are tested singly, it should be because testing them together would be too troublesome or too costly, and he thinks the belief that it would be is usually baseless.

The example in the book is a mixture of four ingredients. Each is tried at a larger and a smaller amount, which gives sixteen mixtures. Make six tests of each, ninety-six in all, assigned at random. Now look at one ingredient. Half the tests, forty-eight, used the larger amount of it and half the smaller. The forty-eight are not identical, because they come in eight sets that differ in the other three ingredients. But each set of six that got the larger dose has a twin set that got the smaller and matches it exactly on those three. So there are eight matched pairs of sets, and the difference between the two groups of forty-eight can be put down, apart from chance, to the ingredient. The precision equals what you would get if all ninety-six tests had gone to that one ingredient. The same holds for each of the four. Every trial answers every question at once, which is why the design needs a quarter of the observations that four separate experiments would take for the same precision.

The second gain is interactions. Suppose a larger amount of A helps if B is present and does nothing, or harms, without it. A series of one-at-a-time experiments, even ninety-six per ingredient, would never show it, because A was always tested with B held in one state. The factorial design tests A with B present and with B absent as a matter of course, and the contrast between the two is the interaction. Four ingredients allow eleven interactions beyond the four single effects, and the sixteen mixtures deliver all fifteen.

The third gain bears on trials. A conclusion drawn from an experiment in which the other conditions were deliberately varied rests on a wider base than one drawn from an experiment in which they were held rigidly constant. The standardised experiment tells you about the narrow range of conditions it standardised, and nothing about the conditions you meet when you use the result. Standardisation, Fisher says, weakens rather than strengthens our ground for inferring a like result when conditions are somewhat varied, as they always are in practice. Its use is confined to the units being compared directly with each other. Everywhere else, varying conditions on purpose widens the inductive basis, and in a factorial arrangement the width comes free.

A trial run in one tightly specified setting, with a single type of site, of staff and of participant, has been standardised on every axis at once, and the claim that it will generalise is one its design never earned. The usual defence is precision. Fisher's reply is that precision comes from comparing matched units within the experiment, which does not require every unit to resemble every other.

The chain so far runs from a cup of tea through a field and a pot of maize to a mixture of ingredients, and each step has the same principle: build the comparison so that what you measure as scatter is what the experiment suffered. It is a design for settings where you control the assignment. What happens where you cannot?

Chapter Six: The Case He Lost

Fisher answered that question in public, by opposing the finding that smoking causes lung cancer. In the late 1950s the Medical Research Council's statistical unit in Britain, and the British Medical Journal, were telling people that cigarette smoking caused lung cancer. The evidence was observational. There were retrospective studies, which asked patients with lung cancer and patients without it about their smoking, and prospective ones, which followed smokers and non-smokers forward. Fisher, who held the chair of genetics at Cambridge, wrote to the British Medical Journal in 1957 and to Nature in the summer of 1958. The Nature letter, headed with a question mark, opens by saying that the association between cigarette smoking and cancer of the lung, to which the Council's statistical unit had directed attention, had been interpreted by that unit almost as though it demonstrated a causal connexion. The paywall stops there, and the rest of what I say about the letters comes from Salsburg's account and not from the letters.

Start with the steelman, because it is a good one and Fisher's critics have often declined to make it. Everything in The Design of Experiments says the same thing about observational comparisons. A probability statement about a difference holds only if treatments were allocated by a process that cannot correlate with anything else. In an observational study the smokers chose their smoking, and whatever led them to choose it may also lead to the disease. Fisher made this concrete. Suppose a constitutional factor, some inherited disposition, makes a person both likelier to smoke and likelier to develop lung cancer. Then smoking and cancer would travel together with neither causing the other, and no number of studies of the same kind could reveal it, because each would reproduce the same confounding. His letter cites a 1939 twin paper by von Verschuer, and Salsburg says Fisher assembled twin data showing that identical twins tended to match in whether they smoked, and challenged critics to show that lung cancer was not similarly inherited.

He had a second point, awkward for the investigators. In the first study by Doll and Hill, as Salsburg describes it, the inhalers had less lung cancer than the non-inhalers. The authors took this to mean that respondents had misunderstood the question. Fisher asked why the finding was not announced as it stood, that smoking was bad for you but that if you had to smoke, inhaling was better. The question was then dropped from the later study of doctors. He also asked for years for the raw data behind the published summaries, and complained that the authors would not release it.

Then there was his anger at the campaign. A British Medical Journal annotation had urged that every device of modern publicity be used, and Fisher objected that it was not quite the work of a good citizen to plant fear in very many millions of smokers without knowing for certain that they had anything to fear. Salsburg reads this as a principled objection to propaganda, put so badly that it was heard as a crotchety man defending his pipe. Fisher was often photographed with one, and accounts I have not held say he took money from the tobacco industry as a consultant. The argument below does not need either fact. If his logic fails, it fails with or without a motive.

Now the case against him, which is largely his own book's case. Jerome Cornfield, whose only formal degree was a bachelor's in history and who had taught himself statistics, joined five cancer specialists in 1959 to write a long review of the evidence. It answered Fisher, Joseph Berkson of the Mayo Clinic and Jerzy Neyman, one objection at a time. Neyman had suggested that the retrospective studies could be biased if smokers lived longer and lung cancer is a disease of old age. Cornfield and his coauthors showed from the patients' own data that the patients did not look like that. They assembled about thirty studies from different countries and populations, retrospective and prospective, and the argument turned on their agreement. Each study could be faulted, and each fault could be matched by a study that did not share it. The study of doctors found a dose-response, with more smoking bringing more cancer. According to Salsburg, Berkson and Neyman were satisfied as the evidence grew, and after 1960 no article in a reputable journal questioned the finding. Fisher died within a few years, still unpersuaded.

Salsburg compresses the clash into two sentences. You cannot prove anything without a randomised design, said Fisher. Some things do not lend themselves to such designs, but the accumulation of evidence should prove the case, said Cornfield. On Fisher's own terms I think Cornfield wins, because of the sentence from Chapter One. Any one experiment can be a fluke, so a phenomenon is demonstrable when you hold a method that rarely fails. Thirty studies with different controls and different recall problems, all pointing one way, are an attempt at such a method where dice are not available. Fisher had written the justification for pooling imperfect comparisons, and then declined to apply it. His twin argument fails by the same standard. An inherited disposition that explains both smoking and cancer is a hypothesis, and he supplied evidence for only half of it, the smoking half, then left the cancer half to his opponents. That moves the burden to the other side, and his own book puts the burden on the claim that something is going on behind the data.

My answer to the question I left in Chapter Four is this. When Fisher analysed Darwin's maize as if the plants had been randomised, he was supposing that the position of a plant in a pot was unrelated to its inheritance, which is reasonable for a greenhouse. For smokers the same supposition was refused. In both cases the probability statement has force to the degree that the supposition is plausible, and Fisher moved the line between plausible and implausible to wherever his conclusion needed it. The dice make the supposition true by construction. That is the best way to get it and not the only way. Where dice are missing you need consistency, dose, and the elimination of rival explanations one at a time. Bradford Hill's list of such considerations is two episodes ahead. It is a nice irony, in a footnote of Salsburg's, that Hill and Doll, the targets of Fisher's fiercest attack, did more than almost anyone to bring his experimental design into medicine.

Cynical frameworks get a dose, and so do heroes. Fisher's method, taken as medicine, is the discipline of designing the comparison so that the error is measured honestly. Taken as identity, as the belief that only the man holding the dice can say what is true, it produced a great statistician defending the cigarette against some thirty studies. One mind made both the achievement and the mistake, and I do not see how to keep the first and bar the second except by asking, at each step, what the method assumes and whether the assumption is present.

Chapter Seven: What the Book Leaves Behind

The book is a manual for agricultural scientists, and it shows. Patients occur once, as a passing item in a list of units an experimenter might subdivide. There is no placebo and no blinding, since a field of wheat does not know which variety it was given. Consent, stopping rules and dropout go unmentioned. When medicine took over the machinery it took over those silences, and the next several episodes are about how the missing pieces were bolted on: the sealed envelope, the large simple trial, the subgroup that torments every analysis.

There is also the five percent. Fisher called it usual and convenient. Within a few pages he warned that no isolated significant result could demonstrate anything. In a section added to the 1960 edition he warned against treating tests of significance as rules of action, procedures in which an irreversible decision must be taken automatically and without thought. For that setting, he said, the cost of wrong decisions has to be assessed in advance and prior knowledge of the material supplied, and in pure research any such assessment is a pretence. A good deal of confusion, he wrote, came from formalising tests of significance in a logical framework different from the one they were developed for. He does not name the target. Anyone who has read ahead in the history will know it has a second author, and that the two men did not like each other.

For a reader deciding what to do with the book, I would take the first four chapters: the introduction, the tea, Darwin and the randomised blocks. Together they run to about fifty pages and need no mathematics beyond what has been said here. Add the factorial section, eleven pages. The chapters from the Latin square onward, on confounding and covariance, are working manuals for the days when you are designing the experiment in question. The book is slow because Fisher does not repeat himself, and a skipped sentence is often a missed step. An evening for each of the first four chapters would do.

For anyone who writes protocols, the book yields a test that can be run on an analysis plan before anything is unblinded. Look for the sentence naming the unit that was randomised. Then look for the one naming what the standard error is computed from. Fisher requires that those two sentences name the same thing, since the estimate of error must come from plots treated alike and is then applied to the difference between plots treated differently. Suppose a school-feeding trial randomises twenty schools, ten to each arm, and measures forty children in each school. The numbers are made up to show the arithmetic. If the analysis treats each of the eight hundred children as a unit, the standard error is built as though eight hundred things had been assigned by chance, when twenty were. The children in one school share a cook, a headteacher, a road and a rainy season. That is the soil and sun of one plot, and the children move together. The design has twenty plots, and an analysis that counts eight hundred is Galton rearranging Darwin's maize: a number that looks regular because the variation it should be judged against has been left out. The repair is to count plots wherever the plan counts people, and to say so in the sentence that names the unit.

Next time, Fisher against Neyman: significance testing against hypothesis testing, the hybrid that neither man wrote, and what a statistical analysis plan commits to when it writes 0.05. Thanks for listening.
