The measurement constellation 2 of 2: targets and terror, the spike at eight minutes, and how to keep a number honest

What happened when England ran its health service on targets with sanctions – Bevan and Hood's 2006 evidence: the ambulance response times that bunched just under eight minutes, the four-hour wait that fell from twenty-three percent to five, the nine hospitals that edited their waiting lists, the three kinds of gaming and the four kinds of people – then the design question the three papers never answered: how a programme that has to report numbers keeps them true, with paired indicators, random audit, denominators that count the people who never started, and a rule about what a number is allowed to cost the person who makes it.

Chapter One: The Spike

Take a distribution. An English ambulance service in the early two thousands had a target: seventy-five percent of the most urgent calls, the ones classified as immediately life-threatening, were to be reached within eight minutes. The service's performance against the target was published, it fed into a star rating for the whole trust, and a trust that missed its targets could lose its chief executive. So imagine the histogram of response times, the count of calls reached in each minute from one to twenty. You would expect a hump somewhere around five to nine minutes, tailing away on either side, and in the services not managed hard against the target, that is what the data showed, a noisy decline. In several that had been, the researchers who looked found something else: a spike at eight minutes, and a hole at nine, the calls that had taken between eight and nine minutes having been recorded as taking less than eight. Nobody had made an ambulance faster. Someone, at some point in the chain between the stopwatch and the spreadsheet, had rounded, and the target had been met, and the number reported to the public meant something different from what the public thought it meant.

That is from a paper by Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood, published in Public Administration in two thousand and six, called What's Measured Is What Matters, and it is the evidence the last episode said the constellation lacked. Ridgway had an employment office and a Soviet plan; Campbell had a police department and a war. Bevan and Hood had a whole national health system that had, for five years, been run on the very principle the three papers warned against, with the data published, and they went through it. The question this episode asks is the one the three discoverers never got to. Once you know the law, and you still have to report numbers, what do you do?

Chapter Two: The Soviet Method

The system they studied had a name, given by the people who ran it, targets and terror, and Bevan and Hood take it seriously, because it names the mechanism. From two thousand and one the government in England published an annual star rating for every hospital and ambulance trust, from zero to three, computed from a set of targets: the share of emergency patients seen within four hours, the maximum wait for an operation, the ambulance response, and a dozen others. A trust with three stars was left alone and given money. A trust with zero stars was named, its board was exposed, and its chief executive was, in a phrase the government used without embarrassment, likely to be replaced. It was, the authors point out, the Soviet method, and they mean the comparison exactly as stated: a central plan with numerical targets for every unit, published league tables, and personal consequences for the manager who missed. The Soviet system ran for sixty years on that basis before it collapsed, and its sixty years produced the best-documented body of evidence about gaming that exists, and Bevan and Hood's move was to take the Soviet taxonomy and see whether the English health service had reproduced it.

It had. The Soviet literature described three kinds of gaming, and the English data showed all three. The first is the ratchet: if next year's target is set from this year's performance, a manager who expects to be in the job next year has a reason not to do too well now, and the Soviet saying, which the authors quote from a nineteen fifty-eight study, was that a wise director fulfils the plan a hundred and five percent but never a hundred and twenty-five. The second is the threshold: a target set at a fixed level, seventy-five percent within eight minutes, ninety percent within four hours, pulls all the effort to the patients just on the wrong side of the line and none to the ones far beyond it or safely inside it, so that performance crowds up against the target from below and a distribution that should be smooth acquires a cliff. The third is output distortion, the general name for meeting the number at the expense of the things the number does not count, and this is where the cases are.

For the four-hour emergency wait, the National Audit Office found five kinds of distortion. Patients were held in ambulances outside the door, since the clock started when they entered the department, so that on a bad afternoon the queue was in the car park and the ambulances that held it were not answering calls, which is one target being met by breaking another. Patients were moved, at three hours and fifty minutes, onto a trolley in a corridor that had been redesignated as an admissions area, so that they had left the emergency department in the data and not in fact; the inspection body recorded hospitals admitting patients to hallways. Staff were drafted in for the week the figures were sampled. Cases were reclassified. And the number went, on the official figures, from twenty-three percent of patients waiting over four hours in two thousand and two to five point three percent two springs later, which is a real change and not all of it gaming, and I will come back to that, because the honest reading of this paper is not that the targets did nothing.

For the waiting lists, the National Audit Office found in two thousand and one that nine trusts had inappropriately adjusted their lists, three of them for three years or more, affecting nearly six thousand patient records; five of the nine came to light only under outside pressure. The adjustments ranged from junior staff following an established procedure that happened to be wrong, which is Campbell's corrupted indicator without a corrupt person anywhere in it, to something more deliberate. And for the four-hour target there was a second channel of data, the patient survey, and Bevan and Hood put the two beside each other: in the year two thousand and two to three, on the official returns, a hundred and thirty-nine of a hundred and fifty-eight acute trusts had seen ninety percent of their emergency patients within four hours, and sixty-nine percent of the patients, asked directly, said that had been their experience. The gap between those two figures is not all gaming either; patients remember waits as longer than they were. But it is a second number, collected by a different route from people with no stake in the target, and the paired indicator, which Grove would recognise, is what made the gaming visible. Without the survey, the official figure would have stood.

Chapter Three: Four Kinds of People

The part of the paper I would give to any manager is the part that stops treating the people in the system as a single type. Bevan and Hood borrow a distinction from the economist Julian Le Grand, whose Hirschman-inflected work the shelf met in the Exit, Voice and Loyalty episode, between knights, who serve the public because they want to, and knaves, who serve themselves, and they stretch it into four.

There are saints, who share the goals of the centre so completely that they report their own failures unasked; the authors' example is a London hospital that, in two thousand, twice drew attention to bad results in its own transplant programme and suspended it. There are honest triers, who broadly want what the centre wants and do not game, but who will not volunteer what they are not asked. There are reactive gamers, who want it too, and will game the target when they have a reason and an opportunity, which under a regime of terror is often, and who are the majority in any large organisation. And there are rational maniacs, whose goals are not the centre's at all, and who will do whatever the target rewards, up to and including harm, if the system's checks are weak enough; the authors' example is Harold Shipman, the family doctor who murdered his patients for years while his practice statistics looked ordinary.

The reason the taxonomy matters is that a target regime is designed for a population of honest triers, and its consequences fall as though everyone were a maniac. The saints and the honest triers are punished by it, because they report the truth and the truth is worse than their neighbours' fictions, which is Campbell's Orlando Wilson, the honest chief whose crime rate rose. The reactive gamers are converted by it, since a system that punishes the truth teaches the truthful to stop, and the conversion is the real damage, because it is a change in the organisation's character rather than in its figures. And the maniacs are not caught by it, because the numbers are the last place a maniac shows. A regime that is severe on numbers and absent from the ward produces, over a few years, a workforce of gamers, a set of numbers that mean nothing, and a Shipman with a clean record, and it does this to people who began as knights.

Chapter Four: The Other Side of the Ledger

Now the honest complication, because the shelf's rule is that a two-sided fact is not flattened. England's waiting times fell. Between the year two thousand and three years later the proportion of patients waiting more than a year for an operation fell steeply in England and did not in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, which had the same health service and the same money and did not run the star ratings, and a series of later econometric studies by Carol Propper and colleagues, comparing England to the other nations of the United Kingdom over the decade, found that targets with sanctions reduced waiting times substantially and, as far as anyone could measure, without the collateral harm to unmeasured care that the gaming literature would predict. The four-hour figure fell from twenty-three to five, and the patient surveys, though they showed a gap, showed improvement too, and the National Audit Office found satisfaction had risen. Bevan and Hood say this plainly. Their argument is not that the targets failed. It is that the reported success was a synecdoche, a part standing for a whole, and that the regime had no way of knowing how large the part was, because it had been built on the assumption that measurement problems did not matter and that the people it measured would not respond to being measured, and both assumptions were false, and the second was false in the direction that the regime itself had caused.

So the finding is narrower and more useful than the law alone would suggest. Targets with consequences do change behaviour, and some of the change is the change you wanted. The problem is that the number cannot tell you which part, and the harder you lean on it the smaller the part you wanted becomes. That is a design problem, and design problems have designs.

Chapter Five: What They Recommended, and What Grove Would Add

Bevan and Hood end with remedies, and they are modest, which is a point in their favour, since the literature's grander remedies have mostly been tried. The first is randomness. If the units being measured do not know in advance which of their numbers will be checked, or when, or against what, then the reactive gamers have no target to game and the effort spreads back across the distribution. They compare it to the speed camera whose location is not announced: the deterrent is the uncertainty, and a system that publishes exactly what it will measure has published the instructions for gaming it. The second is audit, and specifically the audit hole. England in those years measured everything and verified almost nothing; the numbers went from the trust to the centre without anyone independent looking at how they were made, and the authors' proposal was face-to-face scrutiny with random visits, the inspector who turns up and walks the corridor that has been renamed. The third is a set of second channels: the patient survey beside the official return, the complaint data beside the satisfaction score, so that no number stands alone.

Put that beside Grove and the shelf's earlier material and a fuller design emerges, and I want to lay it out as a set of rules, because the listener who runs something will want to carry it. Every indicator has a partner that catches its gaming, chosen so that the two cannot be moved in the same direction by the same trick, which is Grove's rule and Bevan and Hood's second channel, and it is the first thing to do and most organisations never do it. Every indicator is reported as a distribution and not a threshold, since the threshold is what produces the spike at eight minutes, and a manager who sees the histogram sees the spike, and one who sees the percentage sees seventy-five. The sample that will be verified is chosen at random after the numbers are in, so that the people producing them cannot know which ones will be looked at. The verification is done by someone who walks the corridor, at a frequency the producers cannot predict, and it is done as often on the units with good numbers as on the ones with bad, since the good numbers are where the gaming lives. And the denominators are chosen with more care than the numerators, because the exception report, the patient excluded as unsuitable, the case reclassified as not applicable, is where the forty-nine-dollar larceny hides, and a number whose denominator is under the control of the person being measured is not a number.

There are two further rules that come from Campbell rather than from the English data, and they are the hard ones. The first is about stakes. Campbell's law is proportional to the consequence, and a manager who wants a true number has to decide what the number is for, learning or judgement, and cannot have both from the same figure. A figure used to find out how a programme is going can be honest if the people who produce it know that a bad result brings help rather than punishment; one used to rank and reward will drift, whatever the audit, because the audit catches the fraction it samples and the drift is in the whole. That means keeping the figures that judge people few and heavily verified, and the figures that tell you what is happening many and unpunished, and never letting the second kind slide into the first, which is what happens when a dashboard built for learning is shown to a funder. The second is about distance. The law is strongest where the number is made far from where it is read, by someone whose standing depends on it, in a process the reader cannot see. Every one of those three can be shortened: the reader can go to the place of making, the standing can be detached from it, the process can be made visible. Most managers shorten none of them and then complain about the numbers.

Chapter Six: A Programme on Forms

I want to run the design on a case, and the case I know is a programme delivered at a distance: a counselling programme run across a region from a small central team, in which frontline workers record each session on a form, supervisors record each supervision on another, and the centre sees a dashboard built from both. I will keep it general, since the shape is the shape of most community health programmes anywhere, and the numbers I use are illustrative and not drawn from any one of them.

Start with the indicator the centre watches most: sessions delivered. It is a single criterion in Ridgway's sense, and it has already done what such a criterion does. A form can be completed in a minute without a session having happened, and the dashboard cannot tell the difference, and a worker who is asked every month how many sessions she delivered has been told what the job is. The paired indicator is the first repair, and it has to be one the form cannot produce: the duration of the session where the device records it, a recording where the programme makes them, a client's own account gathered by a different route from a random handful of clients a month. Pick the partner so that a ticked form without a session fails it, and report the two together, sessions logged beside sessions confirmed, and watch the gap rather than the count. The gap is what matters, and a programme that reports only the count has published the instructions for gaming it.

Then the denominator. A programme of this kind has, typically, a large share of women who were enrolled and never started, and the temptation, exception reporting under a different name, is to compute completion among those who began. That number will look good and will mean nothing, because the population it describes is the population that did not fail, and the people who never started are the programme's largest finding. The rule is that every rate is computed on everyone enrolled, and the never-starters are reported as their own line, by site, beside it, so that the site with a fine completion rate and a large never-started column is visible as what it is.

Then the distribution. A supervisor asked whether each counsellor was supervised this month will say yes; asked for the date of each supervision she will produce dates, and the dates will cluster on the last two days of the month, which is Soviet storming on a form, and the clustering is the diagnostic. Report the histogram, not the percentage. A centre that sees the last-week spike has learned something the percentage would have hidden, and has learned it without accusing anyone.

Then randomness and the corridor. Each month a sample of forms is drawn after the month has closed, and someone, a supervisor from another district, a member of the central team on a visit, goes and asks the client whether the session happened and what was discussed. The sample is small and the fact of it is known and the selection is not, and the visits fall on the sites with the best numbers as often as the worst. The purpose is not to catch people. It is to make the number true by making it checkable, which changes what the reactive gamer does before anyone is caught.

And then the stakes, the rule the centre controls least and needs most. If a counsellor's continuation, or a supervisor's standing, or a district's funding, rides on the session count, then every rule above will be gamed in the end, because the audit samples a fraction and the incentive works on the whole. The centre has to decide which few numbers people are judged by, verify those heavily, and let the rest be what Campbell wanted them to be, indicators collected by people who know that a bad number brings a visit and a conversation rather than a consequence. The dashboard built for learning must not be shown to the funder as a scoreboard, or it will become one, and the moment it does the programme will have two sets of numbers, the ones it reports and the ones that are true, and will slowly lose the ability to tell them apart.

None of that is exotic. Every element of it is in a fifty-six paper, a seventy-five conference proceeding, a seventy-six occasional paper, and a two thousand and six article about English hospitals. The reason it is not done is the reason the three discoverers had to discover it separately: the people who build the dashboards do not read the people who studied what dashboards do.

Chapter Seven: The Objection, and a Verdict

The case against the constellation is that it proves too much. Stated at full strength, the law says that any number with a consequence drifts, and since every number a manager uses has a consequence, the law forbids management by numbers altogether, which nobody believes, and which the English waiting-time data contradict. The critics of the gaming literature, and there are some, argue that its examples are selected, that the spike at eight minutes is a small distortion in a large improvement, that the alternative to imperfect targets is not perfect judgement but no accountability at all, and that a regime under which an honest trier is occasionally punished still beats one under which nobody is ever asked. A tidy classification of the variants of Goodhart's law, by David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant in twenty eighteen, makes a related point from the other side: there are at least four different mechanisms behind the law, from the merely statistical, where the proxy and the goal were only ever correlated and optimising the proxy finds the part that was not, to the fully adversarial, where someone is deliberately breaking the link, and a remedy for one does nothing for the others. The random audit catches the adversary. It is no help against the surrogate endpoint that was never going to carry the outcome, and the cardiologists in the last episode were not gaming anything.

I accept most of that, and I think it changes the verdict from a warning to a prescription. The constellation is not an argument against measuring. It is an argument that a number is a made thing, made by a person, under conditions, and that the manager's job is to know the conditions before believing the number. Read Ridgway, all seven pages. Read Campbell's section on indicators and skip the rest unless you evaluate programmes for a living, in which case read the rest twice. Read Bevan and Hood in full, because it is the one with the data, and read it with the histogram of your own reported numbers open beside it. Skip Goodhart's paper, which is about British monetary policy in nineteen seventy-four and is not the better for the law being in it, and read Strathern's sentence instead, knowing that it is hers.

The dose is the shelf's usual for a cynical frame, with one adjustment. Diagnostically, the law is a medicine, and a manager who applies it to every number she is given will be right more often than one who does not. Taken as identity, as the belief that all numbers lie and all reporters game, it is a poison, and a specific one, because it produces the manager who stops believing her honest triers, and Bevan and Hood's whole finding is that the honest triers are the ones a regime of suspicion converts.

The question to carry into the week is a design question, and it has three parts, and they are short. For the one number your work is most judged by, name its partner, the figure that would show if it were being gamed. Name the last time someone walked the corridor to see how it was made. And name what happens to the person who makes it if it comes in bad, because that, and not the number, is what the number is measuring.
