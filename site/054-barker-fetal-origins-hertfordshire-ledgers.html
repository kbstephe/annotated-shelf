Barker and the Fetal Origins of Adult Disease: the Hertfordshire baby ledgers, the thrifty phenotype, and the effect sizes that shrank as the studies grew

David Barker read a county's infant-welfare ledgers against its death certificates and concluded that a heart attack at sixty could begin in a mother's pregnancy, and the half century since has been a test of how much of that survives larger studies, famines, twins and genomes.

30 September 2026

Chapter One: A Midwife's Ledgers

Sometime in the early years of the twentieth century, health visitors in Hertfordshire began weighing babies. They weighed them at birth, and they came back and weighed them again at one year, and they wrote the numbers down in ledgers, together with the name of the midwife who had delivered the child and a note on how the baby was being fed. Nobody who wrote those numbers had a use for them beyond the welfare of the particular infant on the scale. The aim was to catch a failing child early, and a ledger is a poor place to look for a theory of heart disease.

The ledgers survived. The Southampton group's account of the Hertfordshire records credits their survival to one midwife, Ethel Margaret Burnside, whose foresight left a county with roughly thirty-seven thousand people, born between 1911 and 1939, whose early lives were on paper. In 1989 a group led by an epidemiologist named David Barker took the men from those ledgers, found out which of them had died and of what, and asked whether a baby's heft at twelve months bore any relation to the manner of his death sixty years later.

The answer they published in the Lancet that September ran against a generation of medical common sense. Among about fifty-six hundred men, the boys who scaled eighteen pounds or less at their first birthday were about three times as likely to die of ischaemic heart disease as those at twenty-seven pounds or more. The gradient ran smoothly across the middle weights as well. It made the small baby the future coronary patient, and it implied that the arteries that fail in a man at sixty had been set on their course before he could walk.

This episode is about that claim, and about the question that sits beneath it, which is much older than Barker. When a disease arrives late in life, where should an investigator look for its beginning? The twentieth century's first answer was in the adult, in his cholesterol and his cigarettes and his blood pressure, and the previous episode followed one branch of that answer through Ancel Keys's tables and Minnesota's basement boxes. Barker's answer was that the adult risk factors were downstream of something else. The trouble with an answer like that is that it is nearly impossible to test cleanly, because the exposure lies seventy years before the outcome, and every step between them is open to confounding. So this is a study of an idea whose evidence is entirely observational, and the question that organizes the chapters is a trialist's: what would have to be true for the association to be caused by the womb, and what else could have produced the same ledger entries?

Chapter Two: Two Maps That Lined Up

Barker did not begin with babies. He began with maps, and the maps came from a habit of British public health, which is to publish death rates by district.

In the early 1980s the standing explanation for the rise of coronary heart disease across the twentieth century was an affluent adult life: rich food, cigarettes, inactivity. The difficulty, which Barker and his colleague Clive Osmond put in a 1986 Lancet paper, was that the geography did not fit. Death rates from heart disease in England and Wales were highest in the poor, industrial districts of the north and the west and in the Welsh valleys, and lowest in the prosperous south-east. If coronary disease were a disease of abundance, the map should have been inverted. What the district pattern resembled instead was the map of infant mortality from decades earlier. Places where babies had died in large numbers in the years around 1900 to 1910 were places where middle-aged men were dying of heart attacks in the 1970s.

A modern reader recognizes the move, because it is the one Keys's critics made against him. An ecological correlation between two maps says nothing about individuals; the districts with high infant mortality were also the districts with poor housing, bad schools, heavy smoking and hard manual labour. Barker knew the objection and treated the map as a hypothesis generator. The mechanism he proposed for the match was that both sets of deaths came from a common cause, poor growth in the womb, which killed some babies outright and left the survivors with a lasting susceptibility. Poor mothers in poor districts, in his account, grew poor babies.

To move from the map to the person, he needed a place where one could see the individual baby's start and the individual man's end in the same record. Hertfordshire was that place, and so, later, were Sheffield and Helsinki and a few others. This is why the work is an archive story as much as a physiology story. The Hertfordshire ledgers were the equivalent of Frantz's basement boxes: a record made for one purpose that a later generation could read for another, and the interest of the finding depends entirely on whether the records were made carefully and the people in them could be followed.

The follow-up in 1989 leaned on the fact that Britain had a national death registry. Men in the ledgers were flagged, and their deaths came back with a cause. That took care of the outcome. The exposure, though, was weight at one year, not weight at birth. In the 1989 paper the association with birth weight was there but weaker, and the strong gradient was with weight at twelve months, which the authors read as a marker of growth and nourishment across the first year of life. That detail matters later. A theory of the uterus had, in its founding paper, its best signal in the months after birth.

Chapter Three: The Thrifty Phenotype

A finding with an unexplained mechanism is exposed, and the reception of the Hertfordshire result was mixed for that reason. The men did not have heart disease because they were small babies in any way a pathologist could point to. Something had to connect the ledger to the artery.

Barker's group reported more of the same in the years that followed. In 1993 they published a Sheffield cohort of 1,586 men born at the Jessop Hospital between 1907 and 1924. Standardized mortality from cardiovascular disease fell from 119 in the men who had been born at five and a half pounds or under to 74 in those above the eight-and-a-half-pound mark. Small head circumference and thinness at birth predicted the same, and a large placenta relative to the baby predicted worse: a standardized mortality of 137 in the highest fifth of the placental ratio. The Sheffield paper widened the story from small to disproportionate, meaning babies whose growth had been cut short in a particular way, and the placenta entered the argument as evidence that the mother's supply line mattered as much as the baby's own biology.

The mechanism arrived in 1992 in a paper by Barker and Nicholas Hales, a Cambridge diabetologist, in the journal Diabetologia. It was titled with the phrase that has stuck to the field ever since: the thrifty phenotype hypothesis. The name plays against an older idea. In 1962 the geneticist James Neel had proposed a thrifty genotype, an inherited tendency, selected during long ages of feast and famine, to store energy efficiently, which had become a liability in an age of abundance. Hales and Barker argued that no genetic story was needed. A fetus that receives too little nourishment, especially too little protein, adapts. It diverts resources to the brain, at the expense of the pancreas, the liver, the muscle, the kidney; its tissues become less responsive to insulin so that scarce glucose is saved for the organs that must have it; its endocrine pancreas is left with fewer beta cells. That is a sound bargain for a child who will live in a hungry world. Delivered into a well-fed one, the same child meets abundance with a body built to hoard, and that mismatch produces type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and the lipid disorders that lead to coronary disease.

Consider what this offered a working scientist. It explained the ledgers, the maps and the diabetes literature with one thread. It was also testable in a narrow sense, because it made a prediction about who would be at risk: those born small who then grew large. A thin baby fed into an obese adult was the person the mismatch would hurt most, and later Helsinki data seemed to say so. Eriksson and colleagues, following 4,630 men born in the Helsinki University Hospital between 1934 and 1944, reported in 2001 that the men who later had coronary events had been small and thin at birth and had put on weight rapidly in childhood. A story in which the trouble was the gap between the fetal forecast and the adult reality had found its clinical form, and the phrase for it, catch-up growth, entered obstetric and paediatric language.

Before the evidence turns, the hypothesis at its best. A reader who thinks the field was foolish has not met the argument, and in its best form no single ledger is the proof. The claim is that a family of findings, which do not share a common bias, point in the same direction. The geographic maps, the individual cohorts in several towns, the diabetes, pressure and cholesterol gradients, animal experiments in which protein-restricting a pregnant rat produces adult offspring with high blood pressure and insulin problems, and, as the next chapter shows, the natural experiment of a famine. The hypothesis also carries a public-health consequence that is hard to dismiss: if it is even partly right, the cheapest cardiovascular prevention program in existence is a well-nourished mother.

Chapter Four: The Winter of 1944

The nearest thing to an experiment that the field ever got was inflicted on the western Netherlands in the last months of the Second World War. After the railway strike of September 1944 and a German embargo on food transport, the daily official ration in the cities of the west fell, by the worst weeks of early 1945, to somewhere below a thousand calories and in places well below that. The hunger lasted until liberation in May. Every birth in the affected area during those months had a known date, the hospitals kept records, and the population was otherwise well fed before and after. Nutrition scientists have found no cleaner design, and no one would want a better one.

The first analysis, by Zena Stein, Mervyn Susser and their colleagues, and by Ravelli, Stein and Susser in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1976, used the draft records of about three hundred thousand nineteen-year-old men. The finding has the shape that rewards a careful reader with a surprise. Men exposed to famine in the first half of pregnancy had higher rates of obesity at nineteen. Men exposed in the last trimester and in early infancy had lower rates. Timing decided the sign of the effect. If undernutrition simply harmed the fetus, the two windows should have moved together; instead they moved in opposite directions, which said that the developing body was responding to something specific about when it was deprived, and the response was not a proportion of the damage.

The heart disease result took longer, since the exposed babies had to grow old. Tessa Roseboom and her colleagues in Amsterdam followed people born in an Amsterdam hospital around the famine and reported in 2000 that coronary disease was more common among people who had been in the first months of gestation during the famine: eight point eight percent, against three point two in the unexposed. Mid and late gestation showed no rise. The exposure groups were small. The early-gestation group held sixty-eight people, so that percentage works out, by my own arithmetic, to six cases. Six people is not a sample on which anyone should hang a field. Still, the direction suited the theory in one respect and embarrassed it in another. Famine before birth did seem to leave a mark on adult cardiovascular health. Yet the association ran independently of birth weight: coronary patients in the cohort tended to have been lighter and small-headed at birth, but the early-gestation exposure did not act through that route. A famine in the first trimester could leave a mark without leaving a small baby.

That result, which the Barker school welcomed as evidence of programming, is in fact a difficulty for the ledger findings. If birth weight is not the pathway, the ledgers may have been measuring something that travels alongside the pathway. The Dutch group's later work on glucose tolerance in the exposed, with impaired glucose handling in late middle age in those who were in utero during the famine, fits the thrifty phenotype better than it fits a story about size. But it also suggests that the mechanism was a matter of timing and tissue, and not of whether a baby was small. Size at birth was a rough proxy for a process nobody could see.

Set that beside the Minnesota volunteers of an earlier episode, who lost a quarter of their weight and recovered on a refeeding program that was the whole point of the study. Keys starved adults to learn how bodies come back. The Hunger Winter starved a population without asking, and the bill came due in the babies. Set side by side, they show what a famine can and cannot license us to say. Minnesota showed that an adult body under a controlled deficit changes its resting metabolism, and that the change reverses. The Netherlands suggested that a fetus under an uncontrolled deficit might not come back.

Chapter Five: The Effect Sizes Shrink

The next stretch of the history is a sequence of failures to hold the effect at its original size, and it belongs to the epidemiologists who did the failing.

The cleanest illustration is blood pressure, because blood pressure was, in the 1990s, the easiest of the fetal-origins outcomes to measure in large numbers. Barker's group and many others had reported that lower birth weight predicted higher systolic pressure in adulthood, and by the late 1990s the association had been reported often enough that a reader could count it among the pillars of the hypothesis. In 2002 Rachel Huxley, Bruce Neal and Rory Collins published in the Lancet a meta-analysis that gathered fifty-five studies with quantitative estimates of the association and added forty-eight further studies that earlier reviews had left out, along with seven studies of identical twins.

What they found was a gradient by sample size. In studies of fewer than about a thousand people, each additional kilogram of birth weight went with a fall in systolic pressure of about one point nine millimetres of mercury. In studies of between one and three thousand, the fall was one point five. In studies of more than three thousand, it was point six. The estimate had shrunk to a third of its original size as the studies got large enough to be trusted. The twin comparisons, which hold genes and mother constant, gave an association of the same small size, about point six. The authors said that the strong claims for an inverse association between birth weight and blood pressure might chiefly reflect random error, selective emphasis of particular results, and misjudged corrections for confounders and for present-day weight. They also found evidence of publication bias, meaning that small studies that found nothing had tended to stay in drawers.

That last complaint needs unpacking, since the trap is easy to fall into. Suppose you want to know whether a light newborn becomes a hypertensive adult. Pressure rises with body mass, so a researcher corrects for present weight, comparing men of equal build. Among equally built men, though, those who began small are by construction the ones who grew fastest afterwards. The corrected analysis is now comparing rapid gainers with steady ones, and it finds the gainers have higher pressure. That result is real. It is also not what the unadjusted birth weight association meant. Correcting for a variable that lies on the causal path between exposure and outcome, or that shares a cause with the outcome, can produce an association steeper than the truth, and the fetal-origins literature had leaned on such corrections for years. Lucas, Fewtrell and Cole made the general version of this point in the BMJ in 1999, and the later critique of the Helsinki paper, by Cole, Fewtrell and Lucas, made the same argument against its claim that low weight gain in infancy raised coronary risk: the analysis, they said, had not separated infant weight gain from weights at successive ages, and if it had, the gain would have looked unrelated to risk.

The most compact of the sceptical assessments came earlier, in 1995, from Nigel Paneth and Mervyn Susser, in a BMJ editorial entitled, with the quotation marks, the Barker hypothesis. Their argument was that the evidence was suggestive and the inference overreached: the cohorts had been assembled from records made for other purposes, the men who survived to be studied were a selected population, the adult confounders were incompletely measured, and birth weight in those years was a poor summary of anything, since it was recorded by hand, by whoever was there, on scales of unknown accuracy.

Those objections do not touch every result equally, and that is the trouble with the phrase, effect sizes shrink. The blood-pressure association shrank the most, and by the end of the debate few investigators wanted to defend a large effect. The diabetes association held up better. And the Dutch famine result, with all its small numbers, did not depend on birth weight at all. A defender of Barker could say, correctly, that the meta-analysis had taken down one of several supports, and the structure was not a single column.

The reception has to be told by the critics themselves. Huxley's group did not conclude that the hypothesis was false. Theirs was a narrower claim: for blood pressure the best evidence showed an association too small to matter clinically, of order half a millimetre of mercury per kilogram, and that the large early effects reflected the way small studies get published. The strongest sceptical reading, from Paneth and Susser onward, was that the field had built a large theory on associations of uncertain size, in cohorts that could not remove confounding, and had mistaken agreement between several biased studies for independent confirmation. Five studies with the same kind of bias, all pointing the same way, do not add up to five confirmations.

Chapter Six: The Explanation That Needs No Womb

While epidemiologists argued about sample sizes, a group of geneticists offered a different sort of challenge. Suppose the association is real, at some size. Suppose it is not caused by fetal nutrition at all.

The argument came from Andrew Hattersley and Jeremy Tooke at Exeter and was published in the Lancet in 1999 under the title The fetal insulin hypothesis. It began with a rare and instructive disease. Some families carry mutations in the glucokinase gene, which regulates how the pancreas senses glucose. Adults who carry one such mutation develop a mild form of diabetes. Hattersley's group had studied babies born to families in which one parent carried the mutation, and found something the thrifty phenotype could not have predicted. When the baby inherited the mutation, the baby was on average about five hundred and thirty-three grams lighter, in a sample of fifty-eight offspring. The mother's diet was not involved. The mutation reduced fetal insulin secretion, and insulin is a growth hormone in the fetus, so a baby who secretes less grows less.

Follow the logic. If genes that impair insulin secretion, or genes that make tissues resistant to insulin, lower birth weight in the fetus and raise the risk of diabetes in the adult, then the two would go together in every population, with no programming involved. A small baby and a diabetic adult would be the same genotype seen at two ages. Barker's ledgers would be reading a genetic signal through the medium of birth weight. Hattersley and Tooke also pointed out that the same reasoning could apply to insulin resistance, hypertension and vascular disease, since insulin resistance is a core feature of all of them, and a resistant fetus is a smaller fetus.

The two hypotheses make different predictions, which is what a good rivalry does. The thrifty phenotype says that the association arises through the environment and should be visible even between genetically identical people, so that among identical twins the lighter twin should have the worse outcomes. The fetal insulin hypothesis says the association is shared genetics and should vanish between identical twins, and should appear when a fetal gene variant is tested directly. Twin studies of low birth weight and diabetes, cited in the Vaag and colleagues' review of the thrifty phenotype, found lower birth weights in the diabetic twin of a pair, which favours an environmental component. That same review noted that only two of forty-five known type 2 diabetes susceptibility genes were associated with low birth weight, which favours the view that the diabetes association is mainly non-genetic. The review's authors, who are advocates of the thrifty phenotype, drew the natural conclusion. It is a conclusion from advocates, and it should be labelled that way.

The genome-wide era gave the fetal insulin hypothesis a fairer trial than it could have had in 1999. In 2016 Momoko Horikoshi and colleagues in Nature reported a meta-analysis of genome-wide data on birth weight in 153,781 people, identifying sixty places in the genome where the baby's own genotype was associated with its weight. Fetal genetics captured about fifteen percent of the variation in birth weight, so a large share of what makes babies heavy or light is not fetal genes, but the fifteen percent was informative. When the authors looked at the same genetic signals in adult disease, they found inverse genetic correlations between birth weight and systolic blood pressure, of negative twenty-two hundredths for systolic pressure, negative twenty-seven for type 2 diabetes, and negative thirty for coronary artery disease, on a scale where one is perfect agreement. Variants that fatten a newborn tended to protect the adult, and variants that slim one tended to harm, in all three conditions Barker's programme had claimed. The authors read this as evidence that the life-course association between early growth and adult cardiometabolic disease is, in part, the result of shared genetic effects, and they identified some of the pathways.

The wording of that finding needs exactness, and the hedges in it are the authors' own. In part. The genetic correlation of minus point three means that about a tenth of the variance is shared, and the rest is not accounted for by this route. The finding does not say that the environment plays no role; it says that any study that attributed the whole of the ledger's gradient to the womb was attributing to the mother's kitchen a signal that was partly in the baby's chromosomes. The fetal insulin hypothesis was right about something, and it was not the whole. A field that had run on a single explanation for twenty years now had two, and the true share of each was undetermined.

Chapter Seven: The Gambia and the Hungry Season

The evidence so far comes from England, Finland and the Netherlands, and the ideal population for testing programming is one where the mother's nutrition changes with the calendar and no one can adjust for it, because everyone is exposed. The Gambia offers that: a country in which the rainy season is also the hungry season, when the stores are low and the farm work is at its heaviest.

The MRC's research station in the West Kiang district has kept demographic records on a set of villages since the middle of the twentieth century, and in 1997 Sophie Moore, Tim Cole, Andrew Prentice and their colleagues used the births and deaths recorded since 1949 to ask whether the season of a person's birth predicted the date of their death. In Nature they reported that people born in the hungry season were substantially more likely to die young in adulthood; the paper's own summary put the excess at up to tenfold for some groups, in a population where adult deaths at those ages were otherwise rare. The authors suggested that the likely explanation was a permanent effect of undernutrition on the developing immune system before birth.

This is the study to compare with Hertfordshire, because it fixes the weaknesses of the ledgers and inherits other weaknesses. The season of birth is not a self-reported trait or an adjusted variable, and it cannot be confounded by adult lifestyle, since a Gambian farmer does not choose his month of birth. On the other hand, the effect the authors observed was not on the diseases of abundance. It was on early adult mortality, which the authors tied to immune development, an outcome a thrifty phenotype in the pancreas would not predict, and the sample was three villages. The finding was a warning that programming may operate on other systems than the ones Barker examined, and a reminder that the hypothesis is a family rather than a claim.

A larger body of evidence for low-income settings came from the five long-running birth cohorts, in Brazil, Guatemala, India, the Philippines and South Africa, that Cesar Victora and colleagues analysed together in the Lancet in 2008. The results were sobering for the hypothesis in the form in which it had been popularized. Undernutrition in the mother and the young child was strongly and consistently associated with shorter adult height, less schooling, lower economic productivity, and, for women, smaller babies of their own. Those are outcomes the fetal-origins literature had barely considered. On the cardiometabolic outcomes the picture was thinner: birth weight and early growth were related to adult blood pressure, glucose and body mass, but modestly, and in the older analyses higher weight gain after the age of two predicted worse outcomes, in keeping with the catch-up story. The authors' policy reading was that the strongest justification for preventing undernutrition in early life lies in human capital, in height and schooling and earnings, and that the cardiometabolic argument, while probably real, was smaller than the founding papers suggested.

A scientist reading the whole record has three things to separate. What Barker claimed: that the fetal environment programmes adult cardiovascular and metabolic disease, with birth weight as the marker. What the evidence supports: that early growth is associated with adult outcomes, that the association is real but smaller than the first estimates, that a share of it is shared genetics, that famine in early gestation had lasting effects in the Netherlands that did not run through birth weight, and that the largest and clearest consequences of early undernutrition are in height, schooling and earnings, rather than in heart attacks. What I would conclude: that the strong form of the hypothesis, in which the womb is the principal cause of adult cardiovascular disease, lost, and that the weak form, in which early life sets the range of adult vulnerability and adult exposures do the rest, is now too general to be wrong.

Chapter Eight: What the Ledger Was For

That verdict cuts against the field's own success, and the accounts need auditing. The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease, the name that replaced the Barker hypothesis, has its own society, its own journal and its own funding streams. Its central claim has been generalized so far that almost anything can be filed under it: the narrowing of a birth-weight claim and heart disease turned into talk of the exposome, epigenetics, the first thousand days, maternal stress, air pollution, and the microbiome. A hypothesis that has lost its measurable centre and gained a shelf of applications is, in the language of Popper, a research programme in trouble, or in the language of a working trialist, a set of associations waiting for an intervention that would test them.

That intervention is the missing piece, and the series has met the pattern before. In the diet-heart duel, the tables could not separate collinear nutrients, and the trials that could had tested one man's hypothesis. In the fetal-origins field, the analogous experiment would randomize pregnant women to better nutrition and follow their children for decades, and it is not impossible: several supplementation trials in pregnancy, in Guatemala, in the Gambia, in Bangladesh and elsewhere, have children now in adulthood, and the results, where they exist, have been mixed and modest. A trial of that kind can be designed, and it is expensive and slow, and the person who commissions it will not see its result. What the field has instead is a set of associations from settings where nobody was randomized, and a considerable amount of conviction.

The conviction comes from somewhere respectable, though. Consider the position of a reader who wants to know what to do with all this. If the effect sizes have shrunk, if the genes explain a share, and if the strongest programming evidence lies in famine and season and not in the ordinary variation of birth weight among well-fed mothers, then the individual mother in a comfortable country has little to learn from Barker. A baby that weighs six pounds and not seven is not a cardiac patient in waiting. The hypothesis is not a basis for counselling anyone. Where it retains force is at the level of populations under real deprivation, where the same mother's diet can be too poor to support a pregnancy at all, and there the argument for feeding her has never depended on the thrifty phenotype, because the benefits in birth weight, infant survival, height and schooling are established without it.

The irony of the theory's reception is that it was oversold in the place where it was weakest and undersold where it was strongest. The headline version, small baby, heart attack at sixty, was the most vivid and the least secure. The more durable finding is the one the Dutch winter and the Gambian season hint at: timing matters, tissues differ in their windows, and the body's early adaptations carry costs that are not always visible at birth. Barker was a clinician who wanted a lever for prevention, and the lever he found points at the mother, which is where public health arrived by other roads anyway.

For the reading list, the 1989 Hertfordshire paper is short and worth an evening, less for its result than for its restraint; the weakness is visible on the page, and so is the ingenuity of the record linkage. Follow it with Huxley's 2002 meta-analysis, which teaches a method: plot the effect against the sample size and see what the small studies were doing. Hattersley and Tooke's fetal insulin paper is a model of how to offer a rival explanation that generates its own predictions. Horikoshi's 2016 genetics paper is dense but its abstract is readable, and it is where the argument stands now. Skip the popular accounts that treat the womb as destiny; they are polemics for a view the evidence supports weakly.

The last question travels beyond nutrition. When a large literature reports an association that has shrunk each time the studies improved, the interesting inquiry is what the shrinkage looked like. Did the estimate decline smoothly with sample size, which suggests publication bias, or did it collapse when one adjustment was dropped, which suggests confounding by the adjustment itself, or did it hold in the twin comparison and not in the population, which suggests genes? Each pattern points to a different next study. A trialist reading any observational literature can ask for the plot of effect against precision before asking whether the effect is real, because the answer to the first often tells you what to do about the second.
