Scott Alexander, Considerations on Cost Disease: why a hospital bed, a classroom, and a subway tunnel cost ten times what they did, and nobody got the money

A 2017 essay's accounting of costs that multiplied while wages stood still, the eight suspects it interrogates and clears, the economists who answered it two years later with Baumol's old string quartet, and what the argument looks like from inside an academic medical centre that adds a safeguard every year and has never once removed one.

Chapter One: The Fifteen-Hour Week

In June of nineteen thirty, with the Depression eight months old and getting worse, John Maynard Keynes gave a lecture in Madrid called "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren." He told his audience to ignore the crash. The long trend, he said, was so obviously upward that anyone who could draw a straight line on a graph could see that a century out, their grandchildren would be somewhere between four and eight times richer than they were. And once people were that rich, Keynes reasoned, they would stop working so hard. He predicted a fifteen-hour week. The real problem of the twenty-first century, he thought, would be boredom: what to do with a life that no longer needed to be spent earning a living.

He got the arithmetic right. Output per person in the rich countries is now roughly four to five times what it was when he spoke. Almost nobody works fifteen hours a week. In the United States, a large and inconsistently measured fraction of households report living from one paycheck to the next, and the sectors that a middle-class family cannot avoid buying from, medicine, schooling, a house near a decent school, have grown expensive at a pace that eats most of the raise Keynes promised. Part of the gap between his prediction and the present is inequality: the gains went disproportionately to people who already had plenty. That part gets talked about constantly. Scott Alexander's essay of February two thousand seventeen is about the other part, the one that draws far less attention because nobody has a satisfying story for it. Even where wages did rise, the price of the things a person most needs rose faster, and the people providing those things did not get the money either.

Chapter Two: The Numbers That Started It

Alexander opens with a graph and works outward from it, and the graphs are the reason the essay travelled as far as it did. Spending per pupil in American public schools, adjusted for inflation, roughly two and a half times what it was in nineteen seventy. The national reading and mathematics test scores for seventeen-year-olds over the same period: flat. Not down, which would at least be a story, but flat, within a couple of points across four decades. College: around two thousand dollars a year in nineteen eighty, in today's money, and around twenty thousand now, a tenfold increase, while nobody serious claims a modern undergraduate learns ten times as much. Health care: about five times the nineteen seventy figure, and closer to eightfold since nineteen sixty. Alexander converts that into a unit a listener can feel. In nineteen sixty the average worker paid for a year of health insurance with about ten days of wages. That same worker's counterpart today pays for it with about sixty days, one sixth of everything they earn.

Then infrastructure, where the comparison stops being a matter of interpretation. The first New York subway opened around nineteen hundred at a cost that works out, in current dollars, to something like a hundred million per kilometre, a figure Alexander flags as rough. The Second Avenue line opening the year he wrote cost about two point two billion for the same distance. Paris, Berlin and Copenhagen were building subway at around two hundred and fifty million per kilometre, a tenth of New York's figure. Seoul was digging for forty million. Same tunnels, same trains, same century, a fifty-fold difference in the bill depending on which rich democracy happens to be holding the shovel.

The subway case deserves a moment on its own, because it is the one where every explanation on offer can be tested against a control. Korean tunnellers are skilled workers in a rich economy, so Baumol's tide reaches Seoul as surely as it reaches Manhattan, and yet Seoul builds for a fiftieth of the price. The most careful attempt to explain the gap is the Transit Costs Project, a team at New York University led by the transit writer Alon Levy that spent several years comparing dozens of projects across countries and reported in twenty twenty-three. Their findings do not flatter any single camp. New York's stations were built two to three times larger than they needed to be, with full-length mezzanines nobody uses. The public agencies had hollowed out their own engineering staff and hired consultants to manage other consultants, at a premium and with nobody in-house able to tell when a bid was padded. Utilities and neighbouring agencies could each demand changes and delays without paying for them. Labour costs were higher, but not fifty times higher. Nothing in that list is a law of economics. All of it is the accumulated habit of institutions that stopped being able to say no, and I will come back to that pattern once the rest of the essay's suspects have been heard.

The essay's opening move, the one that turns a complaint into a puzzle, comes next. If medicine costs eight times more, somebody must be receiving that money. So Alexander goes looking for the recipient, and cannot find one. Teacher salaries, adjusted for inflation, are flat over the period, and have lost ground against other occupations. Professors' pay is up slightly per professor, while the average across all faculty has fallen because colleges have replaced tenured staff with adjuncts earning what a barista earns. Nurses went from about fifty-five thousand to about sixty-three thousand between nineteen eighty-eight and twenty thirteen, roughly the general wage trend, with the added detail that the share of nurses holding a degree doubled over the same stretch, so the profession was paying about the same for a more credentialed worker. Doctors: stable, with a growing fraction of that stable salary going to service medical school debt that has itself been multiplying. His summary, in his own words, is that health care and education costs "increase by ten times without a single cent of the gains going to teachers, doctors, or nurses." The money went somewhere. It did not go to the people doing the work.

Chapter Three: The Author's Own Kitchen Table

Last episode introduced the man properly: Scott Alexander, the pen name of Scott Siskind, a psychiatrist writing from the Bay Area, whose blog Slate Star Codex ran from twenty thirteen until he deleted it in twenty twenty and relaunched under his real name. What matters for this essay is a detail he adds himself. His father is a doctor. His mother is a teacher. He trained in medicine and practises it. The two sectors whose costs he is trying to explain are the two his family has spent its working life inside, and he reports what the essay's numbers cannot: that the people in those jobs are miserable, that his own medical school professors told him to his face they could not understand why anyone would still choose the profession, and that the rising price of the service arrived alongside a falling quality of working life for the people delivering it. That is the opposite of what you would expect if the extra money had bought either better care or happier carers. It bought neither. The essay is, underneath the graphs, a son trying to work out where his parents' raises went.

Chapter Four: The Orchestra

The one established theory of this problem is more than fifty years old and belongs to the economist William Baumol, who published it with William Bowen in nineteen sixty-six in a study of the performing arts and formalised it in a paper the year after. Baumol's example has been repeated so often it has become the theory's name in casual use: the string quartet. A Beethoven quartet written in the eighteen twenties needs four players and about forty minutes. It needed the same quartet and the same forty minutes in eighteen twenty-six, and no advance since has shaved a second off it. Two centuries of astonishing productivity growth in farming, manufacturing and transport have done nothing whatsoever for the productivity of playing chamber music. But the musicians live in the same economy as the factory workers. If wages in the productive sectors rise, and the orchestra does not raise its own wages to match, the would-be violinist becomes an engineer instead. So the orchestra has to pay more for the same forty minutes, year after year, without producing one extra minute of music. The price of the quartet rises relative to everything else. That relative rise is the "cost disease," and Baumol spent the rest of his career, up to a book of that title in twenty twelve, arguing that health care and education are the modern economy's string quartets: sectors where the product is a skilled person's time, and that hour cannot be made cheaper the way a car or a phone can.

Alexander knows the theory and states it fairly. Then he raises what seems like a fatal objection, and it is the objection the whole essay turns on. Baumol's mechanism runs through wages. The orchestra has to pay musicians more. Health care and education should therefore be paying their workers more. They are not. Teachers, nurses and doctors have received, in real terms, roughly nothing. So, Alexander concludes, whatever is driving the increase, it cannot be Baumol, because Baumol's mechanism predicts a rise in the one quantity that stayed flat. Hold that objection in mind, because two years later it turned out to rest on a misreading, and the misreading is more instructive than the essay.

Chapter Five: Eight Suspects

With the standard theory set aside, Alexander does what a clinician does with a patient whose diagnosis will not come: he runs the differential. Eight candidate explanations, each tested against the numbers and most of them cleared.

Could the inflation adjustment simply be wrong, so that these things have not really become more expensive at all? He thinks not, because you can still buy the nineteen seventy version. Small religious and private schools educate children at a fraction of the public per-pupil cost, and a cash-only surgery centre in Oklahoma City posts prices a fraction of what a hospital bills for the same operation. The old price still exists. It has simply become impossible to buy at scale.

The Oklahoma case is worth running to its end, because it shows what that price looks like when somebody insists on it. The Surgery Center of Oklahoma, run by an anaesthesiologist named Keith Smith, began posting all-inclusive prices on its website in two thousand nine: one number for a procedure, covering the surgeon, the anaesthetist and the facility, payable in advance, with no insurance billed. The numbers were routinely a fraction of what a hospital across town would bill an insurer for the same operation, sometimes a fifth. Self-paying patients started flying in from other states, and later from Canada. What the centre had removed was not surgical skill, which it pays for at market rates, and not safety, which its outcomes do not suggest it skimped on. It had removed the billing department, the negotiated discount off an inflated list price, the cross-subsidy, and most of the compliance apparatus that a hospital taking public insurance must carry. That is the essay's point in a single building: the nineteen seventy price is still achievable in twenty seventeen, and the gap between it and the hospital's price is made of things that are not medicine.

Is it a market failure, a case of buyers who cannot judge quality and so pay for prestige and amenities instead? Partly, he grants: colleges add climbing walls and speaker series that no student asked for and every student ends up paying for. But market failure predicts that government provision should be cheaper, or the private kind should, depending on your politics, and neither holds. For-profit hospitals are not cheaper than nonprofit ones. Private schools that charge public-school prices are rare. The public and private versions of the same service have inflated together, which is a strange result for any theory that blames one side of that line.

Is it regulation? This is the libertarian's answer, and Alexander gives it a hearing and finds it wanting on one test in particular. The United States went through two well-advertised deregulatory periods, under Reagan and then under the Gingrich Congress, and the cost curves of medicine and education do not bend at either one. Regulation almost certainly contributes, he says. It does not explain the shape.

Chapter Six: The Consult Nobody Ordered

The suspect Alexander knows from the inside is the fear of being sued, and here the essay stops quoting graphs and starts describing his own hospital. A man is admitted after a heart attack. During the admission someone records that he seems low in mood. From that moment a machine engages that no individual would design and no individual can stop. The patient gets a psychiatric consultation to assess for depression, whether or not anyone thinks he is depressed, because a patient who was noted to be sad and then, weeks later, harmed himself would generate a lawsuit whose central exhibit is that note with no consult attached. The consult generates its own documentation, its own follow-up, and its own bill. Everyone involved records the whole sequence as a well-justified clinical decision, because it is, in the sense that every step of it is defensible, and nowhere in the chart does the word "lawsuit" appear. Alexander's line about it is that people outside medicine have no idea how much of medicine is built on this principle. The surveys that ask doctors whether they practise defensively and report reassuring numbers are, in his experience, simply wrong, and every doctor he knows says so.

He then makes the distinction I have found myself using most often since, and he makes it with a hip. Elderly people fall. An old man getting up in the night to use the bathroom is at some real risk of breaking a hip, and the old men Alexander knows accept that risk daily. They do not spend thousands fall-proofing the six metres from bed to toilet, or hire someone to watch them sleep. That is a revealed preference: at home, a person weighs the small chance of a fall against the cost and indignity of preventing it, and mostly chooses to live with the chance. A hospital cannot make that trade. A hospital where an elderly patient falls faces a lawsuit, an incident report, a reportable quality metric, and a story in the local paper. So the hospital puts rails on the bed, an alarm on the mattress that sounds if the patient tries to stand without permission, and a paid assistant whose job is to walk beside him to the bathroom. The individual's risk tolerance and the institution's risk tolerance are different numbers, and as more of life is delivered through institutions, the institution's number governs, whether or not any actual person's preferences have changed. The cost of care rises without anyone deciding that care should be safer. It rises because the entity paying for the safety is not the entity that would have chosen how much of it to buy.

The last two suspects get shorter shrift. Cross-subsidy is real: the uninsured patient's emergency admission is paid for by markups on the insured patient's bill, and the scholarship student's tuition is paid by the full-fare student's. Pensions and benefits might be absorbing money that salaries do not show, though he doubts they are growing fast enough to account for much. And then he stops, and writes the sentence the essay is remembered for. "What's happening? I don't know and I find it really scary."

Chapter Seven: Every Safeguard Has a Birthday

I work in an academic medical centre, and Alexander's hip is the most familiar object in his essay. Let me widen it, because the mechanism he describes for one patient's bathroom trip runs at the scale of the whole building, and seeing it at that scale explains something his eight suspects could not quite reach: why the cost only ever moves in one direction.

Every safeguard in a hospital has a birthday. Somewhere in the institution's past there was an event, a fall, a wrong-site surgery, a missed lab result, a medication given to the wrong patient, and after that event a committee met, and the committee added a step. A second signature. A checklist. A mandatory screening question. A required consult. A new form in the record that cannot be closed until it is completed. Each step was added by reasonable people responding to a real harm, and each step, on its own, costs a few minutes of a skilled person's time per patient. Those minutes multiplied across every patient, every day, forever, are a salary. Multiply the steps across forty years of committees and you have a building full of salaries attached to no visible service, and a nurse who spends, by the better time-and-motion studies, more of her shift documenting care than delivering it.

Take the bed alarm from Alexander's own example and follow it further than he did. In twenty twelve a team led by Ronald Shorr published, in the Annals of Internal Medicine, a cluster-randomised trial across sixteen nursing units of a Tennessee hospital: units were assigned either to an intensive programme of bed-alarm use or to usual care, and the trial counted falls. The alarms went off, the staff responded, and patients on the alarm units fell at the same rate as patients on the units without them. The most widespread technological safeguard against the most litigated hospital harm was tested properly, once, and did nothing measurable. I do not know a hospital that removed its bed alarms in response. I am not sure I know one that could. The alarm's real function was never mainly to prevent the fall. It was to demonstrate, after the fall, that the institution had done what institutions do, and a safeguard that exists to be pointed at in a deposition is immune to evidence that it does not work.

Here is the part that turns a cost into a disease. Nobody can take a step away. The person who proposes removing the mandatory screening question is proposing to put their own name on the next bad outcome the question might have caught. Robert Jackall's managers, four episodes into this feed's run, protected themselves by making sure blame flowed away from them, and a hospital committee runs on the same hydraulics: adding a safeguard moves blame away from you, removing one moves it toward you, and a system in which every actor's incentive points the same direction moves that direction and no other. Dan Davies would recognise the shape too. The committee that added the step is not accountable for its cumulative cost, because the cumulative cost lands on the budget as a whole and on the patient's bill, both of which are accountability sinks in his sense, places where a consequence arrives with nobody's name on it. And the trap from last episode is here as well, at its most literal. A hospital that unilaterally strips its safeguards back to nineteen seventy levels would be cheaper, and would lose: in court, in the published safety ratings, in the newspaper the week its first preventable fall made the front page. Every hospital that keeps the safeguards keeps them partly because every other hospital does. That is a multipolar trap with malpractice law as the referee, and a ratchet is what a trap looks like when you graph it over time.

Chapter Eight: The Economists Answer

Alexander's essay was widely read, and in twenty nineteen two economists at George Mason University, Alex Tabarrok and Eric Helland, published a short book whose title comes from a New York gubernatorial candidate's slogan: "Why Are the Prices So Damn High?" It is, in large part, a reply to the essay, and its answer is that Alexander's fatal objection to Baumol was a misunderstanding of Baumol.

The string quartet, properly understood, was never about musicians getting raises. It is about the opportunity cost of skilled labour. What a hospital pays a nurse is set by what that nurse could earn doing something else, and what a school pays a teacher is set by what a graduate with those qualifications could earn elsewhere. As the productive sectors of the economy got richer, the alternative career available to the kind of person who becomes a nurse or a teacher got more lucrative, and the labour-intensive sectors had to keep pace with that alternative just to hold their staff. Whether teachers' pay rose relative to a factory worker's is the wrong comparison. The right comparison is between what the sector pays and what its workers could get across the street, and when Tabarrok and Helland run that comparison, the flat-wage objection dissolves. Their evidence for the mechanism is that it fits the shape of the data in ways the other suspects do not. The cost rises are consistent across every labour-intensive service, not just the regulated or litigated ones, which is what you would expect from a secular force and not from a policy. The fastest rises came in the nineteen sixties and seventies, when productivity growth in the rest of the economy was fastest, which is what Baumol predicts and what a regulation story cannot explain. And when they compare industries directly, the ones with the slowest productivity growth are the ones with the steepest price increases. They also test the administrator theory directly and find that administrative spending in colleges has held at about sixteen percent of the total since nineteen eighty, so bloat, whatever else it is, is not a growing share.

Alexander reviewed the book in June of twenty nineteen and did something writers rarely do in public: he conceded most of it. "I find their case pretty convincing," he wrote, and offered to share credit with anyone who could clear his remaining doubts. The doubts are worth hearing, because they are a trialist's doubts. Real wages for factory workers have been close to flat for decades, so if the productive sector's wages are supposed to be dragging service wages upward, what exactly is doing the dragging? Teacher pay is up about six percent since nineteen seventy while spending per pupil has doubled, so even granting the opportunity-cost reading, the pay rise accounts for a small slice of the cost rise. And the one with the most bite: Baumol's disease, by its own logic, should not make anything less affordable. If the whole economy is getting richer and the quartet is merely getting expensive relative to cars, you can still buy the quartet. Yet a year of college took about five months of a median worker's wages in nineteen seventy-one and about fourteen months by twenty sixteen. Something made college harder to buy in absolute terms, and the string quartet does not do that.

Chapter Nine: The Other Camps

The Baumol reading did not win the field. Bryan Caplan, an economist at the same university as Tabarrok, replied the same day the review appeared with an argument about causation that a clinician should find familiar. Tabarrok and Helland had cleared regulation and government spending partly on the grounds that they had not changed much over the period. Caplan's point is that a constant can cause a change. A licensing rule that has been in place for fifty years still blocks the cheaper substitute that would otherwise have appeared in year thirty, the nurse practitioner doing what a physician does, the teacher without the master's degree, the immigrant worker not admitted. The rule did not have to tighten to raise the price. It only had to keep standing while the alternatives it forbids became more valuable. Caplan's own estimate was blunt: Baumol matters, but he doubted it "causes even half the price rise."

I have some sympathy for Caplan's point because I help run a programme built on its inverse. The maternal mental health counselling programme I work with across several districts of Sierra Leone delivers a talking therapy through lay counsellors, women from the communities they serve, trained for weeks rather than years, supervised in tiers by a small central team. The design is a deliberate answer to a Baumol problem: there are not enough psychiatrists or psychologists in the country to see even a fraction of the women who need help, and a skilled clinician's hour would cost more than the whole programme could bear. So the programme buys a cheaper hour and spends heavily on training and supervision to make that hour good enough. That is Caplan's blocked substitute, unblocked. It works, the trial evidence for this model of care is now substantial, and it is also the only reason the arithmetic closes. What it has taught me about the American version of the disease is that the substitute was always available. What stands between an American hospital and a cheaper skilled hour is not a shortage of people capable of it. It is the licence, the liability, and the committee, none of which Baumol put there.

The administrator camp has its own numbers and they are not small. Two Harvard physicians, Steffie Woolhandler and David Himmelstein, estimated in the New England Journal of Medicine in two thousand three that administration consumed about thirty-one percent of United States health spending, against about seventeen percent in Canada, and the gap has not narrowed since. That is a different quantity from Tabarrok and Helland's college figure, and it points at something the string quartet cannot see: the United States pays for a billing and insurance apparatus that a single-payer system does not need, and that apparatus employs people whose salaries appear nowhere in the "nurse, teacher, doctor" comparison the essay ran. When Alexander could not find who got the money, part of the answer was sitting in the claims-processing department.

So three things need separating, as they always do when a question has stayed open this long. What Alexander claims is that none of the standard explanations fits and the honest position is confusion. What the evidence, as it has developed since, supports is that Baumol accounts for a large share of the slope, plausibly the majority in education and a substantial minority in American medicine, and that the residual is real, is concentrated in the United States, and is made of administrative overhead, blocked substitutes, and institutional risk aversion of the kind his hip example describes. And what I conclude is that the essay was right to refuse the single-cause story and wrong about which cause to throw out first. Baumol is the tide. The ratchet is what has been built on the shore while the tide was coming in, and the ratchet, unlike the tide, is something a person could in principle do something about.

Chapter Ten: What the Money Would Have Bought

Alexander closes the section before his Keynes coda with an accounting I have not been able to shake since I first read it. If some government programme found a way to provide poor families with health insurance for a few hundred dollars a year, college for about a thousand, and housing at two-thirds of the current price, it would be, he says, the largest anti-poverty achievement in history. That programme already exists, in principle. It is called things costing what they cost a few decades ago. Every dollar of cost disease is a dollar that could have been the fifteen-hour week, or the raise Keynes promised, or a counsellor's salary in a programme that cannot afford one, and it has instead gone to the consult nobody ordered, the alarm on the mattress, and the person whose job is to code the claim for the alarm.

The dosage test this feed applies to every framework applies here with a twist, because this one is not cynical. The diagnostic use of cost disease is to ask, of any price you are asked to pay for a skilled person's time, how much of it is the tide and how much is the ratchet, since only one of those responds to effort. The poisonous use is the one Alexander's own "I don't know" was written to prevent: picking whichever suspect your politics prefers, administrators or regulators or lawyers or markets, and declaring the case closed. Every camp in this argument has a graph. Half of them are wrong about which graph matters, and what the essay leaves behind is the example of its author, who had every professional reason to blame the lawyers, declined to do it until the evidence arrived, and then changed his mind in public when it did.

Chapter Eleven: The Step With No Birthday Anyone Remembers

Somewhere in the institution you work in, there is a step that everyone performs and nobody can date. A form, a signature, a mandatory question, a required review. Find it this week, and ask two things of it: what event it was born from, and what it would cost you personally to propose removing it. If nobody can answer the first, and the answer to the second is "my name on the next bad outcome," you have found the ratchet in its native habitat, and with it the one kind of cost disease that Baumol's tide did not cause and cannot excuse.
