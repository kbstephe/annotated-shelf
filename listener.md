# The listener

Who this show is written for, at the level of a faculty webpage. The narrator
is Claude; this file exists so that when a book's idea has a real application,
it can land on the right part of the listener's work rather than always on
the same programme. Public repo: nothing here that is not already public.

## Role

Kevin Stephenson is a physician and clinical researcher at Washington
University in St. Louis. He is an internist who works as a hospitalist and,
from mid 2026, directs the general internal medicine fellowship, so he trains
early-career physician-researchers and decides how their protected time is
spent. He came up through an NIH career-development award and now writes
and competes for R01-scale and foundation grants, sits on the receiving end of
study-section scores, and reviews for journals in nutrition and global health.

## What he actually studies

Child and maternal nutrition and health in West Africa and Malawi, almost
always as randomised trials run with local partners:

- Treatment of severe and moderate acute malnutrition in children:
  therapeutic food formulations, recovery rates, what the ready-to-use foods
  should contain. Sierra Leone and Malawi, with the Project Peanut Butter
  network.
- Maternal health in pregnancy and after: a nutrition-in-pregnancy trial in
  Sierra Leone, and BLOOM, a maternal mental-health programme delivering a
  talking therapy through lay counsellors across ten districts, supervised at
  a distance and monitored through phone forms and a dashboard.
- School feeding in Ghana: whether a peanut-based supplement changes growth
  and learning, with the methods questions that come with a cluster trial.
- Choline and infant development in Malawi; legume-based complementary
  foods; potassium-enriched salt in pregnancy.

He is often the statistician of record as well as an investigator: he writes
the analysis plans, builds the data pipelines in R, argues with the
databases, and produces the tables the papers rest on.

## The decisions his week is made of

- Which trial questions are worth a decade of someone's life and a
  multi-million-dollar grant, and how to write them so a study section funds
  them.
- How to know, from a laptop in St. Louis, whether a programme thousands of
  miles away is delivering what its forms say it delivers.
- How to read a literature honestly when he has a stake in the answer, and
  how to write a paper so that the next reader cannot misuse it.
- How to train fellows: what to protect them from, what to make them do, and
  when the institution's requirements are spending their best years.
- Safety reporting, ethics committees in two countries, data-sharing rules,
  and the general problem of building things fast inside slow institutions.

## Lenses he brings

A clinician's habits: demand the denominator, ask what the check cannot see,
distrust the unfalsifiable. A trialist's habits: randomise, pre-specify,
worry about who rated the outcome. And a manager's problem he did not train
for: a small central team, a long chain of supervision, and monitoring data
that is itself a performance for the people who read it.

## What he wants from the show

Insight that makes him better at the job above, delivered as analysis rather
than advice. He does not want lessons shoehorned onto his work; when a book
applies, one aside is enough, and most books do not need one.
