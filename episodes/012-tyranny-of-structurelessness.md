## Chapter One: The Decision Nobody Made

In the summer of nineteen sixty-seven, in an apartment on the west side of Chicago, a group of women sat down to build a political organization with no officers, no bylaws, no elections and no chair. Within two years it had all four – unwritten, unnamed, held by people who would have denied, sincerely, that they held anything. That is the finding, and the shape of it is worth holding still for a moment, because it is not the ordinary story of idealists discovering that someone has to book the room. The group did not drift back toward hierarchy out of fatigue or bad faith. It produced a hierarchy at speed, in the presence of everyone, while every member in good conscience believed the opposite was happening – and the structure it produced had a property that the structure they had refused would not have had. It could not be found. There was no office to name, no term to expire, no vote to lose, no minute in which the decision appeared, and therefore no procedure by which anyone who disliked what was being decided could locate the deciding and interrupt it. Eleven episodes of this series have been about organizations that dissolve responsibility, and each of them did it the hard way, through hierarchy, paperwork and the careful downward distribution of blame. Tonight's essay describes a group that achieved the same result in about eighteen months by removing the paperwork entirely. So the question this episode is really about predates the essay by at least sixty years, and the last two episodes left it lying on the table: if power collects in organizations regardless of what their members intend, does abolishing the container do anything to the contents? Michels watched oligarchy form inside a socialist party that held real elections and counted real ballots, and concluded the elections were beside the point. The woman in the Chicago apartment ran the other arm of that experiment, without meaning to, and her results are the ones this series has been missing.

## Chapter Two: The Organizer Who Had Seen a Real Organization

Her name is Jo Freeman, and the reason her account carries weight is that she arrived at that apartment already knowing what an organization looks like from the inside. She finished a political science degree at Berkeley in nineteen sixty-five and went south, to Atlanta, to work for Martin Luther King's Southern Christian Leadership Conference – political education, voter registration, community organizing, in the states where that work was done at genuine physical risk. Whatever else the civil rights organizations of that decade were, they were structured: they had a chain of command, named leadership, defined roles, and a task that punished confusion about who was responsible for what, because the cost of confusion could be somebody's life. Then in June of nineteen sixty-seven she took a course on women at a free school at the University of Chicago, and out of that course and a conference workshop she and Shulamith Firestone convened the meeting that became the first women's liberation group in the city – the Westside Group, so called because it met weekly in Freeman's own apartment. She was a founder, an insider, and a participant with nothing to gain from a hostile account. She was also, by nineteen sixty-eight, a graduate student in political science at Chicago, which meant that the movement she was living inside was simultaneously her professional object; she took her doctorate in nineteen seventy-two with a thesis on the politics of women's liberation as an emerging social movement. Whyte had Fortune's expense account and Parkinson had the Admiralty's archives. Freeman had a case she was standing in the middle of, and the training to write it up. What she wrote up first was not the thesis. In May of nineteen seventy she gave a talk at a conference in Beulah, Mississippi, and the talk became an essay, and the essay's own publication history is evidence for its thesis. It was written up for a movement anthology in nineteen seventy-one and the editors declined to run it. It appeared the following year in a small movement journal, then in the Berkeley Journal of Sociology, then in Ms. in July of nineteen seventy-three, and it travelled the intervening years the way movement documents travelled – mimeographed, passed hand to hand, reprinted without permission, in versions that differ from one another. The early printings carry the movement name she wrote under, Joreen; the later ones carry her legal name. An essay about groups that have no formal way of deciding anything spent three years being circulated by a movement that could not formally publish anything.

## Chapter Three: What the Title Refuses

The essay opens by refusing its own title. There is no such thing as a structureless group, Freeman says – not as a criticism of groups that fail to live up to the ideal, but as a statement about what is possible. Any collection of people that meets for any length of time and for any purpose will develop a structure: patterns of who talks and who defers, who is listened to, who hears about the meeting beforehand, whose objection ends a discussion and whose is absorbed into it. The structure will form whether or not anyone designs it, and here is the move the whole essay turns on – the only real choice available to a group is not structure or its absence, but whether the structure it inevitably has is formal or informal, acknowledged or denied. And a group that declares itself structureless has not escaped; it has simply made its structure unwritten, which means it has made it unregulated. The unregulated version does not distribute itself evenly. In Freeman's account the laissez-faire group becomes, in the essay's phrase, a smokescreen for the strong or the lucky, who establish an unquestioned hegemony over everyone else – and the strength in question is rarely anything as crude as force. It is the ability to keep talking, the free time to attend everything, the prior friendship with the three people whose agreement settles matters. The informal structure that results is built out of friendship networks, and this is where the argument becomes uncomfortable rather than merely correct, because friendship is the one thing in a political group nobody wants to interrogate. Friends listen to each other more closely. They talk between meetings, in kitchens and on telephones, through channels that are not available to anyone else and cannot be opened up without ceasing to be friendship. They arrive at the meeting having already thought together, so their position arrives assembled while everyone else's arrives in pieces. And so the friendship network becomes the group's elite, in the strict sense Freeman insists on: a small number of people whose views carry disproportionate weight in decisions the whole group is formally making. She is careful, and the care is what makes the essay survive contact with its critics, to say what an elite is not. It is not a conspiracy. The members are not meeting in secret to decide what the group will do and then executing a plan; they are usually not even aware, in any articulate way, that they constitute anything. They are friends who happen to share politics, and if accused of running the group they will deny it with complete honesty, because from the inside the experience is not of exercising power but of talking to your friends. That is the watermark this series has learned to look for. Parkinson's officials multiplied through a sequence of individually blameless decisions; Jackall's managers routed blame downward without anyone once deciding to do so; Davies's institutions arranged themselves so that no particular person ever declined to know the thing that went unknown. Freeman found the same signature in a group of forty people who had explicitly organized themselves to prevent it – and the mechanism runs on the most defensible human material any of these books have used as fuel, which is that some people in the room like each other.

## Chapter Four: The Denominator Problem

Now, why should any of this be worse than an ordinary hierarchy? A great many organizations have an inner circle of friends around the chief executive, and this series has spent months documenting what they get up to. Freeman's answer, and it took me a second reading to see how much load it carries, is not about the elite's behavior at all. It is about what the rest of the group can do about it, and the difference is procedural rather than moral. In a formally structured organization, power has an address. Someone holds an office; the office carries a name, a scope, a term, and a way of being taken away; the decisions made under it appear in a record. That address is what makes every remedy possible – you can lobby the holder, appeal above them, vote them out, sue them, or simply know which door to knock on. None of these remedies requires the office-holder to be honest, or even to admit what they are doing. They require only that the position exist on paper. In the structureless group, none of it exists. No office, so no term; no term, so no removal; no record, so no evidence; and no name for the thing you are objecting to, which means that a member who senses exactly what is happening – who can feel the decision arriving pre-made – cannot even formulate the complaint without appearing paranoid, or worse, ambitious. The group's official self-description is available to be used against her: we have no leaders here, so what exactly are you alleging? Put the series' instruments on this and the fit is exact. The Davies episodes named the accountability sink, the arrangement that takes a complaint and routes it somewhere it cannot produce consequences. Freeman has found the sink in its purest available form – not a machine that absorbs responsibility after the fact, but a group that never assigned any in the first place, and so has nothing to absorb. And this, I would argue, is where a clinician's habits earn their keep on material that looks nothing like medicine, because the question a clinician asks of any claimed effect is where the denominator is. Show me not the cases but the population they came from; not the successes but the attempts. A formal structure publishes its denominator continuously and cannot help it: these are the members, this is who was delegated what, these are the votes, here is the count. The informal structure has no denominator at all, and so the question that would begin any audit – of forty women, how many were in the room where this was settled, and which ones – has no answer that can be produced, checked, or contested. What cannot be counted cannot be corrected. The elite is not unaccountable because it is powerful. It escapes accounting because it cannot be measured, and that is by design, out of a principle the group adopted in order to be fair.

## Chapter Five: The Star System, and What It Did to Her

The essay's second consequence comes from outside the group, and it is the one Freeman herself paid for. A political movement is watched by a press that needs someone to quote. Reporters require authoritative statements; editors require names and faces; the entire apparatus assumes that a group which has decided something has also selected someone to say so. A movement that refuses to select spokeswomen does not thereby stop having them. It transfers the selection to the press, which will choose on its own criteria – availability, articulacy, whether the face photographs well – and what results is what Freeman called the star system: women identified nationally as speaking for a movement that had never chosen them and possessed no mechanism to unchoose them. Follow the hydraulics of that for a moment, because it is a closed circuit with no exit. The star has authority she did not seek and cannot decline, since declining is not a thing the press recognizes. The movement has a representative it did not authorize and cannot remove, since removal requires an office to remove her from. And the group's resentment, having no formal channel – no censure motion, no recall, no vote of no confidence – must discharge itself informally, through the only instruments an informal structure possesses, which are social. Punishment instead of removal; a mood instead of a procedure. Freeman published the case report six years later, in Ms., in April of nineteen seventy-six, under the title Trashing. The incident that opened her own case is not in the article – she has told it in later accounts of the Westside Group – and it deserves walking through at its own pace. Ramparts had run a photograph of a woman in a leotard, a button hanging from one breast. The group resolved on a letter to the editor, and four members drafted one without telling her; Freeman, who had magazine experience and could see their draft was too long to print, wrote a pithier letter of her own under her movement name, and Ramparts printed hers and not theirs – and at the next meeting the group excoriated her for it. That was the entire precipitating event: a letter to the editor that appeared under the wrong woman's name. What the article itself documents is what followed, and it was not an argument, because an argument would have required a forum, and there was none. It was ostracism administered in doses individually too small to protest – a thousand cuts with a whip, she calls it: her contributions to collective articles ignored, meeting dates changed without her being told, her name missing from the mailing list, a joint fund-raising plan dissolved on the day she asked to be included in it. And every attempt to get the group to discuss what was happening was met with the assurance that nothing was out of the ordinary – which the listener should recognize, because it is the unanswerable structure of chapter four running as a sanction: no forum, no record, no name for the thing being objected to, and the group's official self-description available for use against her. The friendships that were the group's real structure closed against her one at a time. She dropped out in nineteen sixty-nine, before the essay existed, and described the aftermath as a numb despair and an isolation in which she was, in her own account, psychologically mangled to the point where she could not go on. The article drew more letters than anything Ms. had published to that point, almost all of them from women describing the same thing happening to them. So the theory and the case report were written by the same person about the same organization, four years apart, in the wrong order – the diagnosis first, the presenting complaint second. And the second document establishes something the first only argues: that the informal structure was not merely capable of concentrating power, but capable of enforcement, and that its enforcement was more severe than anything the group's abandoned bylaws would have permitted. No formal organization on this shelf has a sanction like it. A corporation can fire you, which is a defined act with a date and a severance figure, appealable and sometimes actionable. The Westside Group had no power to expel anyone, and so what it did instead had no name, no date, no author, no appeal, and no end.

## Chapter Six: The Case Against

It arrived quickly and from the direction that mattered most, and it deserves a full hearing, because the essay's admirers usually skip it. In the autumn of nineteen seventy-four, in the first issue of Black Rose, the anarcha-feminist Cathy Levine published a direct reply called The Tyranny of Tyranny, and her objection is not that Freeman got the facts wrong. It is that Freeman drew the wrong conclusion from correct facts. Levine's claim is that the small group is where domination is most quickly noticed and most quickly challenged, because everyone can see everyone – that friendship and trust are the material solidarity is actually built from rather than a corruption of it – and that Freeman's proposed remedy, formal delegated authority with procedures and records, is the reintroduction of the very apparatus whose tyranny the movement had left. Reach for the bylaws and you have not eliminated the elite; you have licensed it, given it a title, and made its power permanent instead of contestable. That objection has more teeth than it is usually granted, and the reason is that the previous episode of this series supplies the evidence for it. Michels's iron law was derived from a party that had every one of Freeman's seven principles in some form – delegated authority, elected officers, rotation, published proceedings – and oligarchy formed inside it anyway, which means formalization is demonstrably not a cure. Freeman is proposing structure as the treatment for a disease that the best-documented case in the literature contracted while on that treatment. The second count is the one her own discipline should have caught. The essay ends with a list of principles for democratic structuring – delegate specific authority to specific people for specific tasks by a democratic procedure; require the delegates to answer to those who selected them; spread authority among as many people as is reasonable; rotate tasks; assign them on rational criteria rather than affection; push information out to everyone as often as possible; and give everyone equal access to the group's resources. Read them slowly and they are a good list; several are the standing recommendations of every serious treatment of institutional design, and the last two would have prevented the friendship channel from doing most of what it did. But they arrive as a design proposal from a political scientist with no trial behind them, in an essay whose empirical half is one participant's account of one group. Freeman diagnosed with data and prescribed without any, and the prescription has never, to my knowledge, been tested against the counterfactual it needs, which is a set of comparable movement organizations that adopted the seven principles and a set that did not. The third count is about what happened to the essay rather than what is in it, and it is the one this series keeps having to file. The Tyranny of Structurelessness has become the most quotable available instrument for a particular person: the one who wants an office, a title and a mandate, and who has found in Freeman a way to describe that desire as a service to the powerless. Jason McQuinn's review of it, from the anarchist side, calls it just that – an organizationalist repudiation of anarchism, the text that leftist tendencies reach for whenever they need to justify grafting formal machinery onto anti-authoritarian politics. This is the identity dose again, and the tell is the same as it was with the elite theorists two episodes ago. Used diagnostically, the essay says informal power is real, so find it and give it an address. Used as identity, it says horizontal politics is always a fraud and always was, which conveniently terminates the argument in favor of whoever is currently proposing to chair the meeting. And the honest thing to say about the essay's best-known application is that both readings survive it. When Occupy's general assemblies bogged down – decision-making running aground on process, the floor going to whoever could outlast everyone else in the square – Freeman's essay was the text everyone reached for, and it did describe what was happening. But it did not follow, and it does not follow, that the assemblies would have survived a police raid better with a constitution.

## Chapter Seven: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

The verdict is that this is the field report the iron law never had, and that the two documents should be read as one instrument with two halves. Michels established that formal democratic machinery does not prevent oligarchy. Freeman established that removing the machinery fails too, and costs you every remedy you had. Put them together and the synthesis is not the one the essay's popular reading offers – structure good, structurelessness naive. It is narrower and more useful than that: structure does not stop power from concentrating, it gives the concentration an address, and an address is all that separates an elite you can fight from one you cannot even name. That is a smaller claim than the title promises and it is the one the evidence actually supports. The dose is diagnostic and the essay is short enough to take in one sitting; read it with Trashing immediately after, in that order, because the theory reads as an argument until the case report turns it into a wound, and read Levine's reply the same week, before the identity dose sets, since Levine is the strongest available reason to distrust the remedy while accepting the diagnosis. For the shelf, it pairs backward with the Machiavellians episodes and forward with everything this series has left to do about the antidotes, where the question of whether an organization can be designed to keep correcting itself finally has to be answered rather than deferred. And the question for Monday comes in two halves, both of them cheap. Take the last real decision your organization made – not the announcement, the decision – and ask where it was actually settled and by whom, and then ask whether either of those facts appears in any document that exists. If they do, your shop has an address, and whatever else is wrong with it, you know where to send the complaint. If neither does – if the meeting ratified something that had already been agreed among four people who eat lunch together – then you have found the informal structure, and Freeman's second question is the one that tells you what kind of institution you are in. Ask what those four would lose if their arrangement were written down and given a name. If the answer is nothing, you have found an accident, and it can be fixed with a memo. If it is everything, you are looking at the thing she spent an essay and a case report describing, and the memo is the last thing anyone will let you write. Next time the shelf goes back to the cynical core and to the gentlest-looking book in it – the one that explained, with a smile, why every employee rises to the level at which they can no longer function: Laurence Peter and Raymond Hull's The Peter Principle. Thanks for listening.
