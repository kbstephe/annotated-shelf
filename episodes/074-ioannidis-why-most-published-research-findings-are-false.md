## Chapter One: Ten real variants in a haystack

A research team sets out to test whether any of a hundred thousand gene variants is associated with schizophrenia. From what is known about how heritable the disease is, the team expects that about ten of the hundred thousand are truly involved, each with a modest effect, an odds ratio around 1.3. The study has sixty percent power, meaning that if a real association of that size exists, the study will detect it six times in ten. The significance threshold is the usual 0.05.

Run the study in your head. Of the ten real variants, it finds six. Of the ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety that do nothing, five percent cross the threshold by chance, which is just under five thousand. So the paper reports about five thousand and six associations, and six of them are real. A reader who picks one of the reported variants at random, and who knows nothing else, is looking at something with a chance of about one in a thousand of being true. The paper that works this out gives twelve chances in ten thousand. Before the study the figure was a twelfth of that, and twelve times almost nothing is still almost nothing.

That example is the first boxed example in John Ioannidis's essay "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False," published in PLoS Medicine in August 2005, and it is the least controversial thing in the paper. Nobody who has run a genome-wide scan disputes the arithmetic. The essay's reputation comes from the step after it, in which the same arithmetic is run for medicine in general, and the title says the answer. This episode is about whether that step holds, what the evidence outside the model says, and what the profession then built to repair the problem. It belongs at the end of a run of episodes that started with the cholera map and the childbed ward, because everything in that run, the randomised draw, the sealed envelope, the prespecified comparison, was an attempt to make a single result trustworthy. The question here is how trustworthy a result is when a whole field is producing them at once.

The essay's central idea is a quantity with a plain name. Among all the findings that reach the significance threshold, what fraction are true? That fraction is the positive predictive value, the same quantity a clinician computes for a diagnostic test. Suppose a test is applied in a population where one person in ten has the disease, and then in one where one in a thousand does. A positive result means something very different in the two, and the test itself is identical. Ioannidis applies the reasoning to a published paper. The test is the statistical analysis. The population is the set of hypotheses the field is probing. What the result means depends on how many of those hypotheses were true before anyone looked.

He calls that ratio R, the number of true relationships to the number of non-relationships among those tested in a field. The essay says R varies a great deal by field, from confirmatory work where a hypothesis has already survived years of scrutiny to searches for one or a few real effects among thousands or millions of possible ones. With R, the power of the study and the significance threshold, the paper gives a formula, and the formula yields the sentence that governs the rest of it. A finding is more likely true than false if the chance of catching a real effect, multiplied by the odds that effects are real, beats 0.05.

Put in words, that sentence says a field testing mostly long shots has to be very well powered, or its significant results are mostly noise. A field where one hypothesis in a hundred is right, using studies with half the power, gets a value of 0.005, a tenth of the threshold. The formula says false findings outnumber true ones there, and by about ten to one.

Everything else in the essay is a variation. What would make the hundred thousand polymorphisms look worse?

## Chapter Two: Bias, repetition and the null field

Ioannidis adds two complications to the basic model, and the schizophrenia example shows both with real numbers.

The first is bias, which he defines as the combination of design, data, analysis and presentation factors that tend to produce research findings when they should not be produced. The paper gives it a letter, u, the proportion of analyses that would not have been findings but are presented as findings anyway. It names the routes: serendipitous inclusion or exclusion of patients or controls, post hoc subgroup analyses, investigation of contrasts that were not originally specified, changes in how disease or control is defined, selective reporting. It says commercial data-mining packages are proud of their ability to yield significant results through data dredging. Set u at ten percent, which the paper treats as modest, and the schizophrenia example falls by more than half, to 4.4 chances in ten thousand.

A listener from the earlier episodes will recognise that list as the garden of forking paths, and will recognise the subgroups from ISIS-2, where one trial divided by astrological sign produced a result that the authors knew to be meaningless and showed anyway. Ioannidis is saying the zodiac demonstration is not a joke at the margin of research. It is a model of u.

The second complication is repetition. When several independent teams ask the same question, the chance that at least one finds a significant result climbs with each team, and the one that finds it is the one that publishes with fanfare. In the same example, if ten teams around the world run similar studies and one of them gets a significant association, the probability that it is true is 1.5 chances in ten thousand, barely above where it began. The paper says that is hardly any higher than the probability before all this research was undertaken, and I think that is a fair description of the arithmetic. Ten attempts at a long shot are ten chances to be fooled.

The paper then draws six corollaries, and I will give them as three, because the retention literature the show is built on says a listener cannot hold six. The first group concerns what is being asked: smaller studies, smaller effects, and more relationships tested with less preselection all lower the share of true findings. Large cardiology trials, with thousands randomised, and smoking and cancer, where the relative risks run from three to twenty, sit at the good end. Genetic risk factors for common diseases, at 1.1 to 1.5, and molecular predictors studied in samples a hundredth the size of the cardiology trials, sit at the bad end. The paper adds a line that readers of the nutrition episodes will remember: if the true effects of most nutritional determinants of complex disease are below a relative risk of 1.05, then nutritional epidemiology is largely a utopian endeavour.

The second group concerns flexibility and interest. The more freedom in design, definitions, outcomes and analysis, the more room for u to grow. Fields with agreed outcomes, such as death, and standardised methods, such as Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test, fare better than fields where outcomes are scales invented for the purpose and analytic methods are still experimental. Financial interest raises u, and the paper adds that prejudice need not be financial. A scientist can be committed to a theory, or to promotion, or to a position held for a career, and prestigious investigators may suppress through peer review the findings that refute their own.

The third group is the oddest, and it is the one that has been most disputed. The hotter a field, with more teams involved, the less likely its findings are to be true. The paper's reasoning is that teams racing each other prioritise their most impressive positive results, and negative results become attractive only when they refute a claim by someone else. It names the pattern the Proteus phenomenon, after the sea-god who changed shape, for rapidly alternating extreme claims and extreme refutations, and says the sequence is very common in molecular genetics.

After the corollaries comes a table of simulated cases, and the essay's verdicts on it are the passage most often quoted. A well-conducted, adequately powered randomised trial that starts with a fifty percent pre-study chance that the treatment works is eventually true about eighty-five percent of the time. A confirmatory meta-analysis of good trials does about as well. Underpowered early-phase trials are true about one time in four, or less if bias is present. Exploratory epidemiology does worse. In discovery research where tested relationships outnumber true ones a thousand to one, with thirty thousand genes tested and perhaps thirty real, the value for each claimed relationship is extremely low even with standardised methods.

The table's verdict on randomised trials matters for the show, because the previous episode left a question open. The evidence-based movement ranked designs by their exposure to bias. Ioannidis's model puts numbers on the ranking, and it says the ranking holds. But the number that makes a confirmatory trial good is not the randomisation. It is the fifty percent prior. A trial of a drug that had decent grounds for working is true because it was a decent bet, and a trial of a long shot with the same design and the same power is not.

The paper's most uncomfortable section follows. It supposes a field in which nothing is true at all, a null field, and observes that in such a field every observed effect size is a measure of bias. Its example is a cancer where no nutrient or dietary pattern is truly an important determinant, and where the literature has examined sixty nutrients and claims all of them are related to the tumour, with relative risks of 1.2 to 1.4 for the top intake third against the bottom. The claimed effects, the paper says, are simply the net bias that went into producing that literature. It follows, he writes, that among null fields the ones claiming stronger effects, often with accompanying claims of public health importance, are the ones that sustained the worst biases.

That reverses the usual reaction to a large, highly significant effect, which is excitement. In his reading, too large and too significant is a reason to ask what went wrong with the data, analysis and results. The paper adds that investigators will resist accepting that the field in which they spent their careers is a null field, and that other lines of evidence or advances in technology may eventually dismantle it.

The listener has seen a case. The hormone therapy cohorts in the Nurses' Health Study showed a sizeable heart benefit, and the randomised trial showed none. A reasonable physician in 1995 held the cohort effect as an answer. Ioannidis would have said that an effect that size, from a design with that much room for u, is the signature of the bias and not of the drug, and the Hernán re-analysis, which walked the estimate from 1.42 down to 0.67 by fixing the clock, is what finding the bias looks like in practice. The essay is a general account of why the hormone therapy reversal happened, written before the Nurses' re-analysis showed how.

So the model fits a case the listener knows. What does it say about the base rate in general, outside the cases that were studied because they went wrong?

## Chapter Three: Counting the reversals

An essay that proposes a proof is an invitation to count, and Ioannidis counted the same summer. A paper in JAMA, published in July 2005, took every original clinical research study published in three major general journals or high-impact specialty journals from 1990 to 2003 and cited more than a thousand times, forty-nine in all. Of those, forty-five claimed that the intervention worked. Subsequent studies of similar or larger size and similar or better design were compared against them. Seven, sixteen percent, were contradicted. Seven more had found effects stronger than those of the later studies. Twenty, forty-four percent, were replicated. Eleven, twenty-four percent, remained largely unchallenged.

By design, five of six highly cited nonrandomised studies, five had been contradicted or had found stronger effects. Of thirty-nine randomised trials, nine had. Among the randomised ones, the studies whose effects shrank or reversed were smaller than the ones that held up, although their early citation counts were no different. That is the model's first corollary appearing in the data: small studies of treatments mislead in a way big ones do less often. Citation, the field's own measure of confidence, did not separate the studies that held from those that shrank.

I would not read the sixteen percent as the answer to the title question. It is a sample of the most visible clinical research in the best journals, and it is a floor for the contradicted findings and not a ceiling for the false ones, because twenty-four percent had simply never been tested again. The authors of later work have pointed this out, and so did the paper itself. But it does say something that the title cannot, which is that the claim is falsifiable and that the highly cited sample was nowhere near a majority contradicted.

A second count comes from the people who named a phenomenon. Vinay Prasad, Adam Cifu and ten colleagues published "A decade of reversal" in Mayo Clinic Proceedings in 2013. They reviewed every original article, two thousand and forty-four of them, published in one high-impact journal over ten years, 2001 to 2010. Of those, about thirteen hundred concerned a medical practice, and just over a quarter tested an established practice. The authors classified each such trial by what it found. Of those established practices, forty percent, or one hundred and forty-six trials, found that the existing practice was no better than a lesser therapy. They called these medical reversals. A hundred and thirty-eight, thirty-eight percent, reaffirmed the practice. The rest were inconclusive.

The number needs care, because a reversal in their sense is not the same as a false positive in Ioannidis's sense. A practice can be reversed because the early evidence was wrong, or because a better comparison was made, or because background care improved. Their claim is more modest, and it is the claim the show's listener can use. When an established practice is finally put through a rigorous test, it fails to beat the lesser alternative about as often as it passes. The authors also say the set is not a sample of all medicine; the practices that get tested are the ones somebody doubted. Prasad and Cifu went on to expand the argument in a 2015 book, Ending Medical Reversal. I have not held the book, and what I know of it, that it argues for demanding better evidence before adoption because reversal harms patients who received the practice, comes from its title and its reception. That is the movement's own recommendation, and it is a fair one.

Put the two counts next to Ioannidis's table and the picture is less dramatic than the title and more uncomfortable than a complacent reading. Roughly a third of the visible, much-cited claims were contradicted or came back smaller, depending on how generously the shrinking counts, and about forty percent of the established practices that were tested failed the test. Neither count says most published findings are false. Both say the share is large enough that a physician who treats a single significant paper as an answer is making a bet with poor odds.

The model said so on the page. The question that follows is whether its proof is a proof.

## Chapter Four: The case against the proof

The first serious reply came within two years, in the same journal, from two statisticians with long records on the subject. Steven Goodman and Sander Greenland published "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False: Problems in the Analysis" in 2007. They begin by agreeing with a lot. They say the probability of a hypothesis depends on much more than the p-value, which the medical literature had said for at least four decades. They agree there are more false claims than many would suspect, from poor design, misinterpreted p-values and perhaps manipulation. And then they say the mathematical argument underlying the proof has a degree of circularity.

Their charge has two parts that a listener can follow without algebra. The first is about what the model counts as evidence. Ioannidis calculates the evidential effect of a result only as a verdict, significant or not, and not as the p-value actually observed. A study with a p-value of 0.001 and a study with one of 0.049 are both just "significant." Information is discarded, and the discarded information is the strongest evidence. The bias term is the other, and even at a modest level of ten percent it sharply diminishes the evidential value of any study. Together, they say, these assumptions mean that no study type can produce more than modest evidence against the null hypothesis. That is why, in the paper's table, the only designs that get above fifty percent are the large trials and the meta-analyses of trials, and those are the ones that were given a prior of fifty percent or more to begin with. The model's output reflects what it was fed. So the model, they say, cannot be a proof that most published claims are untrue. It amounts to a claim that no study or combination of studies could ever supply convincing evidence.

The hot-field charge is aimed at the corollary about crowded fields. Goodman and Greenland say the proof offered shows merely that more studies on a subject means a larger absolute number of false positives and false negatives. It does not show that the proportion of false claims rises as studies multiply, which is what the paper's graphs and text claim. I follow that one, and I think it is correct as a matter of the arithmetic, though the intuition that a crowded field has more people chasing significance is another matter, and the Proteus pattern in molecular genetics is an empirical observation, not a theorem.

They are also fair to the paper's use. They write that it offers useful guidance in a number of areas, calling attention to the importance of avoiding all forms of bias, of obtaining more empirical research on the prevalence of those biases, and of studying the determinants of prior odds. What they reject is the word proof and the claim about hot fields. I have the short 2007 reply and not the longer technical paper it refers to, nor Ioannidis's answer to it, which I know exists from the list of linked responses and not from its text, so I cannot say how he met the circularity charge.

A second reply came with data. Leah Jager and Jeffrey Leek, in Biostatistics in 2013, did not argue with the model. They went and estimated. They collected the p-values reported in the abstracts of every paper published between 2000 and 2010 in The Lancet, JAMA, the New England Journal of Medicine, the BMJ and the American Journal of Epidemiology. There were seventy-seven thousand four hundred and thirty papers, of which fifty-three hundred and twenty-two reported p-values in the abstract. They adapted a method from genomics to estimate the fraction of the reported results that were false discoveries. Their estimate is fourteen percent, with a standard deviation of one, "contrary to previous claims," and they found no significant rise over the decade. Their conclusion is that the medical literature remains a reliable record of scientific progress.

I do not take fourteen percent as the answer either, and I can say why in terms of the model's own objections. The method reads p-values from abstracts, which are the places where authors put the results they want read. It estimates the proportion of false positives among the p-values that were reported, and the model's problem is the ones that never were: the analyses run and dropped, the outcomes switched, the subgroup that was found after the primary came up empty. A procedure that begins from what was reported can't see the selection that happened before reporting. That is my reasoning, not a quote from anyone, and a reader who knows the exchange of discussion papers that the Jager and Leek paper provoked, which I know exist by title and have not read, may find the objection made better or answered. What I can say is that the two papers have the same limit from opposite sides. Ioannidis could not observe u, so he assumed ten percent. Jager and Leek could not see unreported analyses, so they assumed none.

The position I would hold is to separate three claims that the title runs together. That a significant result's meaning depends on pre-study odds is arithmetic, and no critic disputes it. That the odds in much of biomedicine are low enough, and u high enough, that falsehoods are common is an empirical claim, supported by the reversal counts and disputed in size. That falsehoods are the majority everywhere is a rhetorical claim, resting on assumptions chosen to produce it, and Goodman and Greenland are right that the model's proof is weaker than its tone.

The title was effective because it made the first claim feel like the third. Did anything change in the field in response to the first?

## Chapter Five: A natural experiment in trial registration

One test of what the fixes do comes from a paper that was not trying to answer Ioannidis. Robert Kaplan and Veronica Irvin, both then at the US National Institutes of Health, published "Likelihood of Null Effects of Large NHLBI Clinical Trials Has Increased over Time" in PLoS ONE in 2015. They collected every large randomised drug or supplement trial for cardiovascular outcomes funded by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute between 1970 and 2012. Large meant direct costs above half a million dollars a year. They ended with fifty-five trials, thirty published before the year 2000 and twenty-five from 2000 on.

Before 2000, seventeen of the thirty, fifty-seven percent, reported a significant benefit on the primary outcome. After 2000, two of the twenty-five did, eight percent. The relative risks after 2000 also clustered much more tightly around one. And the outcomes pre-2000 were, as one reads down the paper's tables, often things like blood pressure, infarct size, regression of plaque on an angiogram, the degree of narrowing in an artery. After 2000 they were death and cardiovascular events.

The authors tested explanations. They checked whether the later trials used active comparators instead of placebo, which makes benefit harder to show; they did not differ much. They checked industry co-sponsorship; it was unrelated to finding a benefit. Publication bias could not explain it, because the large trials were mostly published, eighty-eight percent of them in their set, and a file drawer would have inflated the later positive rate, not cut it. The factor most strongly associated with the shift was pre-registration. Trials registered at ClinicalTrials.gov before publication, an option that grew after the FDA Modernization Act of 1997 and became a condition of publication at many major journals in the years after 2001, were far more often null.

Their reading is that before registration investigators could change the significance level or the direction of the hypothesis after the fact, or build a composite endpoint that favoured their hypothesis, and that registration essentially eliminated that option. This is Ioannidis's flexibility corollary shown in a real dataset, a field that closed the door on u and watched its positive rate fall by about forty-nine points.

The authors do not claim more than they have. They write that they cannot say the trend toward null results is caused by preregistration, that the number of trials is small, and that many things changed around the year 2000. They list rivals, including improved background cardiovascular care, which makes any new treatment's incremental benefit harder to show, and NHLBI's closer involvement, since nearly eighty percent of the later trials ran through cooperative agreements with the institute. They also say their results may not generalise to industry-sponsored trials. I would add one that the paper does not examine as far as I read: the move away from surrogate outcomes would by itself lower the positive rate, since a drug can lower blood pressure far more often than it lowers deaths. It is hard to separate that from registration, because registration is what forced the primary outcome to be named in advance. The natural experiment cannot tell the two apart, and I suspect the real answer is that they are one mechanism, a primary outcome fixed before the data exist.

The result does two things for Ioannidis, and leaves a third undone. It rescues his corollary on flexibility. It also suggests that the early positive rate in this field was inflated by something, and fifty-seven percent positive in trials that cost millions a year is hard to read as the track record of a field with high pre-study odds. But the drop to eight percent doesn't mean the early trials were wrong, and it can't be used to estimate the false share. It shows only that when the room to choose was removed, most of the apparent benefit went with it.

That gives a way to sort the fixes by what each one touches.

## Chapter Six: What each fix fixes

Look at the model's four levers: R, the pre-study odds; power; the threshold; and u, the bias. Each remedy that the field adopted acts on one of them, and almost none acts on R.

Trial registration, which asks investigators to record the question, the design and the primary outcome in a public registry before enrolment, attacks u directly. Once the plan is public, a dropped outcome shows, and so does a trial that was never published. Reporting standards such as CONSORT, introduced in 1996 and expanded in 2001, require a trial's flow of participants and outcomes to be described in a fixed way, which makes selective reporting easier to detect, and the SPIRIT checklist plays the same role for the protocol, as I understand its purpose, though I have not held its text. A prespecified statistical analysis plan, written and dated before the data are unblinded, is registration extended to the analysis itself: the model, the handling of missing data, the order in which hypotheses are tested, the subgroups allowed. Each of these narrows the space of defensible analyses, the very quantity the bias term measures. The hormone therapy and astrological subgroup episodes made the case for the plan from the other direction, with cohorts that started the clock wrong and subgroups that were believed because they were found.

Ioannidis himself lists, near the end of his essay, what he thinks helps. Larger studies and low-bias meta-analyses raise power, and he adds that large studies should be aimed at questions where the pre-study probability is already high, and at major concepts and not narrow claims, so that a negative result can refute a whole field. Attention to the totality of evidence, and not the single team's significant finding. Upfront registration of randomised trials, with some form of registration or networking of data collections for hypothesis-generating work, which he admits would be harder. And an effort to estimate R: before an experiment, investigators should consider what chance they believe there is that they are testing a true relationship. He suspects that several established classics will fail large, low-bias replication.

Consider what is on his list and what is not. Registration, standards and plans are about u. Large studies are about power. Estimating R is about the first quantity, and it is the one the infrastructure leaves alone. A registry can show that a trial of a long shot was properly run. It cannot show that the shot was long. So the choice of which questions get the large trial stays with funders and committees, and the sponsors' choices enter at the level of R. A company can enrol every trial in the registry and still run only the ones it expects to win. Each registered result is honest, and the field's apparent R is set by what sponsors chose to test, so what the record shows is how well the bets were chosen.

Last episode ended on the observation that the sponsors learned the checklist at least as fast as the people who read their studies, and that was Greenhalgh's account of how a measure used to certify quality becomes a target. The registry invites the same drift one step along. It closed the doors it knew about. An outcome can be listed and then reported as a secondary; a trial can go on the registry late; a composite can be defined so loosely that anything fits. The empirical literature on selective outcome reporting in trials, which Ioannidis himself cites, says it remains a common problem even among randomised trials, and he writes that simply abolishing selective publication would not make it go away. I have the line from the 2005 essay and not the studies behind it. A fix that is checked only by the people it constrains drifts, and the audit has to come from someone who did not write the plan.

A case against the fixes themselves belongs here too, and it is the one Kaplan and Irvin's data make easiest. If registration cuts the positive rate from fifty-seven to eight percent, a funder might conclude that it has made trials worse, since fewer of them succeed. That would confuse the honest rate with the useful one. A system in which two trials in twenty-five find benefit is expensive to run and informative to read; the failures are information, and the same paper notes that among two hundred and twenty-one agents tested for Alzheimer's disease in placebo-controlled trials registered at the registry, none had identified a benefit on its declared primary outcome. The cost shows up elsewhere: a field that has learned that most of its ideas fail will fund fewer of them, and the pre-study odds R that Ioannidis wants estimated are themselves partly a product of what the field is willing to try.

So the fixes are real, they work mainly on bias and power, and they invite a particular kind of decay. What does a person who writes the plan do with that?

## Chapter Seven: Two numbers before the data

The working use of the essay is smaller than its title. It is a habit of two numbers fixed before a result is looked at, one written down and one built into a dated plan.

The first number is R, or rather the plain version of it. Before running or reading a study, write down how many interventions of this kind, in this setting, for this outcome, have turned out to work when properly tested. Not whether this one is plausible, since plausibility is cheap, but the track record of the class. One in ten is a common answer, and one in three is generous. Then take the study's power, which the paper reports or the sample size lets you compute, and do the arithmetic from Chapter One. Suppose, and the numbers are made up for the arithmetic, that a class of programme works one time in ten, and a trial has eighty percent power. Of a thousand such programmes, a hundred are real and the trial finds eighty. Of the nine hundred that do nothing, forty-five cross the threshold by chance. So a significant result comes from a real programme eighty times in a hundred and twenty-five, about sixty-four percent. Drop the power to forty percent, which is not rare for the secondary outcomes of a trial built for a different primary, and the same significant result is real forty times in eighty-five, under half.

What the arithmetic shows is the range. If the answer is the same under any reasonable guess of R, the study settles the question. If it flips between a guess of one in ten and one in three, the study is one input and the decision needs a second trial. That is a use for the model that the circularity charge doesn't touch, because it doesn't claim a proof; it asks how much the conclusion depends on an assumption the reader can state.

The second number is u, and the fix is to shrink it by construction. A date-stamped analysis plan names the primary outcome, the model, the handling of missing data and the single subgroup question, if there is one, before anyone sees an outcome by arm. It also lists what the plan will not be used for. A trialist who reads the plan back after unblinding, and finds that the paper reports a different primary outcome, a different population, or a subgroup the plan did not name, has measured u in their own work. The plan makes u visible, and its value is that the reviewer can count the departures.

There is a limit on that, set by the Kaplan and Irvin paper, and it applies to anyone with a small team and a long distance between the analyst and the field. Registration closes the door the registrant knows about. The most likely failure of a careful plan is not a switched primary outcome. It is a plan so loose that every outcome fits, or one revised after the data without a date on the revision. The protection is a version history that someone other than the analyst can read, and a habit of reporting departures as departures, in the paper, in a sentence, where the reader will find them.

The title overreaches as a proof. What survives is that a finding cannot be read without knowing what was asked, how many were asked, and what was allowed to change. The registries and the plans are the profession's answer to the last two. The first, what was asked, is the part nobody has built a registry for, the odds that the thing tested was true to begin with.
