Kelly Johnson handed a drawing back across a desk and said it would be too draggy. The engine inlet that Ben Rich had designed, he said, was about twenty percent too big. Then he was gone. Rich spent the rest of the day refiguring it, and found that the inlet was eighteen percent off.

Rich was twenty-nine, a thermodynamicist at eighty-seven dollars a week. Johnson was the chief engineer, and he had not run the numbers. He had looked at a piece of paper for a few seconds and been wrong by two points. Rich keeps coming back to that scene across the next four decades: here was a company with a man in it who could do in his head what a department took a day to check. The question for this episode is what happens to an organization built around such a man, and in particular which parts of it were the man and which were the arrangement, because only the arrangement can be taught.

The book is Skunk Works, written by Rich with a professional writer named Leo Janos and published in 1994. I held the full text, so the scenes and figures below are Rich's, from the page. It is a memoir by the successor, about the founder and about himself, and I did not read reviews of it, so where a claim is his alone I will say so.

## Chapter One: A Tent Next to a Plastics Factory

The Skunk Works began in 1943 as a rescue. German jet fighters had appeared over Europe, the American services had nothing comparable, and the War Department asked Lockheed's thirty-three-year-old chief engineer for a jet prototype in one hundred and eighty days. Johnson had already designed the P-38. He borrowed twenty-three design engineers and about thirty shop mechanics from the main plant. All the floor space in the complex was taken by wartime production, so he rented a circus tent and pitched it beside a plastics factory whose smell kept visitors away.

The name came from a comic strip, in which Al Capp's Li'l Abner had an outdoor still called the skonk works. An engineer, joking about the smell, answered the telephone with those words, and Johnson, overhearing, fired him on the spot. The engineer came to work the next day, as Rich says people did, since Johnson fired them all the time without meaning it.

The prototype flew in one hundred and forty-three days, thirty-seven ahead of schedule. Rich says Lockheed went on to build nearly nine thousand of them, a figure I suspect counts the later trainer version. That is the founding story, and it holds most of the method already: a customer who got out of the way, a couple of officers allowed to look through the tent flaps, people borrowed from the main plant for the duration and sent back after, and a cost so small that Lockheed's management let Johnson keep the little operation going as long as it stayed on a shoestring and did not distract him from his day job.

The sequel is less often told, and Rich tells it. Johnson's next two development projects were clunkers: a small transport called the Saturn that nobody needed once airlines could buy surplus DC-3s, and a vertical-takeoff fighter whose pilot, at the moment of landing, had to look straight up at the sky. The shop survived both because it was cheap and because the chief executive, Robert Gross, regarded Johnson as a man who walked on water. Gross had bought Lockheed out of bankruptcy in 1932 for forty thousand dollars, and had hired a twenty-three-year-old Michigan student who told the faculty that the company's new twin-engine transport would be unstable with one engine out. The student was right, and the twin tail that fixed it became the Lockheed signature.

So before any rule is written down, there is a protector. Johnson spent his days as chief engineer of the main plant, then at about two in the afternoon drove half a mile to spend the last two hours at the secret one. He lived inside the bureaucracy and drew his people out of it. What that arrangement could do once someone with a real need showed up is the next chapter.

## Chapter Two: Picture Postcards for Ike

In December 1954 Rich was lent to Johnson for a few weeks to solve an inlet problem, and stayed thirty-six years. On the first day Johnson's assistant gave him a mimeographed sheet, which Rich calls the riot act. It had ten rules on it, and the ones he quotes are plain to the point of childishness. There shall be one object, which is to get a good airplane built on time. Engineers shall work within a stone's throw of the airplane. Anyone who foresees a delay shall report it in writing to Johnson. Parts from stock shall be used even at the cost of added weight, because special parts invite delay.

Then Johnson told him what the airplane was. It would fly at least fifteen thousand feet above any Russian fighter or missile, cross the Soviet Union, and send photographs back to Eisenhower. Johnson's phrase for them, as Rich recalls it, was postcards. Edwin Land, who had designed the Polaroid camera, was designing the cameras, and Eastman Kodak was developing a thin film in rolls of thirty-six hundred feet. Johnson had promised a prototype in six months, which meant six-day weeks at the least. Rich went home that night and told his wife it was so secret there were not even secretaries or janitors. She asked whether he was working on the bomb.

I will follow this airplane to the end.

The U-2 was not an Air Force project. A structural designer named Ed Baldwin told Rich so at lunch: the whole thing ran under the Central Intelligence Agency. The pilots were hired by the agency from the Air Force and carried on Lockheed's books, as employees of a high-altitude weather study. And the money arrived in a way Rich calls the wildest government payout in history. Using secret contingency funds, the agency wrote personal checks to Johnson, more than a million dollars in start-up costs, and mailed them to his home in Encino. He banked them through a company called C and J Engineering, the letters standing for Clarence Johnson, and the drawings carried that logo instead of Lockheed's. Parts came to a mail drop at Sunland. The local postmaster, curious about crates addressed to a company that was not in the phone book, had an inspector follow the unmarked van back to Burbank, where security caught him and had him signing secrecy forms until, Rich says, he pleaded writer's cramp.

Count who could say yes. Eisenhower approved the operational plan personally, Allen Dulles was told to crack the whip, and Richard Bissell, a former Yale economist who ran the program for the agency, was the man Johnson actually dealt with. Rich saw him only as a tall figure in a loud checked jacket, known inside the plant as Mr. B. Johnson had told Rich on day one that he could tell no one, wife and priest included. A program with that few decision points does not have meetings in which each person on a list protects himself. It has a man who can sign.

The airplane itself shows what a single number can force. Each pound of weight cost a foot of altitude, so the eighty-foot wings weighed about four pounds a square foot, a third of what jet wings normally did. The landing gear weighed two hundred pounds, a bicycle arrangement borrowed from sailplanes, with jettisonable wheels under the wings for takeoff. The tail was fastened to the fuselage with three bolts of five-eighths of an inch. Rich watched a worker bang a toolbox against the skin and leave a four-inch dent, and wondered whether the thing was too fragile to fly. The engine, a Pratt and Whitney J57 modified by hand, would produce only seven percent of its sea-level thrust at the altitude where the airplane had to live. Ordinary jet fuel would freeze or boil off up there, so Johnson went to Jimmy Doolittle, who sat on the Shell board, and Shell built a special low-vapor kerosene. Its chemistry resembled an insect spray called Flit, and Rich says the fuel run caused a national shortage of bug spray.

The schedule came from borrowing. From the cockpit forward the U-2 was the front half of the F-104 Starfighter, so Johnson used the F-104 tooling and saved months. The test base in the Nevada desert, found by the chief test pilot Tony LeVier, who dropped a shot-put ball on a dry lake bed to check that it was as hard as a table, was built for eight hundred thousand dollars. The first airplane was finished on the fifteenth of July, 1955, seven months after Rich arrived. Johnson inspected it personally and found thirty items to improve. On its first taxi test, on the second of August, LeVier was told to accelerate to seventy knots and brake, and found that the airplane had lifted off by itself because it was so light. He knew only when he hit the ground, blew both tires, and set the brakes on fire under the fuel lines.

Each airplane was to cost the taxpayer about a million dollars, development included, a figure Rich calls the greatest procurement bargain ever and which comes from a man who was in the room. The schedule stands without him. In May 1960 one of the airplanes was shot down over the Soviet Union with Francis Gary Powers in it, and the world learned it existed.

So the method has been demonstrated once, and it had four ingredients: a man with authority and a gift, a customer whose money bypassed the usual channels, a team small enough to sit with their desks touching, and a deadline nobody could renegotiate. Which of the four can a written rule capture? That is the next question.

## Chapter Three: The Fourteen Rules

Rich prints fourteen rules, which he says Johnson wrote one night half in the bag, and which he says he and his people lived and died by. The sheet he was handed in 1954 had ten, so the fourteen presumably grew from it. In order they sound like a list of grievances against procurement. Read for what they are for, they sort into a few jobs.

One job is concentrating the decision. The program manager gets practically complete control, with authority to decide technical, financial and operational questions quickly. The project offices on both sides are strong and small. The contractor, not the government, takes more than the usual responsibility for getting good bids from vendors, because commercial bidding is often better than military bidding, and has the authority to test the finished product in flight. Johnson wants the person with the knowledge to hold the pen as well.

A second job is cutting paper without cutting records. The drawing release system is to be very simple, so changes are fast after a failure. Reports are few, though important work is recorded thoroughly. There is a monthly cost review that covers not only spending to date but the projected cost to the end of the program, with the instruction not to run the books ninety days late and not to surprise the customer with an overrun. Inspection is pushed back to the vendors, so nobody inspects the same thing twice.

A third job is keeping the customer in the loop on trust and nothing else. Specifications are agreed before the contract. Funding must arrive on time so the contractor is not running to the bank to carry the government. There must be absolute trust between the military project office and the contractor, with close daily liaison that keeps correspondence to a minimum.

The last rules are about people. Outsiders are strictly controlled. The number of people connected to the project is held to a bare few, and Johnson's word for how hard to hold it is vicious. And because few people will be used, good performance must be rewarded by pay that is not based on the number of people supervised. Rich saw what that meant in practice. In the main plant, Johnson told him once, raises followed the size of the staff you ran. In the Skunk Works the man who supervised the least got the raise, since he was doing more and carrying more responsibility.

Put that last rule beside Grove, whom the show covered earlier. Grove's manager measures himself by the output of the units he influences, and in most corporations the cheapest way to look important is to enlarge the unit. Johnson wrote the opposite incentive into the pay of the people who might have profited from the usual one. In Grove's terms, headcount is the measure that gets gamed, and the pay rule is the paired measure that catches the game.

Now the last two episodes, which were about a man whose power lived in documents, in the statute he drafted and the bond covenant that turned his authority into a contract. Johnson's rules are also documents, and many of them are instructions about which documents not to have. Rich says Johnson did not even stamp security classifications on paper, on the theory that a stamp tells a clerk which paper is interesting. That works while the covenant is among people who trust each other. Moses's covenant named a trustee who could sue on the bondholders' behalf, and when the trustee turned out to belong to the other side the covenant did not hold. The rule of absolute trust between customer and contractor has no trustee. The people in the room enforce it, and the rest of the book is about what happens when the room changes.

Before that, the rules had to survive contact with metal.

## Chapter Four: The Titanium Airplane

The successor to the U-2 was an airplane that would fly above Mach three. At that speed friction would heat the skin to several hundred degrees, and aluminum would not survive it. Stainless steel would, but a steel airplane would be too heavy to reach altitude. The alternative was titanium, which is as strong as steel at half the weight and which nobody had ever built an airframe from.

Rich proposed in one of the daily seven o'clock meetings in Johnson's office that they use a softer grade of titanium, which would lose strength at about five hundred and fifty degrees, and keep the skin cooler by painting the airplane black. A black surface radiates heat as well as it absorbs it. Johnson, fighting for every ounce, said no: a hundred pounds of paint would cost about eighty pounds of fuel. The next morning, Rich says, Johnson opened the meeting by telling him he had been right and Johnson wrong, and handed him a quarter. That is why the Blackbird is black.

The metal itself was another matter. Only one American company milled titanium, in sheets of wildly varying quality, and the Central Intelligence Agency, finding the supply too small, bought the base metal through third parties and dummy companies from one of the world's leading exporters, the Soviet Union. The Russians, Rich says, never had an inkling that they were supplying the airplane that would spy on them.

Then the first batches arrived brittle as glass. A worker dropped a piece off a bench and it shattered. The cause was bad heat treatment at the supplier, and the Skunk Works rejected ninety-five percent of what came. The other problems arrived one after another. A line drawn on a sheet with a felt pen etched through the metal, because the ink contained chlorine. Bolt heads dropped off hot bolts because the mechanics had used cadmium-plated wrenches. Spot welds failed after six or seven weeks, and the cause, after intensive sleuthing, was that the panels had been welded in July and August, when Burbank chlorinated its water to hold off algae. In the end the team made thirteen million separate parts, and by the middle of the 1960s it employed nearly eight thousand people and delivered one Blackbird a month.

One sentence of Rich's records his astonishment. For an outfit that detested red tape, he says, they now found themselves wallowing in procedure. They tested three of every ten batches of titanium for brittleness, kept records on millions of individual parts, and could trace any part to the original mill pour, so that when one went bad they could pull its siblings before trouble started. The rule about pushing inspection back to the vendors had assumed vendors who knew what they were making. When the material was new, the rule gave way to the material.

That is less flattering to the fourteen rules than Rich is, and truer to his own account. The rules were a default for work whose parts were understood. Where the physics was unknown, Johnson's team did what any team would do, which was inspect everything and write it down. What survived contact with titanium was the speed of the decision, and what bent was the paperwork. The method was never the absence of procedure. It was that the person who needed a procedure could have it that week, without a committee.

## Chapter Five: The Dog

One episode in the book tells the truth about this organization better than the successes do, and it is a cancellation.

In 1956 Johnson sent for Rich and asked what he knew about cryogenics. Rich said not much. Johnson told him to read up on liquid hydrogen, and when Rich pointed out that his engineering handbook called hydrogen a laboratory curiosity too dangerous to handle, Johnson said he did not care what the book said. The plan was a successor to the U-2 that burned liquid hydrogen and cruised well above Mach two. Rich traveled under an alias, talked to the people who ran the country's cryogenic labs, and was told by one of them that he had a screw loose. He then built a hydrogen liquefaction plant in a bunker that had been a wartime air raid shelter, as far from the main buildings as Johnson could put it, and began producing about two hundred gallons a day, more than anyone else in the country. The Air Force, which wanted a role in overflights after watching the agency have the U-2, allocated ninety-six million dollars. The project was called Suntan.

Then the numbers came in short. The airplane missed its lift-to-drag ratio by sixteen percent, and its engine used a quarter of a kerosene engine's fuel at Mach two and a half where the team had expected a fifth. The range fell a thousand miles under the promise. Rich said in the meeting that two thousand miles would take it from Los Angeles to Omaha, and that it would then need a hydrogen tank farm to land at. Johnson agreed. He picked up the telephone and called the Secretary himself.

"I'm afraid I'm building you a dog," he said, and recommended cancelling and sending the money back. It took several meetings in the Pentagon to persuade the Air Force. In the end the Skunk Works had spent about six million dollars and returned ninety.

Hold that for a moment. A contractor was handing back ninety million dollars of the government's money before the government had asked, with the program still on its books and its people still on the payroll. The monthly cost review that projects spending to completion is the mechanism that makes it possible, and the pay rule is the incentive that makes it bearable. A conventional prime has the opposite incentive on both counts. Its managers are rewarded for headcount and for the size of the program, and every month a project continues is a month of revenue. To cancel your own program you need an organization where nobody's pay depends on its continuing. Johnson's team had arranged that, by accident and by design.

Rich ends the story with a punch line, and here the book needs a caution. He says that not long after the cancellation the Soviets launched Sputnik, that its rocket engine was hydrogen-fueled and built by Pyotr Kapitsa, whom Rich says was released from the gulag to do it, so that, as he puts it, they had all guessed wrong. I checked this against nothing but my memory, and my memory says otherwise: Sputnik flew in October 1957 on a kerosene rocket, and Kapitsa was a physicist, not an engine builder. Rich's own text also dates his hydrogen explosions to the autumn of 1959, two years after Sputnik, so the story cannot run in the order he tells it. The cancellation I take as sound, because it comes with its own numbers and a named recipient. His punch line I would not repeat. A good anecdote in a memoir is a test of the memoir, and this one fails it.

## Chapter Six: The Bet

In 1972 a Northrop executive offered Rich a ten-thousand-dollar raise and the title of vice president for advanced projects, with a promise of a secret design shop of his own. Rich took the offer to Johnson, who did not believe a word of it. He would bet his ranch, Johnson said, that Northrop would never build one, because most managements distrust an operation they cannot see into, and executives are empire builders trained to cover themselves by taking votes. Control is what they want, and if such a shop runs right, control is exactly what they do not get. Then he matched the raise, made Rich vice president for advanced projects, and told him he was his choice to succeed him when he retired at sixty-five.

Succession is where the Collison shelf kept finding trouble. Licklider chose people who would make the same call he would. Johnson's choice was a man he would argue with, and the argument came in 1975.

Rich had taken over, and heard from a colleague that the Pentagon's research agency had invited five companies to compete on a stealthy airplane, with a million dollars each. Lockheed was not on the list, because nobody in the Pentagon knew what the Skunk Works had learned about radar from the Blackbird; those results belonged to the Central Intelligence Agency. Rich had Johnson, whom he says the agency treated almost as a deity, carry the request for release, and the results came at once. Then the head of the research agency offered to let Lockheed in for a pro forma contract of one dollar. Rich refused, since taking the dollar would have given the government the rights to the shapes and the materials. Lockheed carried its own risk, and close to a million dollars of company money went in.

The idea came from a young engineer named Denys Overholser, who had found, near the end of a forty-page paper by a Soviet physicist named Pyotr Ufimtsev, published in Moscow nine years earlier and only lately translated by the Air Force's foreign technology office, a way to calculate radar reflection from the surface and the edges of a wing and add the two. Rich's account is that the method worked only for flat plates, because in 1975 the computers could not handle curves. So the shape was dictated by the arithmetic: an airplane made entirely of flat facets, tilted so the radar energy scattered away from the sender. The senior engineers called it the Hopeless Diamond, and one called it a flying engagement ring. The head of aerodynamics suggested burning Overholser at the stake as a heretic.

Johnson was more direct. He kicked Rich in the backside, crumpled up the proposal and threw it at his feet, and told him the thing would never get off the ground. Later he called the claimed radar numbers theoretical claptrap, and bet Rich a quarter that the company's old D-21 drone, a manta-ray-shaped ramjet, had a lower radar signature than the diamond.

They put the two wooden models side by side in an electromagnetic chamber and cranked up the power. The date, Rich says, was the fourteenth of September, 1975. The diamond was a thousand times harder to see than the twelve-year-old drone. Johnson paid the quarter and said not to spend it until the thing flew. Then he sent for Overholser and cross-examined him until the young man was past well done, and learned something that surprised him: with flat surfaces, the radar energy returning to the sender does not depend on the size of the target. A small airplane, a bomber and an aircraft carrier with the same shape have the same signature. He said he would never have believed it. Rich had the impression he still didn't.

The founder, whose judgment had been right about inlets and weight and nearly everything else for forty years, was wrong about the largest design decision of the next twenty, and the organization absorbed it, because nothing in the rules made the founder's opinion a decision. A ten-foot model in a chamber was a decision. What the fourteen rules protected was the right to run the cheap test, and the habit of paying the bet. Rich's version of the victory flatters Rich, but the structure shows through it: a test that could be run in a week, a customer who read the results, and a founder who paid up.

The testing then moved outdoors. At a radar range in the Mojave a technician told Rich that his model must have fallen off its pole. A black bird had landed on top of it, and the radar was tracking the bird. In the spring of 1976, at White Sands, Overholser's account, which Rich prints, is that the Lockheed model came out about ten times less visible than Northrop's, so much so that the range's own mounting pole showed brighter than the airplane, and the two companies split the cost of a quieter one. In April Rich was told he had won.

Then a general rang him to say he was hearing the Skunk Works had passed in phony data. The rumor, Rich says, came from a civilian radar adviser with ties to the makers of electronic jammers, who would lose business if stealth worked. To answer it he invited an MIT professor, Lindsay Anderson, to Burbank, and Anderson arrived with a bag of ball bearings in his briefcase, from golf-ball size to an eighth of an inch. He had them glued to the nose of the model and zapped, and the model measured every one of them. A diamond darker than an eighth-of-an-inch ball bearing is a hard thing to accuse of cheating.

## Chapter Seven: What Trust Cost

The money for the two demonstration airplanes shows the method at its most informal. Johnson's estimate was twenty-eight million dollars, which Rich says came out almost exactly right. The Air Force had twenty million in discretionary funds for secret projects, the kind that bypass the appropriations procedures. Rich went to Lockheed's board for the other ten, told them the program could be worth two to three billion dollars in later sales, was accused by a director or two of hyperbole, and got the money.

The customer's side of it comes in Rich's printed recollection by General Larry Welch, who in 1976 was a brigadier general in charge of planning at the Tactical Air Command. Rich's presentation lasted twenty minutes, and when the generals asked each other what they thought, Welch said he had no idea whether the thing would fly, but that if Rich and the Skunk Works said they could deliver, they would be idiots not to go along. He gave a reason for the confidence. In the early 1970s, when the Air Force was arguing about what the future F-15 would cost, with estimates inside the service running from three and a half to eight and a half million dollars, he flew to Burbank, and Johnson and Rich worked out a figure on a sheet of paper over a single ounce of whiskey. They said seven million. The airplane arrived at six million eight hundred thousand in 1971 dollars.

A track record is a balance a customer draws down in trust. It cannot be photocopied or audited, and here the fourteen rules start to look like a description of an exceptional supplier rather than a recipe for producing one.

Then the program became too large to stay informal. Later in 1976 the Air Force classified Have Blue Top Secret, Special Access Required, a level Rich says was reserved for things like the Manhattan Project. He was pleased. Johnson told him to cancel the whole project or get it reclassified, because the security would raise his costs by a quarter and eat him alive.

The security, in Rich's list, meant every piece of paper stamped and indexed, full field investigations of everyone with access at about nine months each, and a two-man rule under which no machinist could be left alone with a blueprint, so that if one went to the toilet the other had to lock it away. The flight test head designed a coffee mug with the airplane's nose peeking from a cloud, and security classified the mug. Rich, as the airplane's official custodian, was personally responsible for collecting every piece of it if it crashed. It was, he says, like working at KGB headquarters.

And then the customer arrived to look. Before the first flight, an independent engineering review team of civil servants from Wright Field came to Burbank. They had nothing but praise, and Rich was furious. Never before in the history of the Skunk Works had the customer so closely supervised the work. Then he writes that all of them sensed the old independence was doomed to become a nostalgic memory, like a dime cup of coffee.

Have Blue was due to fly on the first of December, 1977, and the team finished it nearly three weeks early. Three days before the first flight the tail overheated in engine runs, and a shop supervisor cut up a six-foot steel tool cabinet to make a heat shield. Rich notes that most of their cherished rules were now in tatters, and that a colleague teased him that he made the date only because the cream of his management was building the airplane by hand.

## Chapter Eight: What Scale Did

The production version, the F-117A, is where the book becomes most honest about what had gone wrong, and a single accident carries it.

The first production airplane was built in 1981, and early in 1982 the first Air Force pilot, a major named Al Whitley, arrived at the plant to watch his airplane being made. The first flight tests found the V-shaped tails fifteen percent too small, and a tail fluttered off in flight. Looking back, Rich was amazed there had not been more major problems, because most of the thousand production workers were starting from scratch at the Skunk Works, and the security rules cost them efficiency everywhere.

They did well on one measure. Building two airplanes every three months, they reported a learning curve of seventy-eight percent, better than other manufacturers reported building twenty-five a month. Rich credits training, inspection, supervision and motivation.

Then, on the twentieth of April, 1982, on the acceptance flight of Whitley's airplane, the test pilot Bob Riedenauer took off and, thirty feet above the runway, found his pitch and yaw controls reversed. The night before, a servomechanism had been moved from one equipment bay to another and rewired, and the wiring had been crossed. He flipped over backward and crashed at the edge of the lake bed, trapped in the cockpit, with leg injuries that kept him in hospital for seven months. Rich was angry that the inspection process had not caught it. The Air Force accident board noted that new safeguards were in place within forty-eight hours. Whitley said that could have been him.

Rich's diagnosis comes a few pages later, in an aside about safety regulators. In the early days the Skunk Works fueled airplanes inside the assembly area, which was strictly forbidden, to find leaks without rolling a secret airplane into daylight, and the practice held up, he says, on the expertise of the crews. It is the logic behind the Challenger launch decision, which the show covered under Vaughan: a deviation is tolerated because the staff are good, and it stays tolerated after the staff change. As the company grew, Rich writes, the skill level decreased and sloppiness became a serious problem, with careless workers leaving objects inside airplanes. By 1985 the Skunk Works workforce, across stealth and other secret projects, had reached seventy-five hundred.

So the method scaled in dollars and failed to scale in judgment, which had been spread by being demonstrated in front of people. Johnson's inlet check took a few seconds and corrected a thousand mistakes that never got drawn. At a thousand workers the check had to be a procedure, the procedure needed a reviewer, and the reviewer had not been in the room.

Then came the bomber. Rich says Lockheed's quoted price was two hundred million dollars an airplane and that in October 1981 the Air Force gave the contract to Northrop on technical merit. He challenged the ruling, and lost. His explanation is that Lockheed had the better team and Northrop the greater need, after a costly lightweight fighter, and I cannot test that from here. Welch's version of the pattern is in the closing chapter: the Blackbird took two Air Force officials, three at the Pentagon and four key members of Congress to get going, the stealth fighter eight on the Hill, and the B-2 every bureaucratic hoop.

## Chapter Nine: The Case Against

The strongest argument against reading this book as a method is that its subject was not a method. It was an exceptional supplier, serving a customer with a blank check and a reason not to ask questions.

Start with the money. The U-2 was financed by contingency funds that bypassed congressional appropriations; Rich says so in plain words, and says the Air Force's secret-project money did the same for Have Blue. The checks mailed to Johnson's house were possible because nobody outside a handful of people was allowed to know what they were for. The insulation that made the team fast was bought with the removal of the ordinary scrutiny a democracy applies to spending. That may have been a fair trade in a year when, by Rich's account, a poll found more than half of American adults thinking nuclear war a likelier death than old age. It is not a trade to export to a medical school.

Then the customer. In every successful instance the buyer was effectively one person with authority and an urgent need: Eisenhower through Bissell, then William Perry at the Pentagon, who met Welch monthly and, as Welch put it, never wasted time on useless meetings. Suppose a customer has thirty stakeholders, a number I am making up. The rule giving the program manager complete control then asks for something the customer cannot grant. When it was granted, Rich's book makes clear, it was granted because the buyer was frightened, and fright is not a permanent condition.

Then survivorship. Rich says there are around fifty-five operations like this across industry, which he finds few, and he offers Ford's Team Mustang, a secret group that built the 1994 Mustang in three years for seven hundred million dollars, twenty-five percent less time and thirty percent less money than comparable programs, on his figures only. His own shop did not always win, and the book says so: the Saturn, the vertical riser, Suntan, a stealthy submarine that a Navy captain waved away, and a B-2 that went to someone else. Part of its credibility is that the failures are in it.

And Rich's own ceiling. In his closing chapter he says a Skunk Works can afford to be less profitable than the rest of a corporation only if its projects are not financial back-breakers and are limited to about fifty units. It is a prototype shop by his own definition. When the Skunk Works's F-22 won its competition, he writes, all five companies in the contest lost money, because the government cut the number of airplanes it would buy and the development cost was spread over fewer of them. His conclusion is that stockholders would have done better putting the money in certificates of deposit. The admission says the method works up to the point where the product has to be built in quantity, and then the qualities that made the shop fast become the qualities that make the airplane expensive.

Against all this the defense is narrower than Rich's, and it is mine rather than his. A good deal of what the book describes can be done without the blank check, the single buyer or the secrecy. The cancellation, the pay rule, the test before the argument, and the stone's throw between the person who draws and the person who builds cost nothing but the willingness to be embarrassed. They are rules about information and incentive, and an organization can have them while spending its own money in public. What it cannot have is the speed those rules produced when joined with a customer who could say yes in one conversation. The fair claim is that the book lists a few cheap habits and one expensive condition, and mostly takes credit for the habits.

## Chapter Ten: Dose

As reading, the book is worth the time for its first half and the titanium chapters, not the whole of it. Rich is good company, and the U-2 section moves like a thriller, but a good portion of the stealth chapters is a corporate victory lap, and several anecdotes, as the Sputnik one showed, should not be repeated without checking. The fourteen rules are printed in full in the early chapters, and they alone justify the price. Read them with the cancellation next to them.

The application comes from the cancellation. Before a project starts, whoever runs it can write down the number at which the funder gets a call and the money goes back, and the name of the person who takes the call. Johnson's version was a sixteen percent miss on lift-to-drag and a thousand miles of range, spoken in one call to the Secretary of the Air Force. A trialist asked to commit in advance to an interim futility threshold, or a fellowship director who has to decide in the second year whether a project is dead, faces the same decision, and the difficulty is not statistical. Nobody around the project has a reason to say the number out loud. Put it in the protocol, and make sure nobody's promotion depends on the project continuing past it.
