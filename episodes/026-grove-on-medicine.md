## Chapter One: A Number of Five

In the autumn of nineteen ninety-four, a few weeks before the Pentium became a story, Andy Grove had a routine blood test that came back with a prostate-specific antigen of five, against a normal range that stopped at four. He did what most fifty-eight-year-old men do with a number like that, which is nothing, for a while. Then, on a sabbatical the following spring, he had it measured again at two laboratories and got six point zero and six point one, and the arithmetic of a rising number did what the first number had not. A biopsy found cancer. The urologist recommended surgery, which is what urologists recommend, and Grove, who had spent thirty years running an organisation on the principle that a decision belongs to the person closest to the data, went to find the data.

He wrote up what he found in Fortune in May nineteen ninety-six, on the cover, under the title Taking on Prostate Cancer, and the article is the document I want to start from, because it shows the method of the two management books applied to a body, and because the method's strengths and its blind spot are both visible in it. He downloaded papers from a CompuServe forum. He read Thomas Stamey's work from Stanford on what PSA levels meant. He found the tables from Johns Hopkins that related what a surgeon found at operation to what happened afterwards, and he worked out that for a man with his PSA and his tumour grade the recurrence rate after surgery was about fifteen percent if the cancer was confined to the gland and about sixty percent if it had escaped it. He then did something that, he says, no one he consulted had done, which was to take the published outcome series for surgery, for external radiation, and for radioactive seeds, and cross-plot them against the patients' starting PSA, so that he was comparing treatments in men who started in the same place rather than in the mixed populations each specialty happened to treat. On that comparison the radiation results looked as good as surgery's, with fewer of the complications the surgeons' papers understated and the patients he interviewed did not. He consulted over fifteen physicians. The three eminent surgeons among them were, in his word, ferociously opposed to radiation. The radiation oncologist who offered the newest method, a high-dose-rate implant through sixteen needles combined with external beam, was asked what he would do himself and said he would probably have surgery. Grove went to Seattle and had the implant and twenty-eight mornings of external radiation, missed three days of work, and lived another twenty years, and died of something else.

A clinician hearing that story hears two things at once, and the shelf should hold both. The first is that a patient with a spreadsheet had found something the specialties had not, which is that nobody was comparing across them on matched patients, and that every specialist's recommendation was a recommendation for the thing that specialist did. Grove's line for that, which he says with no malice, is that urologists are surgeons and surgery is what they know. The second thing, which Grove half-saw and did not fully weigh, is that the cross-plot he built could not tell him what he needed to know, because the men who had been sent to radiation in those years were different from the men sent to surgery in ways that a starting PSA did not capture, and a treatment that looks as good in a sicker population may be better or may be worse. Epidemiologists call that confounding by indication, and it has ended careers. He noticed the selection problem, wrote a sentence about it, and went on to the balance sheet, which was drawn up, characteristically, on a bicycle trip. A Fortune reporter fact-checking the piece found an arithmetic error in the chart he had proudly built himself, which he acknowledged, and which I mention because it is the kind of detail Grove would have wanted kept.

The question this episode asks is what happens when the best-documented manager of his generation, having run his method on his own cancer and found it worked well enough, turns it on medicine as a whole. He did that three times over the following fifteen years, as an essayist, as a funder, and as a critic from the platform, and the field wrote back each time. The argument between them is, I think, the most useful thing Grove left for a person who does medical research for a living, and it is more useful than either side would admit, because both were right about different layers of the same building.

## Chapter Two: Knowledge Turns

In July two thousand and five the Journal of the American Medical Association published a three-page essay by Grove called Efficiency in the Health Care Industries: A View From the Outside. It is a curious document to find in that journal, and the journal knew it; the piece was invited, and it has the tone of a guest who has been asked to be frank and has decided to take the invitation literally.

The argument is built on a comparison and a number. In the forty years since he had joined Fairchild, the number of transistors that could be put on a chip had doubled every year or two, the pattern that his colleague Gordon Moore had named. In the same forty years, the war on cancer, declared by Nixon in nineteen seventy-one, had produced what Grove, looking at the mortality curves, called slow progress. He did not think the people were the difference. He said in so many words that both industries were populated by dedicated and well-trained people working from science. He thought the difference was the rate at which each industry turned knowledge over, and he gave the rate a name, knowledge turns, by analogy with inventory turns in a warehouse: the time it takes for an experiment to produce a result that generates the next experiment. In the semiconductor business a knowledge turn was measured in weeks, because you could build the thing, test it, and build the next one. In medicine, he argued, it was measured in years, because the test was a clinical trial, and the trial took years, and the result, when it came, was often a single number that told you the drug had not worked without telling you why.

From that he drew three recommendations, and they were specific, which is why the essay is still cited. The first was biomarkers: he wanted the National Institutes of Health to put something like a tenth of its budget, which he computed at two point eight billion dollars, into finding measurable signals that would tell a trial in months what it now learned in years from survival. The second was translational institutions: hospitals built for the purpose of moving discoveries into practice, on the model of the development fabs that Intel ran alongside its research, where a process was proven at scale before it went to production. The third was operational, and it was the one that made him angriest. He had found that no more than fifteen to twenty percent of American medical institutions used any kind of electronic record, in a year when the rest of the economy transacted hundreds of billions of dollars online. He wanted Medicare to require a common data standard as a condition of payment, and he called it a Rosetta stone, so that a patient's information could move between institutions the way a purchase order moved between companies.

Read that essay as a manager's document and the structure is familiar from the two books. There is a limiting step, the knowledge turn, and everything is arranged around shortening it. There is a leverage argument, that a measurement which shortens every subsequent trial is worth more than any single trial it could have funded. There is a paired indicator, though he does not call it that: he wanted the survival curve, the outcome, paired with a biomarker, the process signal that tells you early whether the outcome is likely to move. And there is the operational floor beneath the science, the records, which he treated as the equivalent of a fab that did not know its own yield. The essay is High Output Management with the breakfast factory replaced by an oncology ward.

What it does not have is any theory of why medicine's knowledge turns are slow, beyond the assumption that they are slow for the same reason a badly run factory's are, and that is where the physicians came in.

## Chapter Three: The Funder

Grove's second engagement with medicine was not as a writer. In two thousand, two years after he stepped down as chief executive, he noticed a tremor in his right index finger, and the diagnosis was Parkinson's disease. He kept it private for some years, treated it the way he treated most things, with a programme, doubling his coffee, ninety minutes of exercise a day, delaying medication to preserve its effect for later, and then, when it became public, did what a person of his means and temperament would do, which was to fund the research and then to manage it.

The numbers, reported by Forbes in January two thousand and eight, were twenty-two million dollars committed to Parkinson's research over the decade and a forty million dollar bequest to the Michael J. Fox Foundation, where he served as an adviser. The programme was characteristic. Five million went to stem cell work at the University of California in San Francisco early in the decade, when almost nobody was funding it, with a joint grant alongside the Fox foundation to eight more laboratories; by two thousand and four the attempt to turn embryonic cells into dopamine neurons had failed, the cells reverting or forming tumours, and Grove noted the failure and moved the money. Two million went to something he called the At Home Box, an Intel-built device that measured a patient's speech and movement objectively, because the standard rating scale for the disease, the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale, was a clinician's subjective score and Grove regarded a subjective score as no measurement at all. He funded a neurosurgeon, Krystof Bankiewicz, who was working on delivering a growth factor, GDNF, directly into the brain, and Bankiewicz said afterwards that the funding had moved his work forward by years. And when the drug company Amgen abandoned its own GDNF trial, Grove tried, in late two thousand and seven, to license the programme and restart it, because one patient in the abandoned trial had improved by eighty percent and, as far as Grove could discover, nobody had gone back to find out why.

That last detail is the one I would keep, because there Grove's experience and the field's practice were furthest apart. In a fab, a failed lot is the most valuable thing that happens all week: the failure is analysed until it is understood, because the understanding is what stops it happening again, and a fab that threw away its failed lots without analysis would not survive a quarter. A trial that fails is closed. The drug is shelved, the data are archived, the one patient who responded is a footnote, and the field moves on, partly because the incentives run that way and partly because a single responder in a failed trial is, statistically, more likely to be noise than signal, which is a real reason and not one Grove had much patience for. The Forbes profile quotes him telling a room of researchers that if they could not get a drug to the target repeatedly and reproducibly they had wasted a career, and it quotes the blood-brain barrier as the thing he could not believe so little work went into. He was, in that room, the Cassandra of his own second book, the outsider who sees the ten-X problem the insiders have learned to live around. He was also a man who had not run a trial and did not know what one costs.

## Chapter Four: The Speech, and the Reply

The third engagement was from a platform. In November two thousand and seven Grove gave a talk at the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience in San Diego, to several hundred people, and around the same time gave an interview to Newsweek, and between them he said what he had been building up to for a decade. He criticised the National Institutes of Health for how it allocated money. He criticised researchers for not sharing data. He criticised the pharmaceutical industry for a culture that valued caution over what he called time to money, and for discarding failed drugs instead of mining the trials for what they could teach. He called, in a phrase that the reports all picked up, for a cultural revolution that valued curiosity, follow-through, and a problem-solving orientation, and that put all the data being generated in full view, where anyone could scrutinise it. And he made the comparison that had been implicit in the JAMA essay explicit: his industry had delivered Moore's law, and medicine, with comparable money and comparable people, had delivered levodopa, a forty-year-old symptomatic drug, and not much else for his disease.

The reply came within days, and the person who wrote it has to be named, because his phrase for the argument has outlived Grove's. Derek Lowe is a medicinal chemist who had spent his career in drug discovery and who wrote, and still writes, a widely read blog about it called In the Pipeline. His post was titled Andy Grove: Rich, Famous, Smart and Wrong, and the premise it attacked he named the Andy Grove fallacy, and the name stuck. Lowe's argument was that Grove had walked into a field where the equivalent of Moore's law is linear and was wondering where the curve had gone. Semiconductors were built on a century of solid-state physics that was, by the time Intel existed, essentially complete: the engineers knew how every component worked because they had designed every component. Biology was not designed by anyone, had a few billion years' head start, and was still yielding new components, and new interactions between old ones, every week. A chip that failed could be redesigned and retested in a month; a molecule that failed in a person could not be modified without risking a toxicity nobody could predict. And the two industries' failures were not the same kind of thing. David Hamilton, writing in VentureBeat the same week, made the point with a name: a failed chip cost Intel money, and a failed drug was Vioxx. The FDA existed because the failures were bodies, and the caution Grove was calling a cultural defect was, in part, the memory of the bodies.

Grove did not retreat. Four years later, in September twenty eleven, he wrote an editorial for Science called Rethinking Clinical Trials, and it was the knowledge-turns argument taken to its conclusion. Once a drug had passed its early safety testing, he proposed, patients who wanted it should be able to take it, with their physicians entering what happened into a shared database, so that the evidence would accumulate from use rather than from a trial, and be analysed by what he called, borrowing from the encyclopaedia, a Wikipedia-like process. He was proposing to replace the randomised trial with an observational registry and to let the crowd do the statistics. Lowe wrote back again, in a post he called A Note to Andy Grove, and this time the objection was one that any reader of this shelf could have supplied, because it is the objection to Grove's own cancer analysis: patients who choose a drug differ from patients who do not, and a database of the chosen tells you what happened to the people who chose, and nothing about the drug. The randomised trial exists because the alternative had been tried for two centuries and had produced bloodletting.

Lowe and Hamilton were, I think, right, and the shelf's own instruments say why. Grove's whole method depends on a fast, cheap, informative test, the thing that makes a knowledge turn short. The breakfast factory works because you can taste the egg. Everything in the two books, the paired indicator, the experiment, the debate, assumes that the world will answer a question in a reasonable time if you ask it clearly. Biology often will not, and the reason is not culture but the object. Grove had run into what the Seeing Like a State episodes called the limits of legibility from the other direction: not a state that could only see what it had simplified, but an engineer who assumed the object was as legible as the one he had spent his life with.

And yet. Hamilton, in the same piece that refuted him, said what Grove could usefully do instead, and it was the thing Grove was already doing: fund the unglamorous translational work between an academic finding and a company's willingness to spend on it, the validation nobody's incentives cover. And Lowe's fallacy, correctly stated, is about the science layer. It has no bearing on the records, or on whether specialists compare outcomes across modalities, or on whether the one patient who improved by eighty percent gets a phone call. Grove's critics won on knowledge turns and did not contest the floor beneath them, and the floor was where most of his complaints lived.

## Chapter Five: What He Was Right About

I want to score the essay, since it is twenty-one years old and most of its claims can now be checked, and to do it honestly, which means separating the layers his critics ran together.

On records he was right, and the field agreed with him within four years. The American stimulus law of two thousand and nine put something over twenty-five billion dollars into paying hospitals and practices to adopt electronic records, with penalties for those that did not, and the adoption rate went from Grove's fifteen to twenty percent to something over ninety percent of hospitals in a decade. Whether it produced what he wanted is another matter, since the systems that were built do not talk to each other much better than the paper did, and the Rosetta stone he asked for, a common standard enforced by the payer, is still being argued about in twenty twenty-six. But the diagnosis was correct and the remedy he named was the remedy that was used, and a manager who is right about the mechanism and early about the timing has done what a manager can.

On biomarkers he was half right, and the half matters. A measurable signal that predicts the outcome months before the outcome arrives is exactly what the field has spent the last two decades building, in oncology above all, and the fraction of trials that use one to decide whether to continue is far higher than it was. But the essay's assumption was that the biomarker would shorten the knowledge turn without changing what was learned, and the field's experience has been that a biomarker can be moved without moving the patient, that a tumour can shrink on the scan while the person dies on schedule, and the last twenty years of drug regulation are, in part, a record of the surrogate endpoints that were trusted and should not have been. Grove's paired-indicator rule from the first book would have told him this. The biomarker is the process signal; survival is the outcome; and an organisation that rewards the first will get the first. He knew that about factories. He did not apply it to the thing he was recommending.

On translational institutions he was right about the gap and wrong about the model. The gap between a finding and a treatment is real, careers and molecules die in it, and the field has built things with the word translational in their name ever since. But the fab he was modelling them on works because the process being scaled is understood and the yield can be measured next week; the equivalent in medicine is a trial, and a trial is not a scale-up but an experiment whose answer is not known, and that is why it is being run. He was proposing to shorten the time between hypothesis and test. The time is mostly in the test, and the test's length is set by the disease.

And on the culture, on data sharing and failure analysis and the patient who improved and was never called, I think he was right and the field has been slow to say so. The data-sharing argument he made in two thousand and seven is the argument that produced trial registration mandates, results-reporting requirements, and the current pressure toward sharing participant-level data, none of which were the norm when he spoke. The failure-analysis argument has been made since by people inside the field, and the practice of going back into a negative trial to ask who responded and why is now done under a respectable name, though not often enough, and not usually with the urgency of a fab that has lost a lot. Grove did not cause any of that. He was one loud voice among many, and a rich one, which helped and also made him easy to dismiss. But the direction he pointed in is the direction the field went, and the fallacy Lowe named, which was real, was a fallacy about the part of the building Grove cared least about.

There is a fourth thing, and it is smaller and I think it is the one to carry. In his own cancer, and again with the Parkinson's rating scale, Grove's instinct was to distrust a measurement that depended on who was doing the measuring, and to build one that did not. The At Home Box was a two-million-dollar argument that a clinician's score on a scale is not a measurement, and the field's move since toward wearable sensors and objective motor assessment in that disease is the argument being accepted. That instinct, that an indicator which the person being measured or the person measuring can move by wishing it moved is not yet an indicator, is the one that connects this episode to the next, and it is the instinct a person running any programme on reported data should hold onto hardest. The shelf will spend two episodes on what happens when it is ignored.

## Chapter Six: Swimming Across

A word on the memoir, because it explains the temperament, and because it is the best of his books as writing. Swimming Across, from two thousand and one, covers the twenty years before the Andy Grove that Intel knew existed. András Gróf, born in Budapest in September nineteen thirty-six, nearly killed by scarlet fever at four and left partly deaf; the German occupation of forty-four, hidden with his mother under a borrowed Christian name while his father was in a forced labour battalion on the eastern front; the siege; the Soviet liberation and the things the liberators did; the Communist years, in which the family's small dairy business was taken and a boy who wanted to be a journalist learned that the newspaper printed what it was told; the uprising of fifty-six, and, at twenty, the walk across a marsh to the Austrian border with nothing, and a ship to New York. Kirkus called the book tight, simply told, and extremely intimate, with what it called a sardonic but unmistakable faith in the American dream, and that is fair. The title is a teacher's remark that life is a lake to be swum across, and the book is not about Intel and never mentions it.

The connection to this episode is that a person who has lived through three changes of regime before the age of twenty, each of which arrived without warning and punished the people who had adapted to the previous one, is going to run a company on the assumption that the rules can change at any moment, and is going to treat a medical establishment that has not changed its rules in forty years as something between a puzzle and an affront. The paranoia of the second book and the impatience of the JAMA essay are the same trait, and Swimming Across is where the trait was formed. It does not make him right. It explains why he could not leave it alone.

## Chapter Seven: The Case Against, Stated Hard

The objections to Grove's medical writing were made by people who knew the field, and I have given the best of them. Let me put them together as a case, because the shelf's rule is that the case against is stated at full strength before the verdict.

He extrapolated from a designed system to an evolved one and did not see the difference until it was pointed out, and, on the record, did not accept it afterwards. The knowledge-turns argument is not a management insight applied to medicine; it is a category error, and a manager of Grove's quality should have caught it, because the first question his own method asks is what the limiting step is, and in a trial that step is the disease. His cancer analysis, which he offered as the model of what a patient can do, walked into confounding by indication and came out with the answer he preferred, and it is possible to admire the effort and to say that a patient with a spreadsheet can be as wrong as a surgeon with a bias. His Parkinson's funding, on the Forbes numbers, produced a failed stem cell programme, a measurement device that was ahead of its time and did not change practice, and an accelerated growth-factor line that has still not produced a treatment; what it produced was pressure, and pressure is not a result. And the Society for Neuroscience speech, whatever its merits, was delivered by a man worth a great deal of money to a room of people who were not, telling them that they lacked urgency, which is a thing rich men say to rooms, and the field's resentment was not entirely unearned.

I accept all of it and I still think the ledger comes out in his favour, and the reason is the one the shelf has used before. Distinguish what the author claims from what the evidence supports from what the narrator concludes. Grove claimed medicine was a slow factory. The evidence says that its floor, the records and the comparisons and the follow-up of failures, was run like one, and that its science was not a factory at all. What I conclude is that he was an outsider who was wrong about the part he found most interesting and right about the one he found boring, and that the field, having won the interesting argument, has spent twenty years conceding the dull one.

## Chapter Eight: Verdict, Dose, and a Question

The verdict is that the JAMA essay is worth an hour of any clinician-researcher's time, read beside Lowe's reply, and that the pair are a better education in what management thinking can and cannot do for medicine than any of the books that have been written to bridge the two since. Read the Fortune article as a case study in patient reasoning, with the selection problem circled in the margin. Skip the Newsweek interview. Read Swimming Across for its own sake.

The dose is a caution about direction. Grove's outside view is a medicine for a field that has stopped asking why its floor is so badly run, and it is a poison for anyone who takes from it that biology is a process problem awaiting a better manager. Take the records complaint, the cross-modality comparison, the call to the patient who responded, and the distrust of a score that depends on the scorer. Leave the knowledge turns.

The question to carry into the week is his, from the abandoned trial. Somewhere in the work you run there is a result that did not come out, a study, a programme, a district, a quarter, and somewhere in that result there is one case that went unexpectedly well. Name it. Then ask whether anyone has gone back to find out why, and if the answer is no, ask what the reason is, and whether the reason would survive being said to a man from a fab.

Next time, the measurement constellation, in two parts. First, one law discovered three times: a nineteen fifty-six paper by a researcher named Ridgway who watched a state employment office count interviews and stop placing anyone, a Bank of England economist named Goodhart who watched the money supply misbehave the moment it was targeted, and a psychologist named Campbell who watched a president's war on crime lower the crime figures and nothing else. Thanks for listening.
