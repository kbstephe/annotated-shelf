## Chapter One: The Pasture Everybody Knows

In nineteen sixty-eight the biologist Garrett Hardin published a short essay in Science with a title that has done more work than its argument. The Tragedy of the Commons. You know the picture even if you have never read it, because it is taught in the first week of every economics course and half the environmental ones. A pasture is open to all. Each herdsman gains the whole benefit of adding one more animal and bears only a fraction of the cost, since the overgrazing is shared. So each adds another, and another, and the pasture is destroyed, and no one could have done otherwise. Hardin's conclusion was that the only escapes were privatisation, which gives each herdsman his own fenced field and his own consequences, or coercion, which gives the state the whip. Freedom in a commons, he wrote, brings ruin to all.

The essay was about population, the part everybody forgets, and it recommended coercion on that subject too. But the pasture escaped the essay and became a fact about the world. For twenty years after it appeared, policy toward every shared resource on earth, forests, fisheries, groundwater, grazing land, was argued from that picture, and the argument was always between the two escapes: sell it, or nationalise it. Governments in Nepal nationalised the village forests in nineteen fifty-seven, and the forests, which had been managed by the villages for generations, were stripped in a decade, because a forest that belongs to the state in Kathmandu belongs to nobody on the hillside. Fisheries were privatised into tradeable quotas. Both escapes were taken, all over the world, on the strength of a two-page parable that contained no data.

Elinor Ostrom's contribution, in the plainest terms, was to go and look. Governing the Commons, from nineteen ninety, is a report on what she found, and what she found was that the pasture in the essay does not exist. Real pastures have people on them, and the people talk, and over centuries they have worked out, without a landlord and without a state, how to keep the grass. This episode is about what those arrangements look like, why some fail, the eight properties the durable ones share, and what the shelf, after a season of watching institutions fail, should make of a book whose subject is institutions that work.

## Chapter Two: Who She Was

Ostrom was born Elinor Awan in Los Angeles in nineteen thirty-three, to a family that the Depression had made poor. She stuttered as a child and was put on the debate team to fix it, which she later credited with teaching her that every question has two defensible sides and that the job is to find out which one the evidence favours. She wanted a doctorate in economics at the University of California at Los Angeles and was refused, because she had not taken the trigonometry that the department required and, as a woman, had been steered away from it in school. She took political science instead, where the entrance exam was easier and the faculty told her openly that a woman would be wasting a place. Her doctoral work, in the early sixties, was on the groundwater basins under Los Angeles, which were being pumped faster than they refilled, and which the users, cities and water companies and farmers, had organised themselves to save through a mixture of litigation, negotiated quotas, and a new taxing district. She watched a commons being governed, in the middle of a city, by its own users, thirty years before she wrote the book. Everything after was an attempt to find out whether what she had seen in Los Angeles was general.

With her husband Vincent she built a research group at Indiana University that they called the Workshop, and the name was a statement. Research there was a craft, done in collaboration, on cases, with the theory revised every time a case broke it. She sent students to Nepal and Kenya and the Philippines and taught them a coding protocol so that a forest in one country could be compared with a forest in another. In two thousand and nine she was awarded the Nobel Prize in economics, the first woman to receive it, shared with Oliver Williamson, and the citation named the thing she had shown: that common property can be successfully managed by the people who use it. She died in two thousand and twelve, and the last piece she published, that same year, was on the same subject as the first.

## Chapter Three: Törbel, and the Rule About Winter

The book's method is to take the cases first and the theory after, and I will follow it. The first case is a village in the Swiss canton of Valais called Törbel, six hundred people, on a mountainside, and the reason it is in the book is that its records go back to fourteen eighty-three. In that year the villagers signed a document establishing an association to govern their common land, the alp where the cows grazed in summer, the forests where they cut timber and firewood, the waste land, the irrigation channels, and the paths. Private property existed alongside: each family owned its own fields and vineyards and hay meadows in the valley. What was held in common was what could not usefully be divided, the high pasture that is only good for grazing for a few months, and the forest whose value depends on being managed as one.

The rule that governed the alp is the one to remember, because it is so simple and so hard to think of. No villager could send more cows to the summer pasture than he could feed through the winter from his own hay. Hardin's herdsman adds an animal because the pasture is free. Törbel's herdsman cannot, because the number of animals he may graze is tied to a resource he owns and pays for, his winter hay, and the tie is checked in public every year when the cows go up. The rule uses the private property to ration the common one. The village elected officials to count the cows and impose the fines, kept the fines in the community's own accounts, and used the money for the paths and the channels. The forest was managed by the same association: trees were marked for felling by the officials, the felled timber was divided by lot among the households, and no one cut what had not been marked.

Ostrom is careful to say what this is not. It is not an unmanaged commons, in Hardin's sense. Nobody in Törbel could walk onto the alp with a herd. It is not private property either; the alp was never divided, and the association that owned it had no owner. And it is not the state: the canton and the confederation left the villagers to it. It is a third thing, and the book's argument is that this third thing is everywhere once you look, and that the two-escapes framing had made it invisible. The Japanese villages that Margaret McKean studied, with their common mountain land and their rules about how much grass each household could cut, and the day each spring when the cutting was opened, and the patrols that caught the early cutters, are the same institution in a different language. So are the Philippine irrigation societies, the zanjeras, in which the labour of maintaining the canal is owed in proportion to the water received, and a member who fails to turn up for the maintenance day loses water in proportion.

## Chapter Four: The Water Court at the Cathedral Door

The second case is the one I would send a sceptic to, because it is still running and you can go and watch. In the huerta of Valencia, the irrigated plain around the city, farmers have shared the water of the Turia river for something like a thousand years, through a system of canals with Arabic names, and the disputes between them are settled by a court called the Tribunal de las Aguas. It sits every Thursday at noon outside the Apostles' door of the cathedral. The judges are farmers, elected by the irrigators of each canal. There are no lawyers and no written record; a complaint is heard, the accused answers, and the judges rule on the spot, in Valencian, in public. The fines are small and the judgements have never, in the tribunal's recorded history, been appealed to a state court, which is a way of saying that the farmers regard it as theirs.

What Ostrom draws from the huerta is the mechanism of monitoring. Water in a canal system is stolen by taking it out of turn, or leaving your gate open longer than your allotted time. The person best placed to see that happen is the farmer whose turn is next, because it is his water that has gone. So the monitoring is done by the users, in the course of using, at no cost to anyone, and it is backed by a court that the users run and that meets within days. The huerta's system has an official, the guard of each canal, but the guard is not a policeman; he is the man the next farmer complains to. The sanction is graduated: a first offence is a small fine and public embarrassment, a repeat is larger, a persistent offender can lose his water. The graduation is not there for deterrence in the criminal-law sense. Its purpose is that the first fine is small enough that a farmer who has taken water in a dry year because his crop was dying, and everyone knows it, can be fined without being ruined, and can go on being a member. The rule is enforced, and the community survives the enforcement.

There is a detail from the neighbouring huertas that I like more than any other in the book. In Murcia and Orihuela, further south, the water is scarcer, and the rules are more elaborate; in Alicante, scarcer still, the water rights were separated from the land in the thirteenth century and are auctioned. The institutions vary with the scarcity, and vary in the direction a designer would choose, and no designer chose them. The people who needed them built them, one dispute at a time, over centuries, and they fit the river the way a glove fits a hand because they were shaped on the hand. The shelf met this idea last month in James Scott's mētis, the local practical knowledge that the high modernist cannot see. Ostrom's cases are what mētis looks like when it has been institutionalised, written down, and given a court.

## Chapter Five: The Ones That Failed

A book containing only successful commons would prove nothing, and Ostrom knew it, so a third of hers is about failure. The Turkish fisheries are the cleanest comparison because they hold the culture, the law, and the fish constant and vary only the institution. At Alanya, on the Mediterranean coast, a hundred fishermen had spent the early nineteen seventies fighting over the best spots, with violence, rising costs, and falling catches, until the local cooperative devised a system: a list of the good fishing locations was drawn up, the eligible fishermen were listed, and in September each year they drew lots for their starting spots and then rotated daily, eastward, through the list. The rotation meant that every boat got every good spot in turn, so nobody had to fight for one, and it meant that a boat in the wrong spot was instantly visible to the boat that should have been there, so the monitoring was free. The system was not written into national law. It was enforced by the fishermen and, occasionally, by the coffee house.

At Bodrum and in Izmir Bay it went the other way. The number of boats was too large and too fluid, the government's development loans had brought in trawlers and outsiders with no stake in the local rules, and the fishermen never managed to agree who was in and who was out. Without a boundary there was no group to make a rule, and without a rule there was nothing to monitor, and the fisheries declined the way Hardin said they would. Ostrom's reading is that Hardin's tragedy is real, and that it happens in a specific condition: where the users cannot communicate, cannot define who belongs, and cannot make their own rules stick. Where those conditions are absent, so is the tragedy. Hardin's parable described a special case and sold it as a law.

She adds the Sri Lankan irrigation schemes, where the state built the canals and appointed the officials and the farmers stole the water and broke the gates, because the rules were Colombo's and the water was theirs; and the Nova Scotia inshore fisheries, where a functioning local system was overridden by a national licensing regime that treated the coast as one fishery; and the Californian groundwater basins where negotiation failed. The pattern of failure is consistent, and it is often the state's arrival, with good intentions and a general rule, that breaks a local arrangement that had been working. That is Scott's argument from below, and Ostrom got there first, with a more modest claim and better data.

## Chapter Six: The Laboratory, and the Canals Nobody Built

Ostrom did one more thing that separates her from every other author on this shelf: she took the field cases into a laboratory and tried to break them. With Roy Gardner and James Walker, through the eighties and into the nineties, she ran experiments in which students at Indiana played a commons game for real money. Each player chose how much to invest in a shared resource whose return fell as total investment rose, the pasture in numbers, and the theory predicted that they would overinvest until the joint return was destroyed. On the first runs they did. Then the experimenters allowed the players to talk to each other, for a few minutes, with nothing enforceable, what game theory calls cheap talk because it should change nothing. It changed nearly everything. Groups that could communicate cut their overinvestment by most of the distance to the optimum and stayed there for round after round. Then the experimenters let the players fine each other, at a cost to the fining player, and the groups that could both talk and sanction did better still, while the ones who could only sanction without talking punished each other into a worse outcome than silence. The experiments are written up in Rules, Games, and Common-Pool Resources, from nineteen ninety-four, and what they show is that Hardin's result depends on a condition he did not state: that the herdsmen never meet.

This is the answer to an objection I will raise properly in a moment, about old, small, trusting communities. The students in the laboratory had met an hour earlier. They had no history, no shared church, and no expectation of seeing each other again, and a few minutes of talk was enough to move them most of the way to the villagers' outcome. Trust in those rooms was manufactured by the rules of the room, in the time it took to hold a conversation, and that is the claim a hospital department needs, because it has no five hundred years and it does have a meeting room.

The second piece of evidence is from Nepal, and it is the one I would put in front of anyone who still believes the state's engineers must know best. In the nineties the Workshop compiled data on more than a hundred irrigation systems in the Nepalese hills, some built and run by government agencies with concrete headworks and permanent canals, and some built by the farmers themselves out of stone, mud, and brush that had to be rebuilt after every monsoon. Wai Fung Lam's analysis of the data, published in nineteen ninety-eight, found that the farmer-managed systems delivered more water to the tail end of the canal, had higher cropping intensities, and kept their channels in better repair than the agency systems with the superior engineering. The mechanism is the one from the huerta. In a farmer-run system the people at the head of the canal need the people at the tail to turn up for the annual rebuilding, so they cannot take all the water; in the agency system the concrete does not need anyone, and the head-enders take what they like. The better technology broke the institution that made the water reach the end. The shelf spent two episodes last month on James Scott's argument that the state's improvements destroy what they cannot see. Here it is in litres.

## Chapter Seven: The Eight Properties

The theory, when it comes, is not a model. It is a list of the properties that the durable cases share and the failed cases lack, and Ostrom calls them design principles while insisting that nobody designed them. There are eight, and for the ear I will give them as the questions a user of a shared resource would ask.

Who is in? The successful commons have a clear boundary, both of the resource and of the people entitled to use it. Do the rules fit this place? The rules for taking match the local conditions, the winter hay, the Thursday turn, the September rotation, and they match the rules for contributing. Who makes the rules? The people affected by them can take part in changing them. Who watches? The monitors are the users themselves or people answerable to them. What happens when someone breaks a rule? The sanctions are graduated, small for a first offence, larger for persistence. Where do disputes go? There is a cheap, fast, local place to settle them, the cathedral door. Will the outside let us? The state at least does not challenge the community's right to organise. And, for the big systems, are there layers? Appropriation, monitoring, enforcement, and dispute resolution are organised in nested units, the canal within the district within the river.

The principles have been the subject of a large empirical literature since, and I will come to it, but I want to draw out what they have in common, because it is not what a reader expecting a theory would expect. None of them is about incentives in the economist's sense, and none is about virtue. They are about information and about proportion. A boundary tells you whose behaviour to watch; user-monitoring makes the watching free; graduated sanctions make the first enforcement cheap enough to actually happen; the local court means the enforcement is fast enough to matter. Every principle reduces the cost of somebody finding out what somebody else did and doing something small about it, soon. Hardin's herdsman overgrazes because nothing he does is seen and nothing that is seen has a consequence. Ostrom's villagers do not, because everything is seen and the consequence is a fine on Thursday.

## Chapter Eight: The Case Against

The objections come from two directions, and the more damaging one is friendly. Arun Agrawal, a scholar of forest commons, argued in two thousand and one that the design-principles literature, Ostrom's and the two other major lists that appeared around the same time, had between them produced more than thirty conditions said to matter for success, and that a theory with thirty variables and a few dozen cases cannot be tested; any case can be explained after the fact by whichever conditions it happens to display. That is the clinician's objection to a prognostic model with more predictors than patients, and it is correct. The reply came in two thousand and ten from Michael Cox, Gwen Arnold, and Sergio Villamayor-Tomás, who coded ninety-one published case studies and found that each of the principles, taken one at a time, was associated with success, and that the principles held up better when two of them, the boundaries one and the congruence one, were split into their components, making eleven. That study has itself been criticised, for taking the case authors' judgement of success at face value and for treating outcomes as one thing when they are ecological in one study and social in the next. I read the exchange as a field in the ordinary condition of a young science: a proposed set of predictors, one meta-analysis in favour, the meta-analysis under review. The principles survive as a checklist that the evidence has not contradicted, which is more than can be said for the parable they replaced.

The second objection is about scale, and Eduardo Araral put it plainly in two thousand and fourteen. Ostrom's cases are small: villages, a coast, a river plain. The commons that now matter, the atmosphere, the oceans, the antibiotic pool, are enormous, their users cannot meet, and the boundary cannot be drawn. Her principles, he argued, may be true of the Swiss alp and useless for the climate. Ostrom's own answer, in a paper for the World Bank in two thousand and nine, was the idea she called polycentric governance: that a global commons can be governed by many overlapping units at many scales, each doing what it can, rather than by a single global agreement, and that waiting for the treaty is itself a way of ensuring the tragedy. Whether that answer works is a question about the next fifty years, and I will not pretend to know. What I will say is that the objection concedes the book. Araral's complaint is that the principles do not scale, which grants that at the scale she studied they hold.

There is a third objection I want to raise myself, because it is the one this shelf is equipped to make. Ostrom's successful commons are old, small, and homogeneous. Törbel had six hundred people who had intermarried for five centuries. The huerta's farmers were Valencian Catholics who had shared a language and a calendar since the Reconquista. The design principles may be the visible surface of something harder to transplant, which is a community that already trusts itself, and a hospital department assembled from people who arrived last year and will leave next year is not that. I think this is a real limit, and I think the book's own cases, and the laboratory, answer it in part. The Alanya fishermen were not a five-hundred-year community. They were a hundred men who had been fighting each other in the early seventies and who built a working institution in a few years, out of a list and a lottery. Trust in that case came after the rules, not before, and that sequence is the possibility the shelf's cynical season had stopped believing in.

## Chapter Nine: What a Hospital Holds in Common

I want to run the eight properties over something closer to home, and I will build the case rather than borrow one, so it is constructed, assembled from things that happen in every hospital I have worked in. A department shares an ultrasound machine. Six teams use it; it lives on a trolley; it is needed most between eight and ten in the morning, and by ten it has usually vanished into a side room with a probe missing and its battery flat. Complaints go to the manager, who sends an email saying the machine must be returned to the store after use, which is Hardin's coercion with no coercer, and it changes nothing, because nobody can see who took it and no consequence attaches to the taking.

Now read the principles over it. Who is in? Nobody has said, so the machine is also used by the outpatient clinic, a visiting fellow, and a radiographer from the next floor, and none of them know the rule because there is no group to belong to. Do the rules fit? The rule is return it to the store, which fails at eight in the morning when four teams need it inside the same hour; the rule that would fit is a rotation, the way the Alanya fishermen rotated spots. Who makes the rules? The manager did, alone, in an email; none of the six teams was asked, so none of them owns it. Who watches? Nobody; there is no sign-out sheet, so the team whose turn is next cannot see who has it, and that visibility was the free monitoring the huerta got from the farmer downstream. What happens when it is broken? Either nothing or a furious email to everyone, the opposite of graduated. Where do disputes go? To the same manager, weeks later. Does the outside allow it? The hospital would not object to six teams making their own arrangement, but no one has asked. And the layers: the machine is one of a dozen shared things in the department, each with the same problem, and there is no body that meets about any of them.

The fix that the book implies is not a better email. It is a group: the six teams named, meeting once, for half an hour, to agree a morning rotation, a sign-out board on the trolley, and a rule that the team who returns it flat charges it before their next turn. It is a commons institution, of the smallest possible kind, and it works where the email did not because the people who use the resource made the rule, can see who broke it, and can do something small about it by lunchtime. Most of what a manager governs is like the ultrasound machine, and most of it is being governed by email.

## Chapter Ten: Verdict, Dose, and a Question

The verdict is that Governing the Commons is the most hopeful book on this shelf and the best argued, and that both properties come from the same source, which is that Ostrom made no claim she had not gone and checked. The reading prescription is the book itself, chapters three and five first, the cases and the failures, and the design principles chapter only after you have the cases in your head, because read alone the principles are a list and read after Törbel they are a pattern you have already seen. Then Hardin's essay, which takes twenty minutes and which you should read to find out what was actually in it. Then Agrawal, for the strongest friendly objection, and Cox and colleagues for the answer.

The dose is the reverse of the shelf's usual warning. The cynical canon is medicine in small quantities and poison as a worldview. Ostrom is safe at any dose, and the risk with her runs the other way: a reader can come away believing that every commons can be governed by its users, and the book's failed cases are there to prevent that. Read the failures twice.

The question to carry into next week is a small one. Name one thing your department holds in common that is being managed by email. The machine, the room, the on-call rota, the fridge, the shared drive. Then ask the first three of Ostrom's questions of it: who is in, does the rule fit the hour when it is needed, and did the people who use it have any part in making the rule. If the answer to the third is no, you have found the reason the email did not work, and you have also found the half-hour meeting that would.

Next time, the third antidote, and the first book on this shelf written by someone who ran a large organisation and thought it went rather well: Andy Grove's High Output Management, on the breakfast factory, the one-on-one, and what a manager's output actually is. Thanks for listening.
