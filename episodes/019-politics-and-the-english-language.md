## Chapter One: Two Sentences About the Same Thing

Here is a sentence from a clinical note, and I have changed nothing about it except the details that would identify anyone; the shape is one I have read a thousand times. The patient was found to have deteriorated overnight and appropriate measures were instituted. Now here is what happened. Nobody checked on him between midnight and six. At six the nurse found him grey and breathing at thirty a minute, and the registrar was called, and the registrar started oxygen and antibiotics and asked why nobody had called earlier. The first sentence is not false. Every word in it is defensible. It is also constructed so that nothing in it can be checked, because it has no nurse, no registrar, no six hours, and no question at the end, and the person who wrote it may not have been lying at all. He may simply have been writing the way the notes are written, in the register that was available to him, and the register did the concealing on his behalf.

That gap, between a sentence that is true and a sentence that can be checked, is the subject of the essay this episode is about, and you should have the subject in front of you before the essay arrives, because the essay is famous for something else. It is famous for six rules about style. The rules are the least defensible thing in it, and the linguists have spent forty years saying so, with numbers. What is underneath the rules is a claim about how language protects the people who use it from what they are saying, and that claim is not about style, and it has survived every objection that has been made to the rules. The plan for this episode is to take the objections seriously enough to concede the essay, and then show why the mechanism does not go down with it.

## Chapter Two: An Essay Written in a Hurry by a Man Who Knew

Politics and the English Language was published in Horizon in April nineteen forty-six, four episodes ago in this shelf's chronology and a month before the essay on Burnham. Orwell was writing at the rate of a man who knew his time, and the two essays are twins: one about a mind corrupted by power, the other about a language corrupted by the need to defend it. He had reasons of his own to be interested. He had spent two years of the war at the BBC's Eastern Service, producing propaganda for India, and had come away with an insider's knowledge of what it feels like to write sentences whose purpose is to be broadcast rather than believed. He had also spent a decade reading the political pamphlets of the left, and he was the only prominent English socialist of his generation who wrote about the left's prose as if it were evidence.

The essay's structure is simple. He puts five specimens of bad contemporary writing on the table, a professor, a scientist, a psychologist, a Communist pamphlet, and a letter to Tribune, and he reads them the way this series tries to read everything, for the tells. Then he names four habits that the specimens share. Dying metaphors, figures of speech used so often that they no longer call up an image and are used only because they save the writer inventing a phrase. Operators, or verbal false limbs, the padding that replaces a simple verb with a phrase: render inoperative, militate against, make contact with, be subjected to, give rise to. Pretentious diction, the Latin and Greek words that dress a plain statement in a lab coat: phenomenon, element, objective, categorical, eliminate, liquidate. And meaningless words, the ones that in political and literary use have no agreed referent at all, so that a statement made with them cannot be contradicted: democracy, freedom, justice, realistic, and in criticism, living, vital, human.

Then the demonstration, the part of the essay that is still funny. He takes a verse of Ecclesiastes, the one about the race not being to the swift nor the battle to the strong, and translates it into the prose of the specimens: objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity. The joke is precise. The translation is longer, has more syllables, contains not a single concrete image, and says less, and it is the kind of sentence that would be accepted without comment in any committee paper in the country. And his image for what has happened is the one line of the essay I would keep if I could keep one: a mass of Latin words falls upon the facts like soft snow, blurring the outlines and covering up all the details.

## Chapter Three: The Rules, and What the Linguists Did to Them

The six rules come near the end, and they are what the essay has become in the culture, printed on the walls of newsrooms and copied into every style guide since. No figure of speech you are used to seeing in print. No long word where a short one will do. Any word that can be cut, cut. The active in preference to the passive. No foreign phrase, scientific term, or jargon where an everyday English word exists. And break any of these sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

The case against the rules is the best-documented case against anything on this shelf, because linguists count, and the counting was done by Geoffrey Pullum, co-author of the Cambridge Grammar of the English Language and for twenty years the most persistent critic of usage advice in the English-speaking world. Take rule four, the passive. Pullum went through the essay itself and counted the passive constructions against the transitive verbs, using the strict grammatical definition rather than the loose one style guides use. Roughly a quarter of Orwell's transitive verbs in the essay are passive. The rate in ordinary published prose, in the corpora linguists use to measure such things, is closer to one in six. Orwell used the passive more than the average writer, in the essay in which he told everyone else to stop. And Pullum's broader finding, from years of these counts, is that the pattern is general: the writers most exercised about the passive use it more than the rest of us, E. B. White included, because what they object to is the idea of it rather than the thing, and could not identify the thing in their own sentences if asked.

Take rule one. Pullum's objection, in a piece he called Elimination of the Fittest, is that banning every figure of speech a reader has seen before is a rule against the language. The familiar metaphors are the ones that worked; that is why you keep seeing them. Follow the rule to its limit and you strip out the most successful phrases first, then the next most successful, until what remains is either invented that morning or barbarous, and the sixth rule then tells you to prefer the cliché after all. The rules are not a method. They are a mood, and the last of them is the mood admitting it.

Take the rules' originality, of which there is none. Arnold Zwicky traced the passive prohibition to American composition handbooks of the early twentieth century, Strunk's Elements of Style of nineteen eighteen among the first, and found it a commonplace of the college classroom by the nineteen thirties. Orwell did not discover that plain prose was better than padded prose. He restated a schoolroom doctrine with more force and a more interesting politics, and the doctrine was already wrong in the ways it was wrong before he restated it.

And take the essay's reputation, which its own admirers have started to question. Steven Poole, a critic whose own book Unspeak is the modern descendant of Orwell's argument, has called this essay probably the most wildly overrated of any of his writings, and his objection is to the arbitrariness: a righteous but limited assault on political euphemism carried by a comically arbitrary collection of intolerances, as though a list of the words that irritated Orwell in nineteen forty-six were a theory of language. Ed Smith, in the New Statesman, made the sharper political point. Orwell thought obscurity was the technique of the liar. Smith suspects the opposite is now true: when a politician or a corporation needs to bridge the gap between what it is saying and what it knows, the preferred instrument is no longer the Latinate fog but a misleading simplicity, the short plain sentence that sounds like a man telling you the truth. The rules cannot see that at all. A liar who follows all six rules is a more convincing liar.

There it is, then. The rules are unoriginal, unfollowable, violated by their author at above the average rate, and blind to the form of political lying that has become standard since. If the essay were its rules, this episode would be a short one and the verdict would be Poole's.

## Chapter Four: Steelman, Which Requires Reading the Whole Thing

The essay is not its rules, and the case for it starts with noticing where the rules are. They come in the second-to-last section, after the argument, offered as a practical aside for someone who has accepted the argument and wants to know what to do on Monday. Orwell says so himself, in the sentence before them, and he says something else that the style-guide readers never quote: look back through this essay, he writes, and you will find that he has again and again committed the very faults he is protesting against. Pullum's count does not embarrass a man who counted himself first. It confirms the essay's actual claim, which is that the habits are nearly impossible to escape, including for the person describing them, because they are not habits of style. They are habits of not thinking, and the essay is about thinking.

The argument, stripped of the rules, has three moves, and each is a mechanism rather than a preference. The first is that the causation runs both ways. Language becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, Orwell says, and then the half that matters: the slovenliness of the language makes it easier to have foolish thoughts. A dying metaphor is not merely dull. It is a phrase the writer did not have to think in order to produce, and a sentence assembled from phrases nobody thought is a sentence that nobody, including its author, has checked against anything. That is the clinical note from the first chapter. The patient was found to have deteriorated is a phrase that comes pre-assembled, and for that reason it arrives without the six hours in it, and the writer never has to decide to leave them out. The register decided.

The second move is the one about politics, and it is the sentence everyone quotes and almost nobody puts in context. Political language, Orwell writes, is designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable. The context is a list, and the list is the mechanism. Defenceless villages are bombed from the air, their inhabitants driven into the countryside, their cattle machine-gunned, their huts set on fire: this is called pacification. Millions of peasants are robbed of their farms and sent trudging along the roads with what they can carry: this is called transfer of population. People are imprisoned without trial or shot in the back of the neck: this is called elimination of unreliable elements. Look at what the euphemism does in each case, and it is not what the rules imply. The Latinate phrase is not vaguer than the plain description in the sense of being less specific. It is just as specific. Everyone who used the phrase transfer of population in nineteen forty-six knew what it referred to. What the phrase removes is not the information but the image, and with the image goes the reflex, the flinch that a person would have if he had to say cattle machine-gunned aloud. The euphemism is a device for saying a true thing without having to feel it, and Orwell's argument is that a person who can say it that way can go on to do it that way, which is why political language and political atrocity keep the company they keep. The shelf met that mechanism last episode in the Soviet countryside. Transfer of population is what Scott's chapter on collectivisation looks like from inside the ministry that ordered it.

The third move is the one the essay is least credited for, and it is the one this series has been running on since its first episode. Orwell says that when you watch a tired hack on a platform mechanically repeating the familiar phrases, bestial atrocities, iron heel, bloodstained tyranny, you have the curious feeling that you are not watching a live human being but a dummy, and that the feeling becomes stronger at the moments when the light catches the speaker's spectacles and turns them into blank discs with no eyes behind them. A speaker who has gone over to phrases has stopped, for the duration of the speech, being a person who is choosing his words, and Orwell's word for what he has become is orthodoxy: a set of positions that can be uttered without the utterer having to be present. That is the organization man's interior surrender, seven episodes ago, seen from the outside, at the level of the sentence. Whyte's man gave his beliefs to the group and experienced it as belonging. Orwell's hack gives his sentences to the orthodoxy and experiences it as fluency. The Social Ethic was a prose style before it was a personality.

## Chapter Five: The Mechanism Tested Against the Critics

Now put the critics against the argument rather than against the rules and see what happens.

Pullum's count is correct and it does not touch the mechanism. The passive is not the problem and Orwell, in the argument, never quite says it is; the rule is a crude proxy for something the argument states properly, which is that a sentence with no agent is a sentence in which nobody did anything. The patient was found to have deteriorated. Appropriate measures were instituted. Mistakes were made, the most famous three-word passive in American politics, is not bad because it is passive. It is bad since it has been constructed so that the question who made them has no place to attach. A passive with an agent is fine, and often necessary, and clinical prose has a hundred honest uses for it: the patient was randomised, the sample was centrifuged, where the agent is uninteresting and the process is what the sentence is for. The instrument is not the voice of the verb. It is whether the sentence has a slot for the person who would be responsible if the sentence were checked, and whether that slot has been filled. Orwell's rule four is a bad rule for a real thing, and Pullum has refuted the rule.

Poole's objection lands on the rules and slides off the argument for the same reason. The intolerances are arbitrary; the mechanism is not. A dying metaphor is a phrase the writer did not think; a pretentious word is a plain statement in a costume; a meaningless word is a claim that cannot be contradicted. Those are three ways of producing a sentence that cannot be checked, and Orwell's list of examples is a nineteen forty-six sample of each, and the sample is dated, and the categories are not. Anyone who has read a hospital's strategic plan can supply the twenty twenty-six sample without help.

Smith's objection is the one that actually costs something, and it should be conceded in full and then examined. Yes: plainness has been weaponised. The short sentence that sounds like a man telling the truth is the modern instrument of the man who is not. But look at what the plain-style liar's sentences have in common with the Latinate fog, and it is the thing the argument names. We got this wrong and we are fixing it. The plain sentence is agentless too; it just hides the agent in a we that has no address instead of in a passive that has none. It is checkable to the same degree as appropriate measures were instituted, which is not at all. Smith has shown that the rules cannot detect the modern lie. He has not shown that the mechanism cannot. The mechanism asks the same question of the short sentence that it asks of the long one: who, what, when, and what would I look at to find out whether this is so. The politician's plain style fails that question as completely as the professor's fog, and the essay, read for its argument rather than its list, told you it would.

Which leaves the count. The essay's rules are wrong, and its argument, that there is a kind of sentence which is built so that it cannot be checked, that such sentences are produced by not thinking and in turn make not thinking easier, and that they are the medium in which people say the unsayable to themselves first, is right and has not been answered. The linguists have won every engagement and lost the war, because they were fighting the wrong text.

## Chapter Six: A Second Specimen, From a Grant

Orwell took his five specimens from the prose of nineteen forty-six, and the fair test of his categories is whether they still sort the prose of now, so here is a sixth, and I should say that it is constructed: not a real paragraph from a real application, but one assembled from the phrases that real applications are made of, in the way that Orwell assembled his Ecclesiastes translation. It reads like this. Leveraging a robust, multidisciplinary framework, the proposed initiative will facilitate the co-design of a scalable, community-driven intervention, optimising stakeholder engagement across the care continuum and generating actionable insights to inform sustainable, evidence-based transformation of service delivery.

Run the four categories over it. Dying metaphors: leveraging, which was a metaphor about a lever once and is now a way of not writing use; the care continuum, a line nobody has ever drawn. Verbal false limbs: facilitate the co-design of, where the plain verb is design and the padding exists to avoid saying who will do it; generating actionable insights to inform, which is four words standing in for find out. Pretentious diction: robust, multidisciplinary, scalable, sustainable, evidence-based, six adjectives that assert a quality without any of them naming a thing that could lack it. And meaningless words, which is where the sentence lives: stakeholder, engagement, transformation, initiative, words whose referent is agreed by nobody, so that the sentence can be neither confirmed nor contradicted by anything that subsequently happens in any clinic anywhere.

Now the essay's real test, the one from the first chapter. If a stranger wanted to find out whether that paragraph was true, what would they look at? There is nothing. No number, no place, no patient, no person who would be embarrassed if it failed, no description of what failure would even consist of. It cannot be wrong. And here is the part that makes the sixth specimen worse than Orwell's five: it was not produced by a hack on a platform. It was produced, in every real case the constructed one is built from, by an intelligent clinician who had a specific and checkable plan, a trial, a clinic, a population, a number of patients, an outcome that could be counted, and who translated it into that paragraph because that paragraph is the register the funding body's forms are written in, and because a committee reading forty applications will accept it without comment. The register did the concealing. The applicant signed. And a paragraph that cannot be wrong is, on the essay's argument, a paragraph in which nobody was thinking, which the applicant would deny, correctly, about the plan, and could not deny about the paragraph.

## Chapter Seven: What the Shelf Owes This Essay

I should say plainly that this series is downstream of the essay in a way that no other book on the shelf can claim. The house charter for these scripts contains a list of banned phrases, and the list is an Orwellian document in the exact sense: a catalogue of the pre-assembled moves that let a narrator produce sentences he did not think. The aim is not plainness. Some of the best sentences on this shelf are long and Latinate. The aim is to catch the phrase that grades its own claim, the one that announces an insight as central or a sentence as structural before the listener has heard it, because a phrase like that is one the writer did not have to earn: an operator in Orwell's sense, a verbal false limb that tells the listener what to feel about a claim instead of showing him the claim and letting him feel it. Every episode of this feed is run through a list of those before it is published, and the list has grown as the listener has caught more of them, the essay's method applied to itself. Orwell said you would find the faults in his essay if you looked back through it. They are in this one too, and the difference is only that the looking is now done by a script, a fact about twenty twenty-six rather than about the argument.

And there is one place the essay's argument bites harder in a hospital than anywhere Orwell ever thought about, and it is the reason a clinician should read it. Medicine's register was built for the honest agentless sentence, the sample was centrifuged, and the register has been colonised by the dishonest one, appropriate measures were instituted, without the language changing at all. The same passive, the same Latin, the same institutional calm, and one of them is a method and the other is a defence. The only way to tell them apart is the question the essay hands you: can this be checked, and if not, who benefits from that. Every serious adverse event review I have ever read was an exercise in that question, and the sentences that had to be taken apart were never the ungrammatical ones. They were the fluent ones.

## Chapter Eight: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

The verdict is that this is a flawed essay carrying an intact argument, and that it should be read for the argument and against the rules. Read the whole thing, not the excerpt; it is five thousand words and takes half an hour. Read Pullum on the passive and Poole on the intolerances the same day, and grant them everything, and then read the essay's middle section again, the list that runs from pacification to elimination of unreliable elements, and ask whether anything the critics said touched it. It did not. Do not pin the six rules to the wall. Pin the question.

The dose is diagnostic, and it is cheaper than anything else on the shelf, because the instrument is a single question and it takes three seconds. This week, take one sentence from your own writing, a note, a report, a reply to a complaint, a paragraph in a grant, and ask of it: if a stranger wanted to find out whether this is true, what would they look at? If there is an answer, a date, a name, a number, a document, the sentence has an address and it can be wrong, and being able to be wrong is the only way a sentence can also be right. If nothing comes, if the sentence is true in the way that appropriate measures were instituted is true, then you have found the soft snow, and the useful discovery is not that you were lying. It is that you did not have to decide anything to produce it. The register decided, and you signed.

And the question for Monday is the one the essay asks of the tired hack and the shelf asks of every institution it has read: at what point in the sentence did the person stop being present? Find the phrase in your own organisation's official prose, the strategic plan, the incident report, the all-staff email, at which a person choosing words was replaced by an orthodoxy producing them, and then ask what that phrase was protecting the writer from having to see. It will be something. It was something in nineteen forty-six. Orwell's claim, which the linguists have not answered because they were never really addressing it, is that the something is where the politics is, and that the language was built to cover it, softly, like snow.
