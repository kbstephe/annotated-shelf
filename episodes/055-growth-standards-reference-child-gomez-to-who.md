## Chapter One: A Fraction of a Boston Child

In the early 1950s, a paediatrician at the Children's Hospital in Mexico City, Federico Gómez, was trying to answer a question that sounds easy until you try it. Of the babies who arrive on a ward looking thin and miserable, which ones are going to die? Gómez had an unglamorous instrument, a scale, and an unglamorous method, which was to weigh each child, look up what a child of that age ought to weigh, and divide one by the other. The answer came out as a percentage, and he cut the percentages into bands. A child at seventy-six to ninety percent of the expected weight was malnourished to the first degree. Sixty-one to seventy-five was the second degree. Sixty or below was the third. When he and his colleagues published the result in 1956, in the Journal of Tropical Pediatrics, the pattern in the hospital's records was the one a clinician would hope for and a statistician would admire: the deeper the degree, the more of the children died. The third degree carried a large share of the ward's deaths, and the first degree very few.

The classification spread through the world's wards and health ministries within a decade, and the World Health Organization eventually reprinted the paper as a public health classic. Its virtue was that anyone with a scale and a printed table could use it. A nurse in a village clinic needed no calculation beyond long division.

Look at the denominator, though, since the narrator of this series cannot leave a denominator alone. The expected weight in Gómez's formula was not what a Mexican child weighed, or a healthy child, or the median child on Gómez's own ward. It was the fiftieth percentile of a chart from Harvard's school of public health, drawn from American children measured in Boston in the early decades of the century. A Mexican infant was thin if he fell short of a fraction of a Boston infant. Gómez himself was aware of this; the sources I could reach describe his working figures as a "theoretical weight," and the Mexico City hospital's own children offered no better yardstick because most of them were poor. The weak point was not carelessness. There was no alternative on the shelf, and the Boston curve at least described someone who had been fed.

That detail is why this episode exists. The nutrition series has spent seven episodes on how scientists found causes: a lime, a husk of rice, a corn-based diet, a starvation ward in Minnesota, a protein syllogism, a table of fats and sugar, a ledger of Hertfordshire babies. All of those were arguments about why a body goes wrong. Every one of them assumed that somebody could say, before the argument started, that a body had gone wrong, which means somebody had a line. Item eight asks who drew the line and on what children. The question predates Gómez and has outlived three attempts to answer it, and the answer decides, in a very direct way, how many children in a given country are counted as sick this year.

## Chapter Two: The Trouble With Percentages

Gómez's scheme had a problem that took the field about two decades to say aloud. A weight is not a disease. It is a position in a distribution, and the reference tells you what the distribution looks like. A percentage of the median, the arithmetic Gómez used, makes an assumption about that distribution that nobody had checked, which is that a fixed fraction of the middle means the same thing at every age.

Here is a constructed example, invented to show the arithmetic and nothing else. Imagine a reference in which ten-month-old babies have a median of ten kilograms and a spread, what statisticians call a standard deviation, of one kilogram. A baby at that fraction weighs eight kilograms, which sits two spreads below the middle. Now imagine that at twenty-four months the median is twelve kilograms and the spread is one and a half. The two-year-old on the same line weighs nine and six tenths, which is only one and a half spreads below the middle. Same percentage, different degree of unusualness. The younger baby is rarer in the reference population than the toddler, and a ward that applies one line to both treats them as equals. The effect runs the other way for height and for weight-for-height, where the spread changes in its own pattern. A percentage of the median is a ruler whose inches are different lengths at different ages, and a national prevalence computed with it depends partly on the age mix of the children who happened to be weighed.

The fix was a distinction as much as a calculation. In 1972, John Waterlow, who spent his career at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine and in Jamaica, published in the British Medical Journal a proposal that split the single Gómez number into two. Weight against age confuses two different states, he argued. A child can be short from years of underfeeding, or thin from weeks of starvation, and Gómez's table gave both children the same grade. Waterlow's remedy was to measure height against age, which he read as a picture of the past, and weight against height, which he read as a picture of the present. A child who was short but not thin had been stunted and was now eating adequately, or at least stably. A child who was thin but of normal height had wasted recently. A child who was both had a history and a crisis. The papers' own bands were still percentages of a reference median, in the Boston tradition. Stunting, the height deficit, began at a shortfall of five percent and ran down through ten and fifteen. Wasting began at ten percent below the expected weight for height and deepened in steps of ten.

The point of splitting the two was practical, and the evidence it could produce was different in kind. A weight-for-age classification lumps together a district where children grow slowly for years and a district hit by a failed harvest. Waterlow's two axes separate the chronic from the acute, and they separate the interventions that follow. Seasonal food shortages produce wasting that reverses; chronic deprivation produces stunting that mostly does not. The Hertfordshire ledgers of the last episode were weight at birth and weight at one year, and the whole argument about what they predicted turned on exactly which of those two states the numbers stood in for. Waterlow's grid is why people in nutrition now say stunting and wasting as two words rather than malnutrition as one.

In 1977, Waterlow and colleagues went after the percentage itself. In the Bulletin of the World Health Organization they argued for presenting a group's measurements as standard deviation scores, what everybody now calls z-scores: the distance of the child from the reference median, counted in units of the reference's own spread. In the invented example above, the first baby has a z-score of minus two and the second minus one and a half, and no one could mistake them for equivalents. The z-score does for different ages what a percentage cannot, because it carries the reference's spread with it. A z-score of minus two means roughly the same thing, a child lighter than about forty-six in a thousand of the reference children, at six months, at two years and at four. That is the property a prevalence needs if it is to be compared across ages, across countries and across decades.

The fix repaired the arithmetic and little else. It left the reference alone. A z-score is as good as the population whose median and spread it borrows, and a flawless computation still inherits whatever was odd about the children behind it. The next two chapters follow what happened when the reference itself got examined.

## Chapter Three: Yellow Springs

By the end of the 1970s, the standard reference for the world's undernourished children was a set of charts produced by the United States National Center for Health Statistics, published by Hamill and colleagues in 1979 and based on a task force convened in the mid-seventies. It was the thing every child's growth card, every survey in Africa and Asia, every ministry's malnutrition table was measured against. The World Health Organization recommended it internationally in the late 1970s, with the reasoning that a single common reference would at least let different countries compare their numbers. The reasoning was sensible, and the reference was an American-made object with American limits.

Those limits come from where its data came from. For ages two to eighteen, the charts drew on national health surveys that sampled the United States as a whole. For the youngest children, birth to thirty-six months, the national surveys had little to offer, and the task force turned to a single study: the Fels Longitudinal Study, run by a research institute in Yellow Springs, Ohio, which had been enrolling babies and measuring them as they grew since 1929. The description in the later US critiques is blunt: the Fels data came from a single longitudinal study of primarily formula-fed, white, middle-class infants in a limited area of southwestern Ohio, collected between 1929 and 1975.

Read that sentence as a list of design choices nobody made on purpose. Formula-fed: in the middle decades of the twentieth century the American middle class bottle-fed its babies in large numbers, and a cohort followed across those decades learned to grow as bottle-fed babies grow. White and middle-class: the reference child came from a narrow stratum of one country's ancestry and of its access to food and medicine. Ohio: one geography. Visits came at three-month intervals through the first year, too sparse for the monthly curves the charts promised. The study's length measurements were taken lying down, while the older children were measured standing, so that when a child crossed the line between the two at twenty-four to thirty-six months, the percentile jumped on the chart for reasons that had nothing to do with the child. A baby who was growing perfectly normally could appear to lose ground, or gain it, at the age when the two datasets were sewn together.

The reference stayed in use for nearly three decades, which means a generation of public-health numbers was built on it. When someone says that a third of the children in a country were underweight in 1990, that sentence was computed against Yellow Springs, and a careful reader should ask what the proportion would have been against another yardstick.

There was an argument on the other side, and it should get its best form. In 1974, five years before the American charts were published, Jean-Pierre Habicht and colleagues, among them Reynaldo Martorell, had published in the Lancet a paper whose title asked whether ethnic differences in growth potential were relevant to height and weight standards for preschool children. Their answer, as the later literature summarizes it, was no: that when children from well-off families in several countries were compared, their growth was much more alike than the growth of rich and poor children within a single country, so that most of the gap between a Guatemalan village and an American suburb was a gap in food and infection, and only a small part of it was a gap in genes. This is the intellectual license for the whole project of an international reference. If the differences between populations are mostly environment, then the sensible thing is to pick a well-nourished population, call it the norm, and measure every other child by how far the world's circumstances have kept him from it. A Guatemalan child falling short of the Ohio curve was not a different kind of child. He was an under-fed child.

I think that argument was right about its main claim and misled about its implication. Right, because every subsequent comparison of well-off children has found that environment accounts for more than ancestry. Misled, because it treated the Ohio babies as the well-nourished baseline without asking what they had been fed. Habicht's argument said the right children to copy are the children with the most food. It never established that the best-fed children of the 1950s were the best-grown.

## Chapter Four: Faltering on Paper

The fault line appeared in the first six months of life, in breastfed babies. The finding, repeated in survey after survey through the 1980s, was that healthy infants who were exclusively breastfed, fed in line with the public health advice of the time, grew along the NCHS curves for a couple of months and then seemed to slide down them. In weight, the breastfed baby's growth appeared to falter from about two months onward, relative to a reference built on formula-fed Ohio infants who gained faster in that window. A mother whose baby was thriving by every clinical sign was told that her child was crossing percentile lines downward. In a clinic that took the chart seriously, that was a reason to introduce formula or solid food early, the opposite of what the same health service was telling her to do.

Michael Dibley and colleagues, writing in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition in 1987, had already examined the technical side of this. Their paper, on the interpretation of z-score indicators derived from the international reference, took the NCHS data seriously enough to ask what a z-score of minus two meant when applied to a population that did not resemble the reference. It meant what the reference said it meant only if the population's own distribution was a shifted copy of the reference. In poor populations, the reference's problems at the youngest ages and at the transition from lying to standing produced shapes in the data that were artefacts of the curve, not of the children.

By the 1990s a view had formed, in Geneva and among the people who used the numbers, that the problem called for a different kind of object. The language they settled on was a distinction. A reference describes how some children grew, at some time, somewhere. A standard prescribes how children should grow when circumstances do not interfere. The NCHS curves were a reference: a snapshot of what American children, mostly bottle-fed and mostly white, had done in the twentieth century. What was wanted was a standard, a statement about the biology of children given a good start, and the sample for it had to be chosen with the standard's purpose in mind rather than inherited from a convenient dataset.

The whole intellectual content of the revolution sits in that distinction. A reference answers a statistical question: where does this child sit among those who were measured? A standard answers a normative one: where should this child be? The first is a measurement, and it fails only by being badly done. The second is a claim about what a healthy childhood is, and it can mislead in ways that statistics cannot detect, because the choice of whom to measure is the claim. When a curve is built from children who were all exclusively breastfed, whose mothers did not smoke, who were born at term, and who lived in households that had enough, the curve says something that the older reference did not and could not: this is what the species does when it is not being interfered with. Whether that is true for every child on earth is exactly what the argument in the final chapters is about.

## Chapter Five: Six Cities

The World Health Organization began in 1997 the study that would replace the American curves, and it ran until 2003. The WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study, whose title kept the word "reference" while the product ended up called a standard, followed children in six sites: Pelotas in Brazil, Accra in Ghana, South Delhi in India, Oslo in Norway, Muscat in Oman, and Davis in California. Eight thousand four hundred and forty children were enrolled in total. Two designs were run side by side. A longitudinal study followed newborns from birth to twenty-four months, with frequent measurements. A cross-sectional study measured children between eighteen and seventy-one months, to be joined to the first.

What made it a standard and not another reference was the filter. A child entered the study only if circumstances favoured growth: a mother who did not smoke, a single term birth, no serious illness, a household with enough money and health care that poverty was not holding the child back, and above all feeding that followed the recommendations, which meant breastfeeding for at least four months, with solid foods introduced by about six months, and continued breastfeeding after. Families who did not meet the conditions were not in the sample, and in the longitudinal arm mothers got counselling to keep to the feeding plan. The population being measured is, deliberately, the population in which the hypothesis "all children grow alike given a fair start" is most likely to come out true. If the hypothesis were false even there, it would have shown up as differences between the six sites that no amount of selection could remove.

The result, published in 2006 in the report on linear growth and elsewhere, was that the sites looked strikingly alike. Only about three percent of the variability in length was due to differences among the sites. About seventy percent was variation among individual children, and the rest was variation within a child over time. If you remove any one country from the pooled data, the curves barely move. Between-site differences did exist, but they were small beside the differences between a tall child and a short one in the same village. On those grounds the investigators pooled the six sites into one set of curves and released them on the twenty-seventh of April, 2006, as the World Health Organization's child growth standards.

The new curves shifted numbers in the direction that the faltering story would predict. Compared with the NCHS reference, the standard's weight curve in the first six months sat higher, because breastfed children in the study grew faster in the first weeks than NCHS expected, and the standard's curve lower in the second half of infancy and later, because the breastfed children then grew more slowly than the formula-fed Ohio babies had. The healthy breastfed baby that looked as though it was faltering against the old chart tracked along its z-score line against the new one. At the same time, because the new curves put the typical child taller and leaner in the age range where children are most often malnourished, prevalence figures rose. Stunting was higher at every age. Wasting, and especially severe wasting, was much higher in the first half of infancy, and afterward the severe category stayed one and a half to two and a half times as common as under the old curves. Underweight rose during the first six months and then fell. A country that had counted its problem against Yellow Springs, and now counted it against Pelotas, Accra, Delhi, Oslo, Muscat and Davis, woke up to a different number without a single extra child having gone hungry.

That sentence needs its second half. The children were no hungrier than before, and the new numbers were probably closer to the truth, since the old curves had been built from babies fed in a way that the health service no longer recommended. But a ministry that has told its donors for fifteen years that its severe wasting rate is two percent is not delighted to learn it was five. The change in the yardstick was a scientific improvement and an administrative shock at once, and the organizations that run treatment programmes had to reckon with the second.

The reckoning appears in a document that is easy to skip. In 2009, the World Health Organization and UNICEF issued a joint statement on how to identify severe acute malnutrition, now that the NCHS reference was gone. The older definition had counted a child as severely wasted below seventy percent of the median weight for height on the American reference, or below three standard deviations. The statement kept the three-deviation line, now measured against the new standard, and endorsed a second, independent criterion: an upper-arm circumference below one hundred and fifteen millimetres, raised by five millimetres, because it predicts death well. A child qualifies by either. The statement is dry, and it is also a piece of administrative translation, because what it did was keep treatment programmes' eligibility roughly steady while the curve beneath them moved. A tape measure around the arm needs no reference table at all.

## Chapter Six: The Case Against One Curve

The objection to a single international standard has two forms, one empirical and one about meaning.

The empirical form says that the six sites were not as alike as the headline figure suggests, and that the figure was selected for. This is the argument of Harshpal Sachdev, a paediatric nutrition researcher at the Sitaram Bhartia Institute in New Delhi, set out in a 2024 debate in a BMJ journal on whether one standard should be used to judge the nutritional status of children under five worldwide. His case rests on two sources. One is the study's own data. By his account, linear growth differed between sites by over two tenths of a z-score unit in a bit more than a third of the comparisons across sites and ages, and by over three tenths in about a fifth of them; India against Brazil, for instance, differed by three to six tenths at ages two to four years, and Oman against Brazil by six to seven tenths. The other is a systematic review by Valerie Natale and Anuradha Rajagopalan, published in BMJ Open in 2014, that gathered published anthropometric means from many countries, fifty-five countries and some eleven million children by the figures Sachdev cites, and compared them to the WHO curves. About a fifth of the length-for-age means in the reviewed populations sat half a deviation or more from the WHO mean, and nearly half sat at least a quarter away. For head circumference the gaps were larger still. Sachdev's argument is that the window the WHO accepts for pooling, half a standard deviation either way, is wide when set beside the effect of any intervention. A nutrition trial that moves a population's length-for-age by a tenth or two tenths of a unit is called a success. If countries differ by half a unit, he reasons, then a good part of what the standard calls a deficit is a different baseline.

The second part of his argument is about the Indian sample in particular: the South Delhi children were drawn from privileged households, and in his view a curve built from the top of a very unequal society says as much about the privilege as about the species. He adds that curves based on local children predict later metabolic risk better, a claim I cannot assess from what I have read and so will only report.

Now the response. Borghi, writing for the standard's side in the same debate, made the defence in two moves, as I read it. First, a standard exists to expose deficits that a population has stopped seeing. If Indian children are measured against Indian children, the large share of them whose mothers were stunted in turn by hungry grandmothers become the norm, and the figure of stunting falls without a single child eating differently. Second, a contextual reference risks hiding the burden and, in her phrase, disincentivizing the interventions the burden demands. A health ministry given a curve that makes its children look normal has a reason to stop worrying.

Both are serious, and they do not contradict each other as sharply as the debate format makes them look. They are about different uses of a number. Take a question the standard handles well: is this particular two-year-old in this village's clinic growing badly? For that, an arbitrary choice of a good-childhood curve is fine, since either curve will flag the child who is falling through every line, and the child at minus three on either is in trouble. Take a different question: how much of this country's stunting could be removed by food, sanitation and medicine? There the standard answers in a way that is probably biased upward, because some fraction of the gap is ancestry, and the plain count of children below minus two will include them. Natale and Rajagopalan are, in effect, saying the second kind of number is shakier than the first. The Habicht result says the fraction is small. Neither is wrong about the other's range of validity.

I part from both camps on one thing. The debate treats the small between-site share as the hinge, and it is not. That share is a statement about between-site differences among privileged, breastfed children, which is a population where the hypothesis was hard to falsify, so its smallness is partly manufactured by the filter. That does not make it uninformative. A finding that the filter's removal of poverty also removed most of the variation between continents is a finding that poverty drives most of it. What it cannot say is what the residue means for a given country, because the sample was never built to estimate it. The only experiment that would is a trial of children in the places in question, given everything the six sites had, followed to two years. Such studies exist in pieces. The largest effects on length from nutrition and sanitation trials in poor settings have been small against the half standard deviation at issue, and the gap between a poor child and the standard has not closed even in the trials, which is itself evidence either that environment has not been fixed sufficiently or that the gap includes something the interventions do not touch. That ambiguity is the real state of knowledge, and nobody ought to speak of it as settled in either direction.

The steelman for the standard goes further than Borghi did. A growth curve whose sample was chosen for optimal conditions has the property that the same procedure can be run again anywhere, so a country that wants a locally trusted number can build its own sample of well-off, breastfed children and find out if its curve differs. That is a testable claim, and it has been tested in several places, in Indian and other national studies of children from affluent homes. When those studies find that the well-off children of a country sit close to the standard, as several do, the genetic explanation loses ground. When they do not, the explanation gains it. The standard's critics and defenders can both cite studies; what they cannot do is dismiss the method.

## Chapter Seven: Extending the Idea Backward and Forward

Once the logic of a prescriptive standard was in hand, it was applied outside the age range it had been built for. In 2007, the World Health Organization released a reference for children and adolescents aged five to nineteen, reconstructed from the old NCHS data with the effect of America's rising childhood fatness statistically trimmed off so that overweight did not leak into the norm. The label there is reference, honestly, since no one ran a six-city study of ten-year-olds.

At the other end, the INTERGROWTH-21st project, led from Oxford, reached back to the fetus and the newborn. In eight geographically defined populations where mothers' health care and nutrition were adequate, it followed pregnancies and produced prescriptive standards for fetal growth, for newborn weight, length and head circumference by gestational age and sex, and for the postnatal growth of preterm babies, to be used until sixty-four weeks of postmenstrual age, after which the WHO child standards take over. This is the same wager made at a younger age: that healthy fetuses grow alike, and that a curve built on them tells you what a baby ought to weigh at thirty-four weeks.

It has met the same objections in more clinical form. Some obstetric groups have argued that the standard flags too few babies as small for gestational age and too many as large, about four to five percent of a population under the tenth centile, and over a fifth above the ninetieth, according to the critique on the perinatal institute's pages. Whether that is a defect of the standard or an honest finding that more babies are large than the old charts supposed is a question that neither the WHO nor the INTERGROWTH approach can answer from inside. A prescriptive standard that reports a population as mostly oversized is either exposing a problem or has been built on the wrong women.

The matter of preterm babies is smaller in scale and harder in principle. A premature infant's growth in the neonatal unit is not the growth of a fetus of the same age, because the fetus is inside a placenta and the infant is on a ventilator, and the question of which curve a nurse should plot against is a question about what the clinician is trying to achieve. A reference says what happened to others. A standard says what should happen. A third thing, a target, says what the unit believes it can accomplish, and nothing in this history gives a basis for thinking the three coincide.

## Chapter Eight: What the Curve Is For

Three episodes ago, the series followed Donald McLaren's doubt about the protein story, and what carried it was that a diagnosis hardens when a measurement is attached to it. Growth standards are where that hardening is made official. A line at minus two or minus three converts a continuous distribution into a category, the category becomes a budget line, and the budget line trains a generation of programmes to look for the thing the threshold measures. The line itself is not arbitrary. Minus three is where the risk of dying climbs steeply, and the evidence for that is large and strong. Even so, a z-score line is a decision about where to put a limited number of therapeutic sachets, with an arithmetic form that makes it sound discovered.

The Hertfordshire episode left a related puzzle. When Barker's critics argued about whether a birth weight in a ledger caused a heart attack sixty years later, the effect that survived every adjustment was that early undernutrition shows up as a shorter, less schooled, lower-earning adult, and the Victora paper pooled five birth cohorts from low- and middle-income countries and used exactly this tool, height-for-age at two years, as its measure of early deprivation. The WHO standard is what makes that cross-country pooling possible. Without a common ruler, five cohorts cannot be set side by side, and the claim that a child who is short at two pays for it in classrooms and wages cannot be made across countries. A ruler with a questionable origin is still a ruler. It has enabled a body of work, and it has also fixed in advance which children count as the measure.

My own verdict is a split one, in the pattern of the previous episode. The prescriptive standard won its central argument, with the best evidence available: the original reference was wrong where it could be checked, built on formula-fed babies from a single study, and the new one is a better description of a healthy start. That small share is the strongest single number in the case, and also the one most often asked to carry more than it can. What remains open is whether the width of the permitted window, half a standard deviation either way across populations, is small enough that the standard's prevalence for a given country can be taken as a count of avoidable deficit. I would trust the standard for the clinic and for comparing a country with itself over time, and I would hold the cross-country league tables loosely. The numbers answer to the filter as much as to the children.

As for how to read this literature: start with the Gómez paper's table, because everything afterward is a repair to it, then Waterlow's 1977 piece on standard deviation scores for the arithmetic, and the multicentre study's methodology paper for the filter, which is where the entire argument lives. Skip any summary that says a country has a stunting rate without saying against which curve.

The generative question for the working week is for anyone who has used a threshold. Whenever a threshold sorts people into sick and well, treated and untreated, funded and unfunded, ask what population it was calibrated on, what that population was fed or exposed to or paid, and what the threshold would be if it had been calibrated on a different one. If the answer is not in the paper, the threshold is a decision, and someone made it.
