title: "From Third World to First (Lee), Part One: what Singapore's workers gave up"
description: "Lee Kuan Yew's memoir of the years after 1965, read through its army, the British withdrawal, the foreign factories, the 1968 labour laws, the pension fund and the flat: what each citizen was asked to surrender, and what was put in their name in return."
---

In December 1965, four months after Singapore became a country, the prime minister was about to drive to the opening of its first Parliament. A Malaysian brigadier named Alsagoff, who commanded the Malaysian brigade still stationed on the island, called on him and insisted that his own motorcycle outriders escort the car. Lee Kuan Yew describes the brigadier as acting like the commander in chief of an army in Singapore, and he describes his own reasoning in one line: if he told the man off, the report would go to Kuala Lumpur and the Malaysians would find another way to show him who held real power. So the ceremonial opening of the Republic's Parliament was escorted by the army of the country it had just left.

He let it happen. That is the book's first habit, and it recurs: a man with almost no cards, who knows exactly how few he has, and who spends one visible concession to keep the rest hidden.

The book is From Third World to First, published in 2000, the second volume of Lee's memoirs, covering the thirty years after separation. It runs to about seven hundred and fifty pages and, as he says in the preface, it is arranged by theme rather than by date, and it is not a how-to book on building an economy, an army or a nation. I held the full text, so everything described here is from the pages. I read no reviews of it, and where I raise objections they are the ones the book's own pages invite, plus the obvious ones.

This is the first of two episodes. The question for this one is a plain one: what did Singapore's government ask its citizens to give up in these years, and what did it put in their hands in exchange? The next episode takes the second half of the ledger, the things that were harder to put a price on. Here we stay with work, wages, savings and housing, because the book is strongest there, and because the pattern in them is more interesting than the story of the man.

## Chapter One: Outriders

The army first, because the brigadier's motorcycles show how the army began. At independence the island had two infantry regiments of roughly a thousand men each, and both were under Malaysian command. Malaysia had placed seven hundred Malaysians in them and posted three hundred Singaporean soldiers out to units across the border. The police were mostly Malays from the mainland as well. Lee's worry was a coup: that Malay hard-liners in Kuala Lumpur, who thought Singapore should have been forced to stay, would persuade the brigadier that reversing separation was his patriotic duty. The brigadier, with his brigade on the island, could have arrested the whole cabinet. So the government kept what Lee calls a quiet, non-challenging posture while building something behind it.

The something was assembled by Goh Keng Swee, the finance minister, who took defence on and whose military training, by Lee's account, was a stint as a corporal in a volunteer corps that surrendered in February 1942. Lee wrote to the prime minister of India and to President Nasser asking for military advisers. Both replied with good wishes and ignored the request. So Goh went to the Israelis, who arrived in small groups from November 1965 and were called Mexicans in public, because they looked swarthy enough and because open Israeli help would have inflamed Malay Muslim opinion on both sides of the causeway.

Then came a fight over a camp. The Singapore battalion returning from duty in Borneo was due to take back its barracks, and the Malaysian regiment sitting in them would not leave. The returning troops lived under canvas at Farrer Park, and Goh warned Lee that soldiers in tents with poor latrines might mutiny, and that a brigadier with a grievance to exploit was a risk. Lee moved his family into the Istana grounds with a company of Gurkhas standing by. The Malaysians shifted to another camp in March 1966 and did not leave Singapore until November 1967. Lee's own reading is that the Malaysians' unreasonableness only made the government more determined.

The other army problem was arithmetic of a different kind. On the first of February 1966 a major told recruits at a training depot to separate by race, discharged the Malays, and a riot followed. Goh had ordered recruitment frozen after learning that eighty per cent of recent recruits were Malay, and the commander had misread the order. Lee went to the police building that evening with a loudhailer and spoke to the detained men in Malay. He told them the major had misunderstood: only Singapore citizens were eligible, and Malays who were citizens would report back the next day. Ten ringleaders would be charged; the rest could go home. He gives the reasoning: if all of them were held, some three hundred and sixty-five families would miss their sons that night, and rumours of Malays being oppressed would be across the island by morning. He asked the British high commissioner to have British troops ready, since the police and army would sympathise with the rioters.

Look at what is being built. The state in its first months has to hold together a force whose composition is the problem, in a population that is three-quarters Chinese. The solution was not to dissolve the problem but to convert it, slowly, into a citizenship test and a draft. By 1968 the government was buying French light tanks from the Israelis, thirty in June 1969 and forty-two more in September, with a hundred and seventy armoured vehicles. Four years after it could not escort its own prime minister, the republic had a tank force, bought at a discount from a seller that was upgrading.

## Chapter Two: A Heart Without a Body

The army was the first problem and the economy was the one that frightened him. Singapore had been a trading post that the British turned into a hub, and Lee's phrase for what it inherited is that it was a heart without a body. The wider area that the British had governed as one unit was breaking into national pieces that each wanted to bypass the port. Indonesia was confronting Singapore and trade with it had stopped. Malaysia intended to deal directly with its own trading partners through its own ports. Unemployment was alarming and rising. The Dutch economic adviser Albert Winsemius told him in 1965 that without a common market or Indonesian trade it would pass fourteen per cent by the end of 1966, and that Singapore was walking on a razor's edge.

The government's first responses are the most instructive pages in the economic chapter, because they are a list of failures, and Lee tells them that way. An economic planner seconded by India handed over a thick report built on a common market with Malaysia. Lee scanned the summary, saw the assumption, thanked him and never read it again. A trade delegation was sent to Africa on the off-chance of picking up some business, and little followed. A soft-drink manufacturer suggested tourism, which was labour-intensive and needed little capital, and a tourist board was set up. It created jobs and reduced the unemployment problem without solving it. Local factories were encouraged to make vegetable oil, mosquito coils, hair cream and mothballs.

Then the larger errors. The Jurong industrial estate was empty in spite of the money spent on its roads and services. The Economic Development Board went into a joint venture to recycle paper with a businessman who had no manufacturing experience, and another into ceramics with no technical knowhow, and both failed. A shipbuilding venture with a Japanese heavy-industry firm built sixteen vessels of one type and three tankers and then stopped, because the island made neither steel plate nor engines and imported both, while repair work, where the labour content was high, paid. In January 1968 the chairman of Marks and Spencer saw Lee on British television, looked at him, judged him forlorn, and offered the idea that Chinese had nimble fingers and Singapore could make fish-hooks and trout lures. Nothing came of that offer, though a Norwegian firm later opened a fish-hook factory that employed several hundred.

Picture a government in its third year after separation trying tourism, mothballs, paper, ceramics and ships, none a strategy, all a search. The strategy that came out of it is the object of the next chapter, but the point to take from it now is that Lee is candid that it was found by elimination. A man who will say his development board failed at ceramics can be trusted a little further on the numbers elsewhere.

## Chapter Three: The Bill From London

While the search went on, the ground was being removed. In October 1966 Lee and Goh asked the British defence secretary, Denis Healey, to sell them a squadron of Hawker Hunter fighters. Healey laughed, wagged a finger and said British forces would look after them. They left London reassured. Three months before, Lee had learned from Harold Wilson that Britain might take away half of its fifty thousand troops guarding the region, and in the spring of 1966 he had given Wilson his estimate of what the British bases meant to Singapore: about twenty per cent of the gross domestic product. Later in the book the number is given more fully: over thirty thousand jobs directly, another forty thousand in support services.

What follows in the chapter is a year and a half of being told two things at once. In July 1967 a British defence white paper announced a halving of forces by 1970 or 1971 and complete withdrawal by the mid-seventies. In November of that year, on a Sunday, Britain devalued the pound from two dollars eighty to two dollars forty. Singapore had kept its reserves in sterling, partly because it did not want to be blamed for precipitating a devaluation while British troops defended it from Indonesia. The loss was fourteen point three per cent of those reserves, which Lee totalled in a letter to Wilson as a hundred and fifty-seven million Singapore dollars. Healey told Parliament that the cuts meant no acceleration. Lee wrote to thank him, and then writes in the memoir, in the flat voice of someone recording his own mistake, that he was wrong, because Healey could not speak for the government. In January 1968 the cabinet decided to leave by 1971.

Lee flew to London. At Downing Street on a Sunday evening he made the argument that confidence, not aid, was the issue: investors from Hong Kong would take their money elsewhere if they heard that all British troops would be off the mainland of Asia by 1971, and the housing and barracks the British owned, worth more than fifty-five million pounds, would not fetch half their value if sold in three years. Wilson replied that the decision was economic and would not change. George Brown, whom Lee had met in Stockholm two years before and who had then wanted to pull out as fast as possible, asked how much time Lee needed. Lee said the thirty-first of March 1973. He did not get it. The date moved only from March to December 1971.

The second half of the chapter is about what Lee refused. He had visited Malta in 1967, after the closure of the Suez Canal had stopped the ships that its dockyard repaired. The workers, on full pay, were playing water polo in a dry dock that they had filled with water. The British had given generous redundancy payments and retraining, which Lee describes as nurturing a sense of dependency rather than a spirit of self-reliance. He came home and said in Parliament that a Singapore had thrived before the bases existed and could thrive after them. He told workers that the world does not owe them a living.

It could pass for a speech made in a hard time. It also governed what happened next. He took British aid, a package of fifty million pounds, a quarter in grants and three quarters in loans, half spent on British defence equipment, and he held down the negotiating friction so that the handover of land, buildings and floating docks would be done in good humour. The British held fifteen thousand acres, eleven per cent of the island, and land needed for economic or defence purposes was to be handed over without charge; the logic of the conversion was to turn each site into somewhere that employed people. The naval dockyard went to a British shipbuilding firm to run on a five-year contract, and the company that grew from it was later listed as a conglomerate. A gurkha island became a resort. The Royal Air Force base at Changi became the airport. The military complex at Pasir Panjang became a university campus. And the thirty thousand retrenched base workers were, in his account, absorbed by the factories that came, so that by 1971 there was no unemployment and no derelict building.

## Chapter Four: The Oasis

The factories came because of a decision about who to ask. After the years of trial and error, Lee wrote, the government concluded that the best hope was American multinationals. The Hong Kong and Taiwanese entrepreneurs who had come in the sixties brought toys and garments, labour-intensive and small. American firms brought higher technology and bigger plants. They also brought something Lee names directly, which is that they believed their government was going to stay in Southeast Asia, and so their investment was safe from confiscation or war.

The academic fashion of the day said the opposite. The dependency school argued that multinationals were exploiters who locked poor countries into selling raw materials and buying finished goods. Lee's reply was an inventory, not a rebuttal: Singapore had no raw materials to exploit. It had a harbour, an English-speaking population, a clean administration, and two million people who needed work, and if foreign firms would train them, the arrangement was a good one. He and Goh, he says, could not afford to be conscripted by a theory.

On top of that he laid a second idea, to make a First World oasis in a Third World region. Public order, health, telecommunications, transport and schooling were to be held to the standards of the countries the investors came from, so that Singapore became a place where engineers and managers who had business to do in the region could live and be unbothered. The Economic Development Board was the instrument. It had been set up in 1961 on Winsemius's advice as a single agency, so that an investor would not have to deal with a string of ministries, and the story of how it worked is of young officers doing legwork. When Chan Chin Bock first called on American chief executives, they did not know where Singapore was, and he pointed it out on their globes, a dot at the tip of the Malay peninsula. The officers sometimes called on forty or fifty companies to get one to send a mission. Lee himself became part of the sales machinery: in the autumn of 1968 he addressed the Economic Club of New York, and the board's man there found afterwards that doors opened, because chief executives wanted to assess the man in charge before putting a factory under him.

That detail feeds the next two chapters. What the executives were assessing, Lee says, was political, economic and financial stability, and sound labour relations, so that nothing would stop production which supplied their customers around the world. Investors were buying a promise about the future behaviour of Singapore's workers. Somebody had to give that promise, and it could not be the investors. So the next question is what it cost the people on whose behalf the promise was made.

## Chapter Five: Hire and Fire

Between July 1961 and September 1962 there were a hundred and fifty-three strikes in Singapore, a record. In 1969, for the first time since before the war, there were none. The chapter on the unions is Lee's account of how.

He starts with an admission that the story would be unfair without. In the 1950s he had been the unions' lawyer, and the British had sent trade union advisers to wean non-communist leaders from communist influence, and the advisers had taught them how to squeeze employers for pay and benefits whatever the consequences to the company. Lee says he was responsible for some of those practices himself. His example is triple pay on public holidays, under which cleansing workers let rubbish pile up before a holiday to make sure they would be called in to clear it. His summary of the effect is capital-intensive firms, buying machines to avoid hiring, a small group of privileged union members with high pay and a growing band of underpaid and underemployed people outside. In effect the argument is that the unions as they stood protected insiders at the cost of the thirty thousand school leavers who came onto the labour market each year.

The confrontation came in a municipal strike. K. Suppiah, president of a federation of daily-rated workers, demanded a dollar a day more for fifteen thousand employees, and Lee warned him that seven thousand of the members were Indian nationals who needed work permits and could be sent home. Suppiah called the strike. On the first of February 1967 about twenty-four hundred cleansing workers walked out. The government's reply was assembled in advance. The police charged Suppiah and fourteen others with calling an illegal strike. The registrar of trade unions issued notices to show cause why the union should not be struck off. The health ministry declared that the strikers had sacked themselves, and anyone who wished to be re-employed could apply the next day. Ninety per cent applied. Two months later the cleansing union and the federation were deregistered.

Lee calls it a turning point and the account supports him. The method was coordinated firmness, each piece of the state arriving on the same morning, and its effect was on everyone who was not on strike. The government then used the moment. In 1968, after a general election in April that returned it with an overwhelming mandate and with the British announcement fresh, Parliament passed the Employment Act and amended the industrial relations law. They set minimum conditions, limited retrenchment benefits and overtime bonuses, handed hiring, firing, promotion and transfer back to management, and made a secret ballot compulsory before any strike, with the union and its officers liable to prosecution otherwise. Strikes in certain essential services were banned outright. An old union colleague in Parliament, Seah Mui Kok, objected to the width of the power to dismiss; Lee says he accepted the need for the change and added safeguards against misuse.

The results he reports for the first years are stated in plain numbers. In 1969 fifty-two new factories were built, creating seventeen thousand jobs. In 1970 new investment added twenty thousand more. In 1972 a tripartite National Wages Council began publishing yearly guidelines, under the principle that wage increases must not exceed productivity increases.

A sceptic would say that the book is describing the dismantling of the labour movement, and the sceptic would be correct in the literal sense. What the book is saying, though, goes a step further, and his candour here carries the argument. Lee writes that strict laws and tough talk alone could not have done this. The mood, he says, was altered by the prospect that the British would leave, and the people on the other side of the table accepted a hard-headed approach because they were shaken by separation and afraid. He credits, as the last piece, the trust people had in him from years of association. An outsider can take that as vanity. A more useful reading is that he is giving the mechanism: the surrender was extracted in a crisis window, and the window was real. The British announcement made it possible to do in a year what would otherwise have taken ten. He says himself that he seized that moment.

## Chapter Six: A Name on the Deed

What did the workers get? The chapter titled for a fair and not a welfare society is the answer, and it begins with what Lee, in his own account, believed in 1965. The ministers, he says, had believed in socialism and fair shares, and learnt that personal reward was needed for production. They also knew that a winner-takes-all society, like the Hong Kong of the 1960s, would offend a sense of fairness, and in Singapore a government had to face an election every five years. So the state would redistribute through things that raised citizens' earning power, such as schooling, and through housing and health, always watching for abuse and waste.

His primary preoccupation, in his words, was to give every citizen a stake in the country and its future. He wanted a home-owning society, and the reasons he gives are two and they are blunt. A completely urban electorate, he had noticed, tended to vote against whoever was in government; people with mortgages did not. And parents whose sons would do national service should have something to defend. If a soldier's family did not own its home, Lee thought, he would conclude he was fighting for the properties of the wealthy. Goh, as defence minister, was the strongest supporter among the ministers; the rest thought home ownership desirable but not vital.

The instrument was a pension scheme from the colonial period. The Central Provident Fund took a twentieth of wages from the employee, matched by the employer, to be withdrawn at fifty-five, and as a pension it was inadequate. The housing board had offered loans from 1964 and almost nobody bought, because buyers could not raise the twenty per cent down payment. In 1968 the fund's contribution rate was raised by amendment and workers were allowed to use their savings in it to pay the down payment and to service a twenty-year loan for the balance. From 1955 to 1968 the contribution had never moved. Lee raised it in stages to twenty-five per cent in 1984, for a total savings rate of fifty per cent of wages, later reduced to forty. The labour minister kept asking him to put less into the fund so that workers would carry more cash home; he regularly overruled him.

The mechanism that makes this work is how he raised the rate without a revolt. Once a worker became used to a higher take-home pay, Lee knew, he would resist any increase in his contribution that reduced it. So the National Wages Council recommended a raise each year based on the previous year's growth, and Lee raised the contribution almost every year by an amount smaller than the raise, so that take-home pay still rose. He describes it as painless, and then adds the condition: it was possible only because of high growth year after year.

Set the two chapters side by side and the shape shows. In the labour chapter the state takes away the right to strike without a ballot, the right to triple pay, the union's say in hiring and firing. In the housing chapter it takes a growing share of each pay packet. Both are taken in the same period, from the same people, and both were paid for with a promise the state could keep only if the economy grew. The promise was kept, on his numbers: the applicants for new flats rose from about three thousand in 1967 to seventy thousand in 1996, and of seven hundred and twenty-five thousand flats in 1996, only nine per cent were rented out. The remainder were owned by the people living in them.

Here is my reading of the first half of the book, as an argument rather than a summary. Each surrender in these years was matched with an asset registered to the surrendering person, and the thing surrendered was what produced the asset. The right to strike went, and the factories came and brought jobs. Part of the pay packet went, and became a deed. The two were linked in a way that the citizen could see at the kitchen table, and linked by a state that wrote the terms. A trade of that kind lasts longer than an order, because the person who holds the deed has a reason to want the arrangement to continue. Lee says in nearly so many words that this was the design.

## Chapter Seven: The Cost of the Deed

There were parts that do not fit the tidy version, and the book mentions them, which is one of the reasons to keep reading it with trust.

Take land. In 1961 a fire destroyed a squatter settlement of forty-seven acres at Bukit Ho Swee and left some sixteen thousand families without homes. Lee amended the law so that after a fire, the government could acquire the site at the price it would have fetched with squatters still on it, about a third of its market value with vacant possession. His argument in Parliament, which he quotes, was that no profit should be made from a fire, because profit would be a temptation to arson. Later he extended the logic. The government could acquire land for public purposes at its value on a fixed past date, set first at the thirtieth of November 1973, because he saw no reason private landowners should profit from increases that public infrastructure produced. The base year was moved forward over the decades toward market rates. To a landowner this is a forced sale at a price the law sets. To the state it is the reason housing could be kept cheap.

Then the people moved. The first estates replaced squatter huts that had no water, power or sanitation, and so no utility bills, with high-rise flats that had all three and a monthly bill. Lee calls it wrenching. He tells the stories anyway, of pig farmers coaxing their pigs up the stairs and families who feared the lifts, with affection and some condescension. They mark a cost with no line in any budget: a way of living that, for those who lived it, had no bills.

And the book records the state's own mistakes, which I would like to count in its favour. In 1982 to 1984 the housing board more than doubled its building at a minister's assurance that it could cope, the contractors could not, and poor workmanship surfaced years later at cost to everyone. In the early 1990s, as flat prices rose, Lee agreed to build more instead of charging a levy on the windfall profits of people selling old flats to upgrade. That fed a property bubble, which hurt more when the currency crisis arrived in 1997. Had they choked off demand in 1995, he writes, they would have been far better off. Note who is talking: the person who was running the machine, saying he yielded to popular pressure beyond the capacity to deliver.

## Chapter Eight: Against the Ledger

Now the case against, with the strongest version first. Here is the version I would defend if I were on the other side: the book is a single witness giving evidence about his own conduct. It is Lee on Lee, and where he gives motives, such as the reasons for the home-ownership scheme, there is no one to contradict him. He says himself in the preface that the book is an account of the problems he and his colleagues faced and how they solved them, which is a description that excludes the opposition. I read nothing from those who left, who were detained or who disagreed, and the second episode has to look for them.

Second, survivorship, and luck. By his own account the handover of British land and buildings ran from 1968 to 1971, before the oil crisis of 1973, in a world economy where trade was growing eight to ten per cent a year. A tight-fisted wage policy and an empty industrial estate do not look the same in a recession. The CPF increases, as he explains them, depended on growth year after year. Had the growth stopped, the take-home pay trick would have failed, and a country whose strikes had been banned would have had the worst of both.

Third, the recipe may not travel. He is clear on how much of the setting was unusual: a natural harbour on a major sea-lane, a population that spoke English as a working language, a cold war that made America want a friendly island. Hong Kong, the only comparable place, he describes as sheltered by having China for a hinterland. A reader in a landlocked, agrarian country with a hundred languages would find the lessons hard to reuse, and the book says it is not a how-to.

Fourth, the cost fell unevenly. The unionised workers in the cleansing service lost their federation. The families in squatter huts moved into a monthly bill. Landowners lost the difference between a legislated price and a market one. The people who gained, the owner-occupiers, were the same people who in a free market might have earned the same through higher wages, with the state taking the middleman's role. That is the sceptic's reading of the trade, and I cannot refute it from the book alone, since the counterfactual does not appear in the text.

What I would say in reply is narrower than a defence. The book does demonstrate something about method that does not depend on one's view of the politics. Lee decided early what he would spend and what he would not: he let a brigadier have his outriders, spent British goodwill to get airfields and docks, spent union rights to buy investor confidence, and refused to spend self-reliance on aid. Aid was the clearest case: he treated Malta's dry dock full of water as a warning about what free money does to a workforce, and he created a department under his own office to turn the British bases into working sites.

## Chapter Nine: Reading It

How to read it. The book is long, and the useful parts for this half are the first seven chapters, in the order the book gives them. The first chapter, going it alone, is short and best taken whole. The army chapter can be skimmed unless soldiers interest you. The chapters on Britain's withdrawal, the hinterland, the unions and the fair society are the working core, and the strongest reading is the book's labour chapter and its housing chapter in one sitting: the strike of the cleansing workers followed within a few pages by the story of a down payment. Skip the foreign policy chapters until you want them; they belong to a different book. Treat the passages on motive with the caution you would give to any memoir, and the passages on numbers as the part most likely to hold.

Suppose you have to ask a group to give up something it values: a protocol amendment that removes a site's discretion, say, or a shared data rule that takes away a team's first look at its own results. Lee's pattern suggests a test before the ask. Write down the asset, a thing the person will hold in their own name, that the surrender will produce, and see whether the person could point to it within a year. If the answer is a vague benefit to the group, expect the cost to be remembered longer than the benefit. If you can write a name on the deed, the ask is a trade; if you cannot, it is a levy, and levies need a crisis to pass. A reform that passes only in a crisis, with no such asset behind it, should be expected to unwind when the crisis lifts.
