## Chapter One: The Navy That Grew by Shrinking

Between nineteen fourteen and nineteen twenty-eight, the Royal Navy conducted an experiment it did not know it was running. Over those fourteen years the number of capital ships in commission fell by about two-thirds – the Grand Fleet that had faced Germany at Jutland was scrapped, treaty-limited, and laid up – and the number of officers and men shrank accordingly. The empire's naval work, by any measure a navy's work can be given, had contracted on a heroic scale. The number of Admiralty officials administering that work rose by seventy-eight percent. Dwell on the shape of that for a moment, because the shape is the discovery. It is not that the administration grew faster than the fleet, which would be ordinary sloppiness. It is that the administration's growth had no visible relationship to the fleet at all – the curve went up, smooth and confident, straight through the largest demobilization in British history, as if the ships were a rumor from another department. A magnificent navy on land, the man who found these figures called it. And he checked the pattern against the opposite case, an organization whose workload was not merely shrinking but heading toward abolition: the Colonial Office, custodian of an empire in liquidation. As the colonies went – India gone, territory after territory following – the Colonial Office grew from three hundred and seventy-two officials to one thousand six hundred and sixty-one in under twenty years, a compounding increase of five or six percent a year, indifferent to the direction of history it existed to administer. Two curves, two departments, one conclusion, and the man who drew it published it in The Economist in November of nineteen fifty-five, unsigned, in that magazine's tradition, in the driest scientific prose he could counterfeit: the growth of administration obeys laws of its own. It has a rate. The rate can be measured. And the work – this is the finding under the finding – has nothing to do with it. The officials would have multiplied at their stately five to six percent whether the fleet had doubled, halved, or sailed into myth. This series has spent eight episodes inside organizations that decide, deceive, and refuse to know things. Tonight's book is about something more elementary, the way metabolism is more elementary than thought. Before the machine can shred a squirrel or script a helpline, it must do the one thing every machine in this series has done without exception. It must grow.

## Chapter Two: The Historian Who Kept a Straight Face

The author of the finding was Cyril Northcote Parkinson, and his position, like Whyte's, was the argument's warrant. He was not a disappointed clerk or a crusading journalist; he was a naval historian of entirely respectable output – decades of scholarship on trade routes, dockyards, and the eighteenth-century sea war – holding a chair at the University of Malaya in Singapore, a man who had spent a career in archives where the Admiralty's paperwork accumulates. The essay that made him famous is a parody of exactly the kind of paper his profession wrote: a law stated in the passive voice, factors labeled with letters, a formula with coefficients, the whole apparatus of quantitative social science wrapped around the observation that officials multiply because they are officials. Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion – the opening sentence, now so proverbial that people assume it is folk wisdom, was constructed to sound like a finding from a laboratory. The joke did what jokes do: it got the essay read, then the book of nineteen fifty-seven bought, in the hundreds of thousands, by the very administrators it dissected, who displayed it on their shelves the way a cardiologist might display a jar of cholesterol. And the joke had a cost, which is the reason this episode exists and the reason the sign-off last time said what it said. Stated as comedy, the law was received as comedy. The reviewers chuckled; the ministries expanded. It is the neatest available demonstration of a mechanism this series met in the Davies episodes – an organization's genius for absorbing criticism without transmitting it anywhere that matters – except that here the absorption was cultural, and total, and the anesthetic was laughter. So the useful reading, seventy years on, is to take Parkinson at his deadpan word. Suppose it is not a joke. Suppose it is what it pretends to be: a natural law, with mechanisms. What are they?

## Chapter Three: The Two Engines

Parkinson gives two, and each one runs on individually blameless decisions – which by now the listener will recognize as this series' watermark of a real mechanism. The first he states as an axiom: an official wants to multiply subordinates, not rivals. Take a civil servant – he names him A – who finds himself overworked, or believes he does, which the first law guarantees he will, since his work has expanded to fill and then overflow his day. Three exits exist. He can resign, which forfeits pension and standing. He can ask to share the job with an equal – a B hired beside him – which is unthinkable, because B becomes the obvious comparison and the obvious successor. Or he can ask for two juniors, C and D, and this door is the only one that opens sweetly, because two juniors make A a manager: his responsibilities now, by definition, exceed what one man can hold, since three men hold them. Two, note, never one – a single junior would be a B in embryo, a co-holder of the file. There must be enough of them that each controls a fragment too small to threaten anyone. And the arithmetic never rests, because in due course C finds himself overworked – sincerely; the first law is working on him too – and the only door open to C is the door that opened for A. Seven officials, Parkinson calculates with a straight face, now do what one did, and here is the turn where the essay stops being a joke about laziness and becomes a theorem about structure: all seven are genuinely busy. That is the second engine – officials make work for one another. The seven do not divide A's old work and idle in parallel; the fragments must now be coordinated, and coordination is itself work of a special, self-replenishing kind. A draft that one man would have written and sent now circulates for concurrence: C drafts, D disputes the draft on behalf of his fragment, their juniors annotate, A reconciles – and the reconciliation is real effort, applied conscientiously, often late into the evening. The file is genuinely thick. Every hour logged against it was honestly worked. The output – one letter, roughly the letter A would have written alone fourteen months earlier – bears no relation to the input, and no one anywhere in the chain has shirked. Hold this against the shelf and the fit is disquieting. Whyte's pipeline was delivering, in exactly these years, cohorts of well-rounded men selected for their fitness to be C and D – team players, coordinators, men whose skills only exist in groups of seven. And Rao's cynical taxonomy waits at the far end: an organization whose middle grows by fragmenting responsibility into unthreatening pieces is manufacturing its Clueless layer by statute, seven sincere believers per original job, each with a title, none with the file. And the book carries a third mechanism, less quoted than the engines because it is not funny, and Parkinson knew it was not funny: he gave it a deliberately ugly clinical name, injelititis, the disease of induced inferiority. It begins when an organization admits to its senior ranks one man who combines, in Parkinson's formula, incompetence with jealousy – a chief who cannot do the work and knows it, and whose response to ability nearby is not recruitment but removal. Such a man staffs downward: he hires people safely duller than himself, who in turn hire duller still, until mediocrity is not an accident of the org chart but its organizing principle, three ranks deep and self-sealing, because by the third generation there is no one left with the standing to notice what has happened. The infected institution does not collapse; that is the diagnosis's bite. It persists, smug and busy, its officials multiplying on schedule, taking a quiet pride in how little it attempts – and Parkinson's proposed treatments escalate through the essay from transfusion to surgery until he arrives, with the courtesy still perfectly intact, at the recommendation that the terminal case be condemned like a building and its personnel scattered where they cannot reinfect one another. Jackall's readers, eight episodes back, met the mature form of the disease wearing better suits: a hierarchy in which the criterion for rising had detached from the work entirely, and attached to the comfort of the man above.

## Chapter Four: The Bicycle Shed

The book's other law has escaped even further into the language than the first, and its vehicle is a committee meeting that Parkinson invents and admits to inventing – a constructed example, flagged as constructed, which this series can therefore retell with a clear conscience. A finance committee faces three agenda items. Item one: a contract for an atomic reactor, ten million pounds. Item two: a bicycle shed for the clerical staff, three hundred and fifty pounds. Item three: the refreshments budget for the committee's own meetings, twenty-one pounds a year. The reactor is approved in two and a half minutes. Most of the members do not know what a reactor costs, or contains, or risks; the two who might understand it cannot explain their unease without a lecture nobody has the vocabulary to follow; and hesitation, at this altitude of money, reads as weakness. The bicycle shed gets forty-five minutes. Everyone has seen a shed. Everyone can hold three hundred and fifty pounds in the mind, has an opinion about aluminum roofing versus galvanized iron, and can propose a saving with genuine, checkable competence. The coffee budget – twenty-one pounds – runs an hour and a quarter and ends unresolved, referred back for further information. Parkinson states the law with his customary lethal courtesy: the time spent on any item will be in inverse proportion to the sum involved. Now put the shelf's instruments on it, because the joke is a diagram of a concept this feed built three episodes ago. The Davies series introduced Ashby's law – a controller can only control what it has the variety to match – and the committee is Ashby's law wearing minutes and a quorum. The reactor's variety exceeds the room's: the members cannot generate distinct responses to its distinctions, so the item passes through them, uncontrolled, transparent as glass. The shed matches the room's variety exactly, and so receives the full governing capacity of eleven adults. Attention is flowing, as it must, along the gradient of what the committee can process rather than what matters – and the sum of those two behaviors, viewed from outside, is an institution that rubber-stamps its catastrophes and litigates its coffee. Parkinson wrote it as farce eighteen years before anyone in this series' bibliography could name the mechanism. That a working diagnostic of variety-mismatch spent those decades filed under humor is itself a datum – the finding was received at the exact altitude of the bicycle shed, priced in the room's currency as an amusement, because that was the variety the culture had available for it. And the committee chapter has a sequel about the committee itself, built from Parkinson's own trade, English constitutional history. Governing councils, he observed, have a life cycle as regular as an organism's. A cabinet begins small – five members, perhaps – and grows, because membership is an honor that costs the chairman less to grant than to refuse; and somewhere past twenty members, on his mock-solemn calculation of a coefficient of inefficiency, stated of course to the decimal, the body crosses a threshold and quietly dies as a decision-making organ. It does not disband. It becomes ceremonial, and the real deciding migrates to a small inner group that meets beforehand over the agenda – whereupon the inner group begins, in its turn, to grow. He traced the cycle through five successive English councils of state: each born small and potent around the sovereign, each swollen with honor into an audience, each survived by a compact successor, from the Curia Regis down to the cabinet of his own day, which was by then comfortably past the fatal number. The whole apparatus of formal governance, on this reading, is a shed of nested sheds – each ring of the org chart a former decision body, preserved in amber at the size at which it stopped working.

## Chapter Five: The Case Against, and the Curves That Kept Rising

Now the steelman has run long enough, and the case against deserves its innings, because it is real. Count one: the numbers are curated. Parkinson's Admiralty and Colonial Office series are true, but they are two series, chosen for effect, with no controls – a historian's anecdotes in a lab coat, and he knew it; the lab coat was the joke. An honest account adds that later, sober attempts to test the law have kept finding its footprints: administrative headcount growing independent of enrollment in school systems, of caseload in agencies, of fleet in navies – and, in one study by a team of complexity physicists half a century on, a companion finding across nearly two hundred governments that the larger a country's cabinet, the worse its standard indicators of governance ran, which is Parkinson's committee chapter restated with error bars. The law is under-evidenced by its author and over-confirmed by his critics; the direction of seventy years of checking has been toward it, not away. Count two is heavier: the law explains growth without explaining anything else, and a growth law with no theory of function slides toward a politics. Parkinson's numbers cannot distinguish the seventh redundant official from the safety inspector hired after the fire; a reader who takes the law as identity – and its cheerful cynicism invites exactly the identity dose this series keeps flagging – graduates to the view that administration as such is parasitic, and that view has spent decades as the all-purpose solvent of every audit function some machine found inconvenient. Recall what the Davies episodes established about what organizations do with their perceiving organs, and be careful which knife you hand them. And count three is the one Parkinson's own biography files: nothing followed. The book sold like almost nothing else in its decade, its author became a global brand, lectured to the very ministries he had measured – and the curves did not so much as flinch. Comedy, it turns out, is an accountability sink of its own: the laughter acknowledges the finding, discharges the discomfort, and routes the implication nowhere. The ministries bought the book by the crate, and the purchase was the response – filed, as it were, under refreshments.

## Chapter Six: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

The verdict is easy to state and strange to hold: this is the most accurate book this series has covered per page and the least consequential per reader, and the two facts share a cause. Read it – it is short, it is genuinely funny, and its two engines and one committee will name things you have watched happen this quarter. But read it the way part two of this series learned to read Burnham, with the reception as part of the text: what happened to Parkinson's Law is a case study in the phenomenon Parkinson's Law describes, an organization-culture metabolizing its own diagnosis into merchandise. The dose, accordingly, is one read, taken seriously – which concretely means refusing the book's own tone while keeping its content, the exact inverse of what its first ten million readers did. The prescription pairs it backward with Davies, because the two books are one argument at two magnifications: Parkinson found the metabolism, Davies found the cognition, and an organization that grows without reference to its work will in due course decide without reference to its world. And the question for Monday is the cheapest experiment this series has yet proposed. Take the last meeting you sat through and price its agenda: note what the big item cost, what the small item cost, and where the minutes actually went. If the gradient ran toward the shed – and it ran toward the shed – you have measured your institution's variety the way Parkinson measured the Admiralty, from its own paperwork, and the question that follows is his real bequest: what in this organization is currently passing through us in two and a half minutes, transparent as glass, because none of us can afford to admit we cannot see it? Next time the shelf goes back a step to the book Burnham wrote after the Managerial Revolution, the one episode seven called, quietly, his best – four dead Italians and Frenchmen and a renegade German, assembled into the claim that all government, every government, is the rule of an organized minority, and that freedom survives only where minorities fight. The Machiavellians, part one. Thanks for listening.
