## Chapter One: Three Trips to a Basement

Robert Frantz is a physician at the Mayo Clinic, and in 2011 he received a request from a stranger at the National Institutes of Health. The stranger, a researcher named Christopher Ramsden, wanted to know whether anything survived of a clinical trial that Frantz's late father had run in Minnesota in the late nineteen sixties. Ivan Frantz, a professor at the University of Minnesota, had died in 2009. The trial had enrolled thousands of people. Its results appeared in 1989, sixteen years after it ended, without the autopsy findings that later proved the most awkward part of the record.

Robert Frantz looked. He went to his childhood home and searched the basement and found nothing useful. He went back and found less. On the third trip, in the far corner, he found unlabeled boxes holding reels of magnetic computer tape and reams of yellowed paper. Technicians converted formats that no machine still read, and what came out of the tape was the raw record of a randomized trial that had fed nearly ten thousand people, most of them patients in Minnesota state mental hospitals and one nursing home, one of two diets for up to fifty-six months. Because the participants lived in institutions, the investigators knew what each of them ate, meal by meal, in a way that almost no diet study before or since has managed.

That trial is called the Minnesota Coronary Experiment, and its hypothesis was the most widely believed idea in twentieth century preventive medicine: replace the saturated fat in a diet with vegetable oil, blood cholesterol falls, and heart disease follows it down. The cholesterol did fall. The heart disease did not. The people whose cholesterol fell furthest did not live longer than the people on the ordinary hospital diet, and by the recovered analysis published in the BMJ in 2016, they may have died sooner.

This hour is about the argument that trial was supposed to settle, and about why a trial of that kind, however carefully run, could never have settled it. The argument was a duel. On one side stood Ancel Keys, the Minnesota physiologist this shelf met two episodes ago starving thirty-six volunteers, who by the early nineteen sixties had put saturated fat on the cover of Time magazine as the enemy of the heart. On the other stood John Yudkin, a London professor of nutrition who believed the culprit was in the sugar bowl, and who published a popular book in 1972 with the least subtle title in the history of nutrition science. Each man had a table of country-by-country numbers. Each read his table correctly. The tables agreed with both of them, and the question this episode follows is why that was possible, and what happened when people finally went looking for a way to make the tables disagree.

## Chapter Two: A Physiologist Goes to Naples

Keys did not begin as a heart researcher. He began as a man who wanted to know what bodies could stand, in altitude chambers and at the bottom of a starvation diet, and heart disease reached him the way it reached many of his generation, as an epidemic nobody had a model for. Coronary deaths among middle-aged men in the United States had climbed through the first half of the century, and had dipped, oddly, during the war years, when meat and butter were short. Keys took the dip seriously.

In 1951 and 1952 he spent a sabbatical at Oxford, and a colleague in Italy told him that the working men of Naples rarely had heart attacks. He went to see for himself, with his wife Margaret, who ran the blood tests, and what he found in Naples was a diet built on bread, vegetables, olive oil and very little meat, and a population whose cholesterol ran low and whose heart attacks were, by American standards, scarce. It was one town, one impression. It gave him a question.

The question got its first formal answer in a paper Keys published in 1953, comparing six countries on two numbers: the share of calories that came from fat, and the death rate from what was then called degenerative heart disease. Plotted against each other, the six points climbed in a tidy line. Japan sat at the bottom left, with little fat on the plate and little heart disease in the morgue. The United States sat at the top right, with a great deal of both. Between them, the other four lined up like railings on a staircase.

Four years later a statistician at Berkeley, Jacob Yerushalmy, and New York State's health commissioner, Herman Hilleboe, published a paper that Keys never entirely lived down. They pointed out that data existed for twenty-two countries and that Keys had shown six. When they plotted all twenty-two, the tidy line dissolved into a cloud, and the association between fat and heart disease became weak. Their objection was a statistician's, and it had two layers. The selection of countries could have manufactured the staircase, and even a real association between national fat intake and national heart death would show that two things rose together, not that one caused the other. National statistics of the fifties were poor in both directions, since the reporting of causes of death differed from one country to the next, and the countries that ate the most fat were also the richest, and the longest-lived, long enough to reach the ages at which coronary disease kills.

Keys's answer to a critique of that kind was to build the study that would not have the problem, and it is the best thing about him as a scientist. The Seven Countries Study began in 1956 with a yearly grant from the United States Public Health Service of about two hundred thousand dollars. Instead of comparing national averages taken from statistical yearbooks, the team enrolled men in specific communities, measured their blood pressure, cholesterol, weight and habits, recorded what they ate in subsamples, and followed them for years to see who developed heart disease. By the time the cohorts were fully assembled, there were sixteen of them across seven countries, Italy and Yugoslavia and the United States and the Netherlands and Japan and Finland and Greece, totalling twelve thousand seven hundred and sixty-three men between forty and fifty-nine.

The results that made the study famous were spectacular. Rates of coronary death in the men of eastern Finland, where the winter diet ran heavily to butter and fatty meat and where the smoking rate was high, ran about thirty-two times higher over the follow-up than in the men of Crete, whose diet was olive oil and legumes and greens. Across the cohorts, the average intake of saturated fat and the average level of serum cholesterol tracked heart disease very closely. Within a few years the American Heart Association, on whose nutrition committee Keys had sat since 1960, issued its first advice to reduce dietary fat and cholesterol, in 1961. Time put his face on its cover in January of the same year.

The steelman for Keys needs stating at full strength, because it is a good deal stronger than the caricature his later enemies drew. He did not rest on the country correlations alone. In metabolic wards, where volunteers were fed measured diets and their blood drawn, Keys and his collaborators showed that saturated fat raised blood cholesterol and polyunsaturated fat lowered it, and they built equations, published in 1965, predicting the size of the change from the fatty acid composition of the diet. Independently, other groups showed that in people, in populations and in later years in trials of drugs, higher cholesterol went with more coronary disease. Two links in a chain, each with real evidence: diet moves cholesterol, and cholesterol moves risk. If both hold, then diet moves risk, and the Seven Countries data looked like the chain's outcome measured in the wild.

That is the case, and the rest of this episode is a search for the place where the chain might have been weaker than it looked. But first the man on the other side of the argument needs his turn.

## Chapter Three: The Professor Who Weighed the Sugar

John Yudkin took the chair of physiology at Queen Elizabeth College in London in 1945. By 1953 the college was admitting the first students to what became the first university nutrition degree in Europe, and in 1954 it converted his chair into a professorship of nutrition. He built a discipline in a country still living under wartime rationing, where what people ate was as much a matter of state policy as of science.

Yudkin's own contribution to the heart disease argument arrived in the Lancet in 1957, the same year Yerushalmy and Hilleboe were dismantling Keys's six-country staircase. He, too, took national diet figures and national coronary death rates, for a set of countries in 1952, and added the trend in British eating between 1928 and 1954. He found that the dietary factor most closely tied to coronary deaths was sugar, more closely than fat did. He kept going for a decade. By 1964 he was writing that in the wealthier countries there was evidence that sugar and sugar-containing foods contributed to several diseases, and he listed obesity, tooth decay, diabetes and heart attack. In 1972 he put the case to the general public in Pure, White and Deadly.

Read as evidence, Yudkin's argument had the same shape as Keys's original. It was an ecological correlation: nations and eras that ate more sugar had more coronary deaths. Its weaknesses were those Yerushalmy and Hilleboe had listed against Keys, the country selection and the confounders and the uncertain death certificates. Yudkin also supplied a mechanism argument, that sucrose, half of which is fructose, was handled by the liver in ways that raised fats in the blood and worsened the body's response to insulin. That is a serious biochemical proposal, and a version of it has since won a large audience. In 1972 that mechanism had far thinner support than the fat-cholesterol chain, whose first link rested on a metabolic ward's worth of controlled feeding studies.

What Yudkin did not have was a friendly reception. Keys attacked in print. In a 1971 paper on sucrose and heart disease, Keys wrote that Yudkin had no theoretical basis or experimental evidence for a major influence of dietary sucrose, and that his population statistics would not bear up under any elementary critical examination. The nutrition scientists who wrote about the exchange in the decades after have often quoted an even sharper phrase, in which Keys reportedly dismissed the sugar case as a mountain of nonsense, and I am reporting that second line at one remove rather than from the original page. The first is safe on the record, and it is severe enough.

Keys's critique had a legitimate scientific core. Sugar consumption in the countries of the fifties and sixties moved together with fat consumption, with car ownership, with the number of television sets and, most consequentially, with cigarettes. A raw correlation between sugar and coronary deaths proved no more than the parallel one for fat did, and Keys, who had already absorbed Yerushalmy's blow once, was not inclined to allow another man the leniency he had been denied. Where he went wrong, in my reading, is that he applied to his rival a standard he had not fully applied to himself. When the criticism of a correlation is the same as the one you spent your own career answering, it belongs in your answer to the other man too.

Yudkin lost, institutionally and quickly. He retired from his professorship in 1971, and he left the college in 1974. When the British government's Committee on Medical Aspects of Food Policy published its report on diet and heart disease in 1974, Yudkin, who sat on the panel, appended a note of reservation stating that the report had overstated what fat might do to the coronary arteries and understated what sucrose might do. The consensus went the other way. A generation of British and American dietary advice named fat as the culprit and treated sugar as merely fattening, and the man who had first proposed otherwise was remembered mainly, for thirty years, as an eccentric.

His return came late and by an odd route. In 2009 a pediatric endocrinologist at the University of California, San Francisco, Robert Lustig, gave a ninety-minute lecture called Sugar: The Bitter Truth that spread across the internet on video and has since been watched tens of millions of times. Lustig acknowledged Yudkin as the man who had said it first, and a 2012 reissue of Pure, White and Deadly carried a foreword by Lustig himself. Both halves of the duel thus had a second act, and between the two acts sits a set of documents that neither man lived to see.

## Chapter Four: One Table, Two Readings

The strangest fact in this whole story is what happens if you take Keys's own study and ask it Yudkin's question.

In 2018 a group led by the Dutch epidemiologist Daan Kromhout, who had worked on the Seven Countries Study for decades, went back to its dietary records with a question nobody had asked in the original analysis: how did sucrose, sugar itself, relate to heart death across the sixteen cohorts? They followed the men for fifty years. The answer is that saturated fat and sucrose both showed strong positive ecological correlations with fifty-year coronary mortality, and the range of those correlations ran between roughly sixty-eight and ninety-two hundredths for the two nutrients together. Sucrose tracked heart disease across Keys's own cohorts about as tightly as saturated fat did. The association held for sucrose specifically and not for the naturally occurring sugars in fruit and milk, which is an interesting wrinkle for the sugar side. Meanwhile the Mediterranean and Japanese dietary patterns, heavy in vegetables and light on sweets and animal foods, tracked inversely with heart death.

Consider what that means for the duel. The study Keys built to escape the objection that his correlations were an accident of which countries he chose contained, without anyone having looked, a correlation for Yudkin's hypothesis as strong as the one for his own. It could not choose between them. The two nutrients rode the same trucks. Where people ate butter, cream and red meat, they mostly also ate cakes and sweetened tea, and where they ate olive oil and beans, they mostly did not. A statistician calls that collinearity, and in an ecological study, where the unit is a whole population and the number of data points is sixteen, there is no adjustment clever enough to pull two threads apart when they were spun together by history.

The original reviews of the Seven Countries Study had already noted a second limit. It is ecological in its headline comparison, meaning that its central correlations are between averages for populations rather than between individuals and their own fates. A reviewer's summary of the study notes that differences in mean age, blood pressure, cholesterol and smoking accounted for roughly eighty percent of the variation in coronary death between cohorts. That statement can be read in Keys's favor, since cholesterol is on the list, and in his critics' favor, since so are blood pressure and cigarettes and age, none of which are dietary at all.

The eastern Finnish men are a good place to hold both readings at once. They smoked heavily, worked outdoors in a hard climate, ate the dairy fat of a dairying region, and had high blood pressure. When their coronary rate was thirty-two times that of the Cretan cohort, the study was recording all of that at once, and the interpretation depended on which variable you had arrived already suspecting. In Finland a few years later a public health program in North Karelia cut the fat and the salt and the smoking together and watched heart disease fall dramatically, a fine outcome for Finland and a poor experiment for anyone who wants to know which change did it.

The situation recalls this shelf's earlier stories in one way. In the pellagra episode, Joseph Goldberger showed that a disease everyone assumed was an infection was a deficiency, and to do it he could not lean on correlations, because in the American South pellagra tracked poverty, filth and bad water all at once. He fed prisoners a diet and waited. Diet-heart was the same problem with a slower disease and a bigger budget. What broke the pellagra deadlock was intervention, a manipulated diet on people whose other conditions were fixed, and it was inevitable that once the two sides of the diet-heart argument ran out of country tables, someone would try to do the same.

## Chapter Five: The Money in the Middle

Before the trials, though, come the papers in the Harvard library. The story of the sugar industry's letters is the best-known part of this affair, and the version that reached the headlines in 2016 is simpler than the version the documents support.

In 1965 the press picked up a wave of research suggesting sucrose might be a heart risk, in part Yudkin's and in part that of other British and American researchers. The Sugar Research Foundation, the industry's trade research body, decided the moment required an answer, and it commissioned what its files called Project 226, a literature review of the relationship between dietary fats, carbohydrates and coronary disease. Its authors were to be members of the Harvard School of Public Health's nutrition department, chiefly Mark Hegsted, with Robert McGandy, under the supervision of the department's chairman Fredrick Stare, himself a man with well-documented ties to food companies. The industry's contact was a foundation executive named John Hickson, and the payment was six thousand five hundred dollars, which the historians who found the letters translate at nearly fifty thousand dollars in the money of 2016.

The letters, which the UCSF researcher Cristin Kearns found in a Harvard library, show how the project worked. The Foundation named the topic and supplied a set of papers it wanted included. It asked for drafts and got them, and by September 1966 Hickson was pressing for more. On the second of November he approved the latest draft as quite what the Foundation had in mind. The review appeared in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1967, in two parts, and it concluded that the evidence pointed overwhelmingly to reducing cholesterol and saturated fat as the dietary change that mattered. The article's disclosure statement listed no industry funding. Kearns, with two colleagues at UCSF, Laura Schmidt and Stanton Glantz, published the analysis in JAMA Internal Medicine on the twelfth of September, 2016, drawing on more than three hundred and forty documents and about fifteen hundred pages.

The headlines said that Big Sugar had paid Harvard to blame fat. The paper itself is more careful than that, and the caution matters. It shows that a trade group with a financial stake in a conclusion selected, paid for and edited a review that reached the conclusion, and that the funding was hidden from readers. It shows that the industry ran a multi-year program in the same direction. It does not show that Hegsted, McGandy and Stare invented the fat hypothesis or that they disbelieved it. They had reached views about saturated fat from the same metabolic-ward research that Keys had, and Hegsted would go on to help write the 1977 Dietary Goals, the document that led to the first federal dietary guidelines in 1980, in the same decade. The documents cannot show what the review's authors would have written had nobody paid them, and I would not claim to know.

That leaves two kinds of harm to keep apart. One is a wrong answer, and the other is a corrupt process that happens to produce an answer that may or may not be wrong. A review paid for and steered by an interested party is a process failure, and the lesson of the archive is that the journal's readers were denied the information they needed to weigh it. That failure stands regardless of whether the review's conclusion about fat was true. And in the specific case of the answer, the industry's money did something subtler than persuade Harvard. It steered attention. If the sugar hypothesis, in the mid-nineteen sixties, still had an unresolved evidentiary status and needed to be tested, and if the largest and best-funded reviewers of that evidence were being paid by sucrose's vendors to look elsewhere, then the hypothesis was never tested at the scale it needed, and that gap has a cost. It is the cost of an experiment nobody ran.

Yudkin's own side of the ledger carries its own asterisk. Stephan Guyenet, a neurobiologist and one of the sharper reviewers of the sugar literature, points out in his critique of Gary Taubes's 2016 book on sugar that Yudkin received research support from the egg, edible oil and dairy industries. Interested money in nutrition science was the water everyone swam in. That does not make Yudkin wrong. It means that in this field no one's hands were clean enough to settle the matter by character, and the question has to be answered the hard way.

## Chapter Six: The Trials Nobody Wanted to Publish

A randomized trial is the only thing that could break the tie, and in this field the trials that exist share an awkward feature: they never tested sugar. The trials of the diet-heart hypothesis, mounted in the nineteen sixties and early seventies, tested Keys's chain. Replace saturated fat with polyunsaturated vegetable oil, and see whether cholesterol falls and whether hearts follow.

Two of them explain the opening basement. The first is the Sydney Diet Heart Study, run from 1966 to 1973 in Australia, in four hundred and fifty-eight men between thirty and fifty-nine who had already had a coronary event. The intervention group, of two hundred twenty-one men, swapped saturated fat for safflower oil and safflower oil margarine, rich in the omega-six fatty acid linoleic acid, while two hundred thirty-seven controls carried on eating as they had. In 2013 Ramsden recovered the data from an obsolete nine-track magnetic tape, with the help of a researcher from the original Sydney team. Over the follow-up, seventeen point six percent of the intervention group died of any cause, against eleven point eight percent of controls. Cardiovascular deaths ran at seventeen point two percent against eleven percent. The diet that was supposed to protect the heart had, in a small trial of men who had already had heart attacks, killed more of them.

The second is the Minnesota Coronary Experiment, Frantz's trial, and its recovered analysis appeared in the BMJ in April 2016. The trial randomized nine thousand four hundred and twenty-three people aged twenty to ninety-seven. In the intervention arm, corn oil and corn-oil margarine replaced about half of the saturated fat. The analysis focused on the two thousand three hundred and fifty-five participants who spent at least a year on their assigned diet. Serum cholesterol fell by thirteen point eight percent in the intervention group and by one percent in controls, and the difference was large. Deaths did not fall. The recovered autopsies showed no reduction in coronary atherosclerosis or in heart attacks. In a statistical model, the participants whose cholesterol fell further had a higher risk of death, twenty-two percent higher for every thirty-point fall, and when Ramsden pooled these two trials with four similar ones, the result was flat. For coronary death the ratio was one point one three, with a confidence interval running from eighty-three hundredths up to one point five four, and for death from any cause one point oh seven, with a range of ninety hundredths through one point two seven. In plain terms, the cholesterol-lowering diet had lowered cholesterol and done little else.

The original 1989 publication of the Minnesota results had reported no differences in cardiovascular events, cardiovascular deaths or overall mortality between the arms, and had added that a favorable trend for all these end points occurred in some younger age groups. The recovered analysis found a signal in the other direction among the older participants. A writer in the BMJ's editorial pages noted that the two sets of authors did not differ much on the numbers and differed a good deal on how to interpret them.

Now the steelman for the diet-heart side of this, which the trials' critics have made repeatedly and which has substance. The Minnesota population was old, sick and institutionalized, and those are patients in whom low cholesterol is often a marker of frailty and not a benefit. A twenty-two percent risk rise per thirty-point fall may mean that people who were dying anyway had lower cholesterol first. That is the reverse-causality problem that undid the aflatoxin theory of kwashiorkor in this shelf's last episode, a toxin blamed for a disease that was itself damaging the organ that clears the toxin, and the same logic applies here. Nortin Hadler, a rheumatologist and a long-time skeptic of overtreatment, told reporters that the trial had some flaws and was as good as it got at the time and not bad today, which is about as fair an assessment as the trial has had. The margarine of that era was also commonly made by partial hydrogenation, a process that creates trans fats, so the intervention might have delivered a harmful ingredient alongside the linoleic acid, an inference of mine and not a finding of the trial. And exposure to the diet, for many of the nine thousand, was short.

Then there is the wider record. The Cochrane Collaboration's 2020 review pooled fifteen randomized trials, published between 1965 and 2019, with fifty-six thousand six hundred and seventy-five adults followed for about four and a half years on average. Its conclusion was that cutting saturated fat makes little or no difference to deaths from any cause or from cardiovascular disease, and possibly reduces cardiovascular events. The hedge is the review's own. The trials inside it differ in what they put on the plate afterward, and a benefit in events may depend on whether the replacement was vegetable oil or starch.

Read together, these trials say something narrower than the headlines of either camp. They do not show that saturated fat is harmless. What they show is that a diet swap engineered to lower cholesterol by roughly a seventh, delivered in one specific way, did not reduce deaths within the years those trials could measure. Whether the same swap would work with different oils, over decades, in younger and healthier people, is a separate matter that the pooled evidence leaves open, and Keys's defenders have a real case that they should not be judged by the worst of these trials.

## Chapter Seven: A Trial With No Sugar in It

What is missing from the whole picture is any sugar trial. The randomized trials of the diet-heart hypothesis tested the fat side of the duel, because that was the side with the institutional backing and the mechanistic chain. Nobody ran the experiment of taking a large population, halving the sucrose in their diet for ten years while holding fat constant, and counting heart attacks. It would be an expensive, hard and probably impractical trial, because people do not eat nutrients, they eat foods, and no one can hold the fat constant while removing the sugar from the pastry that contained both. The result is that the sugar hypothesis, in the half century since Yudkin published, has been argued from the same ecological tables in which it began, from short feeding experiments that measure blood fats and insulin rather than heart attacks, and from cohort studies in which sugar-sweetened drinks track with diabetes and heart disease, and in which it is impossible to exclude that the people who drink them differ in other ways.

Gary Taubes's book The Case Against Sugar, published in 2016, the same year as the Kearns paper and the Minnesota reanalysis, is the fullest modern brief for Yudkin's side, and it does what its author's books do, which is to write the history of an idea as a story of suppression. In Taubes's telling, Yudkin was the honest scientist driven from the field by a powerful, and partly sugar-funded, establishment led by Keys. There is truth in the story. The reception Yudkin received was hostile and in places contemptuous, and the archive shows an industry that acted to keep sugar out of the heart-disease conversation.

The critics of Taubes, though, say something the essay has to weigh. Guyenet's review faults the book for never seriously weighing the strength of the evidence on each side, and notes, as this episode did, that Yudkin's own research funders included food industries. Harriet Hall, the physician and writer, wrote that Taubes made a compelling case that stopped short of proving it. Another reviewer called the book a polemic rather than a balanced scientific review. The pattern across these reviews is one this shelf has met before, in Taubes's field as much as anyone's: the villain narrative is emotionally complete, and it lets the reader skip the question of whether the underdog was actually right.

And here the case against runs both ways, as it should. Against Keys: he was the more rigorous of the two in the metabolic ward and the harsher of the two toward a rival. He knew the ecological critique was fatal to Yudkin, and he made it, and he did not apply it fully to his own tables. The Seven Countries analysis he did not run, of sucrose against saturated fat, would have shown him a correlation as strong as his own, and the field's confidence rose faster than its evidence. Against Taubes and the sugar side: a hypothesis that was suppressed is not thereby true. Suppression describes institutions, and truth describes hearts. The most that the archive establishes is that a competing hypothesis went untested at scale, and that the untested state has since been mistaken, by people who dislike the establishment, for a vindication.

The two men fell into the trap that the protein episode described, from the opposite direction. There, the twenty-year industry built on kwashiorkor rested on a diagnosis that a careful clinician had hedged and the institutions hardened. Here the hardening came from both ends at once. Keys hardened a plausible chain into a national campaign before anyone had tested it in a trial, and the American Heart Association and the Harvard department and later the Senate committee invested their names in it. Yudkin's later partisans hardened a suppressed but still unproven hypothesis into a verdict. In both cases the confidence was proportional to the storyline and not to the evidence.

## Chapter Eight: What the Ledger Says

Take stock, then, of what the record supports, keeping apart what each man claimed, what the evidence shows, and what I would conclude.

Keys claimed that saturated fat causes coronary disease through cholesterol, and that dietary sugar has no important role. The evidence supports the first link firmly: saturated fat raises the cholesterol carried in low-density particles, and lowering those particles by other means, with drugs, lowers heart attacks in trials of large size. The second link, from diet to hard outcomes, is where the diet-swap trials come in, and they say that the swap Keys's generation tried did not reduce deaths, while the broader Cochrane pool leaves the door open on events. I would conclude that Keys was right about a mechanism and overconfident about a food, that the ambiguity between those two was visible in his own data, and that the phrase saturated fat as the cause of heart disease was a summary the evidence never quite earned.

Yudkin claimed that sugar causes heart disease. The evidence for that claim was, in 1957, and remained for decades, of the same kind that Keys's critics had demolished when it was Keys's, and later work has strengthened the case for sugar as a driver of weight gain and metabolic disease without ever producing the heart-attack trial. I would conclude that Yudkin was right to raise the question, wrong to be as sure as his title claimed, and treated worse than the evidence justified, and that the same three sentences could be written about Keys.

There is a lesson in the pair about the design of nutrition research, and it is not a comforting one. A duel of this kind is unwinnable on the evidence its era could produce. The tables could not separate collinear nutrients. The trials that could separate cause from correlation tested one man's hypothesis and skipped the other's. The money, and the prestige, and the pattern of who got funded, decided which hypothesis got the experiment. In the eighty years since Keys starved thirty-six men in a basement to learn how people recover from hunger, nutrition has become better at measurement and no better at deciding whose question gets the trial.

As for what to read: the Kearns paper is short, an evening's reading, and changes how you look at any nutrition review with an author list of professors and a disclosure section. Ramsden's 2016 BMJ paper rewards a reader for its methods more than its headline, especially the reconstruction of a trial's data from a cardboard box, and the accompanying editorial by Lennert Veerman, which is a page and a half and gives the most balanced summary of how the field reads it. Kromhout's 2018 paper on the Seven Countries data is the one to read for the irony. Pure, White and Deadly earns an hour as a historical document, on the strength of its plainness, and not as evidence. Skip the polemics on both sides, including the ones that agree with you.

A last question travels well beyond nutrition. Every diet trial replaces something with something else, a cut nutrient is always matched by an added one, and the swap is the intervention. When a trial reports that cutting one thing did nothing, or that it helped, the useful question is what went onto the plate in its place, and whether the trial's answer belongs to the removal or to the substitute. The same question applies to any program that stops one activity to make room for another. In Minnesota the substitute was corn oil margarine, and it may have been the margarine.

Next time, this shelf leaves the plates of adults for the plates of their mothers: David Barker and the fetal origins hypothesis, the Hertfordshire birth records that suggested a man's heart disease might begin in his mother's pregnancy, and the critics who watched the effect sizes shrink as the studies got better. Thanks for listening.
