description: "A prisoner-of-war doctor turned epidemiologist audits Britain's health service in 1972, finds a service that spends more each year without a measurable return, and starts the argument that ends in the Cochrane Collaboration; here is what the book shows, what it gets wrong, and how to read it."

## Chapter One: The Doctor Who Was Superfluous

During the Second World War, in a prisoner-of-war camp outside Salonika, a young British medical officer had about twenty thousand men to look after and almost nothing to do it with. A quarter of the prisoners were British. The diet came to roughly six hundred calories a day, everyone had diarrhoea, and the camp was passing through epidemics of typhoid, diphtheria, jaundice and sand-fly fever, with more than three hundred men swollen with the pitting oedema of starvation. The pharmacy was some aspirin, an antacid and a skin antiseptic. He was for a long time the only doctor in the place.

He expected a massacre. Diphtheria alone, with no antitoxin, should have killed hundreds. The camp lost four men over those months, and three of the four were shot by guards. The doctor, Archie Cochrane, drew the conclusion that a career in medicine did not entirely dislodge. Whatever had kept those men alive, it was not him, and it was not anything in the pharmacy. The recuperative power of the human body had done the work. When he asked the senior German medical officer for help, he was told, in effect, that doctors were superfluous. He was furious and wrote a poem about it. Years later he wondered whether the German had been wise or cruel, and concluded that he had at least been right.

The second camp, at Elsterhorst, held every prisoner with tuberculosis, of every nationality, behind one wire. Conditions were good by comparison. Red Cross parcels fed the men, sputum smears were possible, and so were a few radiographs. Cochrane could offer bed rest, an artificially collapsed lung, and air injected into the abdomen. He lived with these patients and buried them, and with no priest available he conducted the funerals, becoming by his own account quite expert in Hindu, Muslim and Greek Orthodox rites. A pamphlet reached the camp on clinical freedom and democracy, and he found it impossible to understand. He had complete freedom to choose a treatment, and his difficulty was that he did not know which to choose or when. He had never heard of a randomized controlled trial, but he knew there was no real evidence that anything he offered did any good in tuberculosis, and he feared he had shortened the lives of friends by intervening.

That is the opening of Effectiveness and Efficiency, subtitled Random Reflections on Health Services, published by the Nuffield Provincial Hospitals Trust in 1972. It runs to ninety pages of large print, written as the Rock Carling Fellowship book for 1971. The question that predates it and carries this episode is one that sounds unanswerable until somebody tries: how would a society know whether its medicine does any good? Cochrane's answer was a program that sounds modest and became enormous. Test the treatments, all of them, by the method Austin Bradford Hill had demonstrated twenty years before, in which random numbers, and not the doctor's judgement, decide who gets what.

First he does something almost no author of a policy book does, which is to confess. He says his greatest difficulty was writing opinion at all, because years of scientific papers had left him unable to write a sentence without hedging it, and his solution was to list his biases in advance. He was emotionally in favour of a National Health Service, because of the injustice he had seen as a student in London in the nineteen thirties. He lacked clinical experience. He had spent his career as a field epidemiologist studying whole communities, which gave him what he called a worm's-eye view. And he had a slogan. As a student he wrote out All effective treatment must be free to carry at a rally, and the one person who noticed it accused him of Trotskyite tendencies. Thirty-five years on he went back to the slogan and found its weak point at once. He could not have passed an examination on what the word effective means.

The rest of the book asks what it would take to make the word mean something.

## Chapter Two: Input and Output

Cochrane begins with a joke. He once asked a man who worked at a crematorium what he found so satisfying about the job, and the man said it was the way so much went in and so little came out. Cochrane considered recommending the National Health Service to him and decided against it.

The first part of the book tries to show the gap in numbers, with all the reservations the numbers need. Between 1959 and 1969 hospital medical staff went from about sixteen thousand to about twenty-two thousand, hospital nurses rose by roughly seventy thousand, and annual prescriptions climbed by fifty million to about two hundred and sixty-four million. Requests for pathology tests more than doubled, to nearly thirty-nine million. All of this came in the most productive period in the history of drugs, with the anti-tuberculosis drugs, the blood-pressure drugs, cortisone, the antidepressants and polio vaccine arriving as external gifts the service could take no credit for. Other things being equal, he says, a considerable output should follow.

Then he goes looking, and is frank about how poor the instruments are. Expectation of life barely moved for a man of forty-five, around twenty-seven further years in the late forties and in the mid sixties alike, and he calls that unfair, since a service cannot be blamed for diseases it cannot treat. Mortality ratios for single diseases show roundabouts and swings, tuberculosis falling steeply while heart disease and lung cancer rose, each cancelling the other in the totals. Whether any fall belongs to the service or to wages, housing and natural history, the data cannot say.

The one comprehensive measure of morbidity is days of certified sickness among employed men, and that rose, from about twelve hundred and eighty per hundred men in 1954 to about sixteen hundred and thirty in 1967. The breakdown by diagnosis is where he enjoys himself. Days lost to tuberculosis fell by over four fifths, while sprains and strains rose two and a half times over, and nervousness, debility and headache nearly doubled. He then supplies the credentials for reading such figures. At a punishment camp near Wittenberg he had been the only medical officer, and a man reporting sick without being sick risked the Eastern Front. Even so, he writes, it was perfectly possible to quadruple the sick parade. He had trained the men. He used the French rather than the British, because they were better actors. He stage-managed small epidemics of mumps and acute nephritis, and nobody was shot or punished as a malingerer. He adds, with relish, that the two diagnoses now rising fastest are the two he specialised in, headache and sprains, and apologizes to any general practitioner treating a man he coached.

Some of the rise, he thinks, follows what sickness pays. A married man with two children, out sick, drew about thirty-six percent of what a working man took home in 1951 and about seventy-four and a half in 1968.

The method is consistent. Each figure arrives with a paragraph on why it cannot carry what is asked of it, and a conclusion is drawn anyway, because a rough answer given honestly beats silence. This one says the crude data cannot show a relationship between input and output, and what suggestive evidence exists points to there not being much of one. It is a thin result, and he treats it as permission to look at treatments one by one. For that he needs a way of saying what it is to know that one works.

## Chapter Three: How a Boy Who Got Caned Smoked More

His chapter on evidence opens with two words he thinks have been damaged in his lifetime. Opinion has been promoted and experiment demoted. The promotion he blames partly on television interviewers, who want everything brief, dramatic and black and white and never ask a speaker for evidence. The demotion he blames on journalists, who use experimental for anything novel, and he recalls a sign on a church door about an experimental baptism, which led him to spend a happy half-hour designing a trial to compare the baptismal effectiveness of the waters of the Jordan, the Tweed and the Taff.

Then the ladder. At the bottom is clinical opinion, low in value because nobody measures anything, nobody asks what would have happened without treatment, and every bias is free to act. Above it come comparison groups, many of them grotesque, of which the worst are the patients who refused treatment. Above that come blind measurements. Even there, observational evidence is in his phrase thoroughly unsatisfactory, and his example is not medical, which is a good decision. A colleague, J. W. Palmer, took smoking histories from every boy in a secondary modern school on two occasions a year apart, and compared the change in smoking between boys caned for smoking in that year and those who were not. Of twenty-three caned boys, eight smoked more a year later, thirty-five percent. Of a hundred and twenty-three others, ten did, eight percent. The difference is large and the test comes out highly significant. It reads as a finding: caning makes boys smoke.

Run it to its end. The boys who were caned were the boys who were caught, and the ones caught were smoking heavily and carelessly already. A rising habit is the one that draws attention. Nothing in the table separates the story in which caning raised smoking from the one in which caning followed it, or one in which it did nothing. The result fits caning increasing, decreasing or having no effect, and the comparison cannot cancel the differences between the boys because nobody can list them all.

The randomized trial is the repair, and he describes it with something close to love. Stop trying to make the groups alike. Allocate by a process independent of any human choice, and the characteristics of the patients, those measured and those nobody thought of, are spread by chance, which allows arithmetic to say how often a difference that large would appear by luck. Hill, he says, has been honoured but not enough, and there should be a Bradford award each year for the best medical statistical paper.

He lists the snags, and the list is still serviceable. At the usual threshold one test in twenty misleads, and a research unit running hundreds a year forgets this. Small trials miss effects that exist, and enormous ones find effects that matter to nobody. Subjective outcomes resist even blinding. Rare diseases rarely yield enough patients, and in porphyria, where dialysis seemed life-saving in one acute attack and did nothing in the next two, a trial would need co-operation from most of Europe. And there is ethics. Surgery for lung cancer, cytology for cervical cancer and diet for phenylketonuria had never been tested, because by the time anyone thought of it the procedures were routine and a trial meant withholding them from half the patients, which committees called unethical. His reply carries the rest of this series: when the value of a treatment is unknown, the unethical act is the failure to find out.

One more paragraph is the one forgotten by people who call him a trial fundamentalist. He did not think the randomized trial was the only technique of value. He thought evaluation was the first priority of the service and the trial the most satisfactory way to do it despite the snags, because administrators spend their days choosing between alternatives and can do that well only with comparable data on benefit and cost.

So, in 1971, which of the things Britain's doctors did would pass?

## Chapter Four: Six Boxes

His answer is a classification, offered with the apology that he can classify only roughly. The first box holds treatments with no trial that need none, because the effect is obvious: insulin in juvenile diabetes, vitamin B12 in pernicious anaemia, penicillin for certain infections. The second holds treatments backed by trials, with tuberculosis chemotherapy the best example. The third holds those with good laboratory evidence of some physiological effect and no evidence from trials that they do more good than harm, and he flags it as interesting because doctors are so easily impressed by what looks like basic science. The fourth holds treatments established before trials existed, which ethics now forbid testing, such as the treatment of cancer of the lung and breast. The fifth holds those with equivocal trial evidence, tonsillectomy being the case. The sixth holds treatments that nothing prevents testing and nobody has, and he names psychotherapy and physiotherapy.

The third box has his best case, and it is anaemia. Everyone believed that a low haemoglobin causes tiredness, headache and the classical symptoms. A colleague in his unit, P. C. Elwood, studied iron therapy in non-pregnant women whose haemoglobin sat between nine and twelve grams per hundred millilitres and followed their symptoms. The iron raised the haemoglobin, as everyone knew it would. By Cochrane's account the symptoms were not cured. A mechanism everyone trusted and a number everyone could see had produced a clinical belief that did not survive a comparison.

He applies the same logic to diabetes in middle age. Doctors treated it with diet, insulin and the tablets then in use, because the patients had raised blood sugar and the drugs lowered it. By then two large trials existed. The American University Group Diabetes Program, which he calls beautifully designed, suggested from mortality data that two of those tablets, tolbutamide and phenformin, were dangerous and insulin useless for these patients. Then he states the serious objection to a trial he admires: the placebo group had far fewer deaths from heart disease than experience predicts, so by bad luck randomization may have given an atypical comparison group. A British trial from Guy's Hospital, run eight years in Bedford among patients found by survey, not among patients attending clinics with symptoms, found slightly fewer cardiovascular deaths on tolbutamide, a difference short of conventional significance until cardiovascular events were added to deaths, when it crossed the five percent line. The American trial had pointed the other way. His reading of the pair is that tolbutamide does little or nothing, may or may not be dangerous, and there is no reason to risk it. The lesson is in the assumption behind the treatment, that where a biochemical measure sits abnormally in a group, bringing it back to normal must do more good than harm. In mature diabetes, he writes, it may be the wrong parameter that is being altered.

The decades afterwards bear out his hedge about the placebo group and complicate it. The American study set off one of the longest controversies in the history of trials, with audits, petitions to the drug regulator and lawsuits, and a regulatory warning that extended to the whole class of drugs. Cornfield's defence in 1971 called the investigators' decision prudent and moderately worded, and a 1980 reanalysis argued that the placebo group's cardiovascular death rate was spuriously low. Cochrane's position, that the trials disagreed and neither showed a benefit, was the one that needed no side to win the argument about the deaths.

The six boxes are unlike what a reformer would write, because he proposes to abolish nothing. He proposes an inventory in which every routine treatment sits in its box, so that argument can start from knowing which box that is. And he says the inventory cannot yet be made, since no national data connect diagnosis with treatment. Nobody in Britain knew which drugs were prescribed for which diseases.

## Chapter Five: The Table the Right Way Round

The cleanest story in the book is not about a drug. It concerns where a heart attack should be treated.

By the end of the sixties the coronary care unit was the most fashionable idea in medicine. It was an American product, in which monitors and a defibrillator, applied within minutes, could stop many cardiac arrests, and patients were gathered in one room with the machines and the nurses. The observational support compared death rates before and after a unit opened, in groups nobody could call comparable. In America units spread irresistibly. In Britain, Cochrane writes, only relative poverty and a health service that cared about equality stopped them.

A team in Bristol led by H. G. Mather did the trial. Patients with a suspected heart attack were allocated at random to treatment at home or to hospital with a coronary care unit. It was technically and psychologically very hard, and Cochrane thought randomization had been achieved, and gives the investigators credit for recording the people who were not randomized, whom social circumstances decided for them. It was also the first major trial financed by the Department's new research section.

The result, in a first report in 1971 and a longer one in 1976, was that home care did no worse than the unit and perhaps better. The first report randomized three hundred and forty-three people. By the follow-up the randomized group was four hundred and fifty, and mortality at twenty-eight days was twelve percent in the home group against fourteen in the hospital group, with twenty and twenty-seven percent by about eleven months. These later figures come from journal abstracts as a search reported them, and the book, written earlier, could report only the first.

What the book adds is a story about the first interim report. A few months in, the early table showed slightly more deaths in the hospital group than at home. Cochrane showed a version with the columns swapped, so that the unit seemed to have the higher death rate, to a coronary care enthusiast, who declared at once that the trial was unethical and must be stopped. Shown the table the right way round, he could not be persuaded to say the same about the units. Cochrane calls the bias beautifully illustrated and says no more. The same numbers, and the verdict flipped with the labels.

He is careful about what the trial leaves open, and his five points would serve as the limitations paragraph of a modern paper. It should be repeated elsewhere. It says nothing about patients who must be admitted for social reasons, or whose attack happens in hospital, and on the evidence it was reasonable to go on using the units. His own hypothesis, labelled a guess from a selected experience, is that some people are so frightened by being taken from home into a unit that cardiac arrest becomes commoner than in their own beds. A second study, by Hofvendahl, compared a unit with an ordinary ward, without random allocation, and found lower mortality for those spending their first two days in the unit; the simplest reading, he says, is that hospital wards are worse for a heart attack than home, and a unit cancels part of the damage.

The trial overturned a plausible, expensive belief with an experiment small enough for one city. It also shows a limit that every trial shares. It measures one version of an intervention at one moment, and Cochrane says as much: units may develop new technological tricks that outweigh the advantages of home, or new drugs may improve home treatment. A result of that kind has a shelf life, and he states it on the page.

## Chapter Six: The Smear He Doubted

Cochrane names two sins in the prevention chapter, omission and commission, and the sin of commission is the cervical smear.

The idea was good, and nobody disputes that. A test on cells from the cervix might find disease before it turned invasive, treating the early lesion might prevent the cancer, and the death rate should then fall. Enthusiasts took it up, it became routine in the United States, and it spread to Britain. When a trial was proposed early on it was thought unethical, and every country has since decided the same. His complaint is that a hypothesis was turned into routine practice before it was tested, and the routine then made testing impossible.

His reading of the data is the part to judge him on. Death rates from cancer of the cervix were falling before smears began and went on falling at much the same rate. No convincing evidence had been published of a greater fall where coverage was high than in comparable areas with little screening. He gives mechanisms for why observation could not settle it. Heavily screened areas had more hysterectomies, which takes women out of the population at risk, and found a larger share of invasive cases at an earlier stage, which lengthens survival without anyone living longer. If the natural history is altered at all, he writes, it will be almost impossible to decide which of the three factors is responsible. It seemed to him still possible that smears had some preventive effect, but the world might never know, and health services might spend thousands of millions of pounds on preventing a rare, if severe, condition whose mortality was already falling fairly fast.

What followed he tells against himself. He gave a lecture in Cardiff in 1967 on screening and used a phrase he thought innocuous, that he knew of no hard evidence at present that smears are effective. The Welsh press quoted anonymous colleagues calling him a dangerous heretic. Abusive letters came. A distinguished colleague wrote, rather irrelevantly, accusing him of causing misery to thousands by telling the public there was no cure for cancer of the cervix, and Cochrane replied asking for the evidence that it could be cured. The colleague did not answer, which Cochrane calls a pity, since he had always wanted to know.

The verdict is not clean for either side. In 2004 a team led by Julian Peto in The Lancet estimated, from mortality by birth cohort before and after the national programme, that screening had averted an epidemic that would otherwise have killed about one in sixty-five British women born since 1950, with an annual toll near six thousand, and that eighty percent or more of those, up to five thousand every year, were probably being prevented. The authors flagged real uncertainty about the effects of oral contraceptives and changes in sexual behaviour on the baseline, and the paper opens by noting that recent reports had suggested the reduction was too small to justify the cost, so doubt of Cochrane's kind was still circulating in 2004.

Three things can be held at once. The programme very probably prevents a large number of deaths, so his hint that the expense might not be justified looks mistaken. The evidence that showed it was not a randomized trial but a contrast across cohorts and countries, which his own ladder ranks low. And his account of the flaw in the 1972 evidence was accurate, since it could not tell the programme from the trend. His error was one of prior probability: correct on the data, and underrating a mechanism, a visible precancerous stage, that made the hypothesis likelier than the figures alone allowed. His attackers were right about the outcome and had no evidence for it.

## Chapter Seven: Tonsils, Hearing Aids and the Scatter of Experts

If effectiveness asks whether a treatment works, efficiency in his usage covers every other question about delivering it: the right patient, place, length of stay and amount of testing. His chapter on inefficiency is a list, and two entries placed side by side in the section on the ear, nose and throat form a diptych.

Tonsillectomy was then the commonest cause of admission for children, about a hundred and fifty thousand annually, and in 1967 the rate in the Oxford region was nearly double that in Sheffield. Three trials existed, each finding some advantage for the operation. Cochrane finds two flaws common to all three. The comparison was operation against no or inadequate medical treatment, when the fair contest was best surgical against best medical. And outcomes rested on parents' reports, while the parents knew which children had been operated on, and parents of a child on the waiting list had every reason to exaggerate the illness. The favourable results could have been bias. Pending a perfect trial he would limit the operation to obstruction and refer every other case to medical treatment first, which he estimated would cut tonsillectomies to about a fifth.

The other entry is hearing. Hinchcliffe found that twenty-one percent of a random sample of a defined population had a hearing loss over twenty-five decibels, and other surveys agreed. Yet Townsend and Wedderburn found only about six percent of old people with a hearing aid, though perhaps thirty percent might benefit, a gap of nearly a million people. The service explained it. About a hundred centres in England shared some two hundred and twenty audiology technicians, whose numbers had fallen since 1965 and whose chief earned about thirteen hundred and sixty pounds a year at the top. Nobody followed up after fitting. Local authorities had perhaps twenty social workers for the deaf, and training for work with the deaf was often combined with a course in theology, since so many entrants were clerical. The work is dull, smacks of local government, and serves the old. The first operation, he says, is applied inefficiently because too many get it, and the second because too few do, and between them they show what allocates resources in a health service: the dramatic and fashionable thing gets done.

Midwifery gives the cleanest trend mistaken for a cause. The Peel Committee had recommended hospital provision for all deliveries, on a table showing institutional confinements rising from about sixty-four to over eighty percent between 1955 and 1968 while perinatal mortality fell from thirty-seven to under twenty-five per thousand. Every student of public health, Cochrane says, knows such a correlation is not evidence. He recast the committee's own tables with another column, average length of stay after birth, which fell from nine and a half days in 1958 to under seven in 1966. One could as wrongly or rightly conclude that the shorter the stay, the lower the mortality. Across regions there was little relation between hospitalization and perinatal mortality, and Holland, with a hospital confinement rate near twenty-nine percent, had one of the lowest perinatal death rates in the world. His own question is cruder than the committee's: what is gained by keeping such mothers in for more than forty-eight hours? Nobody had tried to find out.

One figure in the efficiency chapter connects this book to the episode before it. From a Scottish study by Heasman and Carstairs, it plots, for three diagnoses, the median hospital stay of each consultant in the country, each a dot. For peptic ulcer on medical wards the dots run from under ten days to above twenty. For heart attack the spread is from a week and a half to nearly six weeks. The national median sits somewhere in each column and almost nobody is at it, and patients, Cochrane notes, mostly want to leave as soon as possible. A bed is the costliest thing a consultant prescribes, and it is prescribed in doses the diagnosis does not explain. All the consultants cannot be right, and only a large trial of lengths of stay could say who is.

Diagnosis shows the same scatter. In one study eight radiologists read barium swallow films of a hundred and thirty-two people, looking for a web in the throat, and found it in anywhere from six to fifty-nine percent depending on the reader.

Set the Scottish dots beside the Wisconsin study section of episode 065, where two reviewers of one grant agreed about as little as reviewers of different grants. Expert judgement applied to one case scatters, in two professions fifty years apart. The Pier team could measure the scatter only by building a panel for the purpose. Cochrane found it in routine data that the system collected and nobody used. In both, the experts believed in their consistency, no one had looked, and the first look was unflattering. Where Cochrane differs from the funding reformers is in what he expects from the look. He thinks the scatter is a finding that science, applied to administration, can shrink, and that the profession will accept it once it sees the figure. That optimism is the most dated thing in the book.

## Chapter Eight: Why Nobody Had Looked

His account of why the health service ran for fifteen years without applied research is historical, which a reader does not expect from a book of tables. There were three strands. Therapy had done little until the second quarter of the century, so the service was founded on a faith in medicine the evidence did not support, and patients had turned the tradition of the bottle of medicine into something close to a right. Doctors, meanwhile, had every wish to help and the drug and test industries kept supplying new means, and what surprised him was that the explosion was not greater, since pathology requests rose threefold between 1951 and 1968. The third strand interests this series. British science had divided itself into pure and applied, with pure research designated U and applied research non-U, borrowing the class vocabulary of the day. He remembers being told at Cambridge that the best research should be utterly useless.

The Medical Research Council, he says, was founded on freedom to pursue discovery unhampered by pressure to answer the most urgent practical problems. The principle was excellent and had an unfortunate side effect. In 1948 the Ministry relied on the Council for research, the Council went on doing what it had always done so successfully, and there was practically no applied research. He admits that if the Ministry had approached him in the early fifties he would have refused.

That is the mirror image of the Bush episode, 057. Vannevar Bush's Endless Frontier argued in 1945 that basic research protected from the demands of use is the source of everything. The culture Cochrane describes is the same one seen from the receiving end: a system excellent at discovery with no place for asking whether a thing already discovered is done well. Polanyi's republic of science, in episode 061, states why the arrangement felt right to those inside it. Cochrane's complaint is the bill.

His remedy is the least remembered part of the book. He would leave the Council alone and put applied research under the Department of Health and Social Security, with close liaison, in preference to a new independent institute, since the Department knew the service's priorities best. There was a twenty-year backlog. The work is the opposite of the romantic image in which a scientist has an idea in his bath and gets a Nobel Prize a year later, because the hypotheses are ready-made, the technique is nearly always a randomized trial, and the hard part is finding co-operative consultants and general practitioners and young scientists who enjoy playing these games. He wanted the Royal Colleges to examine on length of stay and place of treatment, and reports that his searches had found no such question in the membership examination of the Royal College of Physicians. And he wanted pay for such researchers comparable to the clinicians' merit awards, since nobody of the right calibre would otherwise do it.

He also forecasts the consequence, and it reads well fifty years later. If the results were implemented, the profession's freedom would shrink. Indications for prescriptions, tests and length of stay would be defined, a par would exist for each group of signs and symptoms, and doctors with too many strokes above or below par would be asked to justify themselves to their peers. Funding requests would need hard evidence of gain. Teaching hospitals would lose the freedom to set up any prestigious operation, since eighteen London hospitals did open-heart surgery and nobody knew how many cases each did, and a 1967 survey found forty-five hospitals implanting pacemakers, ten of which did one in the year. He expected concentration in two or three centres, bigger and more intensive district hospitals, fewer chronic outpatients, and general practitioners in health centres. Parts of this came to pass, and this episode did not check how much.

## Chapter Nine: Care, Cure and the Number He Would Not Write

The last third of the book is about equality, and it is the part that does not fit the picture of Cochrane as a trial evangelist.

Effectiveness and efficiency, he had said, apply to cure, meaning therapy and diagnosis, and not to care, the board and lodging and kindness around it. So a third yardstick was needed, and he chose equality. He considers three kinds. The class gradient in use of services, which Julian Hart's inverse care law had made a polemic, he thinks overstated: surveys show a gradient in GP consultations, but the service had gone a long way toward narrowing the gap, and the critics set a standard nobody could specify. Regional differences in beds are grossly unfair, and likely stem from where hospital beds stood in 1948.

The inequality he ranks highest is between types of hospital, and the table is the argument. One in-patient week in an English acute hospital of over a hundred beds cost about fifty-six pounds, in a long-stay hospital about twenty-six, in a chronic ward about twenty-two and a half, in a mental illness hospital about seventeen and a half, and where the mentally handicapped lived about fifteen. Medical staff, nursing, catering and cleaning all descended the same staircase. He gives the two defences he had heard. District general hospitals, runs the first, merit a higher standard because they are more effective, which would persuade if they were powerhouses of effective treatment, but the one comprehensive follow-up of acute-hospital discharge, by Ferguson, found about thirty-six percent dead after two years and in all about fifty-seven percent dead or unimproved. The second defence is that long-stay patients are less sensitive to their surroundings. Some are, he grants, but they are a minority of the inmates, and they are our brothers and sisters and may very likely someday be ourselves. This is the least excusable inequality, he concludes, and the most easily remedied, and it descends from the poor-law institutions on one side and the voluntary hospitals ruled by honorary consultants on the other.

The last inequality he finds the most interesting is between diseases. How is the medical budget divided among activities? He does not know how it is done and would guess, last year's figure plus or minus five percent plus pressure groups, without cynicism, since administrators lack the information to do otherwise. He sketches what would be needed with an amateur's diffidence: list the symptomatic groups, assume only proven treatments are used, enumerate the outputs from prolonged life to relief of illness to care for those who cannot look after themselves, cost them, and calculate how each would rise with a ten percent increase in money.

Here he got stuck and says why. The next logical step was to put values on the outputs, and he found that oddly repugnant. Two things changed his mind. He had slowly accepted that the process happens every year anyway, unconsciously and inaccurately, and must be better done consciously. And Professor Alan Williams of York, who is not responsible for the views, persuaded him. If saving the life of a man of twenty is rated at one hundred, what number goes to the care of a severe schizophrenic? Many people dislike quantifying value judgements and he is now convinced it is necessary, though the exercise is far ahead and valuable for the intellectual satisfaction of thinking it through and seeing the difficulty. Then comes the sentence that a modern health economist might use as a warning label: there are too many enthusiastic economists willing to assume that all medical therapy is a hundred percent effective and efficiently deployed, in order to justify their hurry to optimize. He hoped they would be discouraged.

The passage holds half a century of health technology assessment in embryo, discomfort included. His position is that the exercise cannot honestly begin until effectiveness is known, since optimizing among treatments of unknown effect is arithmetic on invented inputs, the objection he made to cost-benefit analysis in his introduction.

## Chapter Ten: The Case Against

The book makes three claims of different quality.

The first is the inventory: for many routine treatments no trial existed, and where trials existed many treatments failed, and for much of what the service did nobody knew which drug went with which disease. A book's worth of examples supports that, most of them checked by him, his unit or colleagues he cites. The second is a diagnosis. The simplest explanation of the figures, he says, is that the cure sector suffers a severe inflation, with output rising far less than input, and his summary rates it as short of proof though in his view reasonably conclusive. The third is a prescription, that science and above all randomized trials could control the inflation, and the savings pay for the neglected care sector.

Take the weak points in turn. The diagnosis rests on output measures that he spent a chapter showing are crude. Input rose, and mortality and sickness did not obviously improve, but the step from there to inflation, a sector producing less per unit, ignores the chance that output was real and unmeasured. A nurse-hour on a ward of frail old people may produce what no table in the book captures, a point he makes about care and does not apply to cure. The prescription has a related hole. It assumes that when trials name the ineffective treatments, resources will move, and his own tonsil and midwifery examples show practice shifting slowly with the figures in hand. And it assumes the profession will accept limits on its freedom once the evidence is plain, and the later fights over guidelines suggest optimism.

There are criticisms from inside the movement he started. A 2014 paper in the BMJ by Trisha Greenhalgh, Jeremy Howick and Neal Maskrey, written for an Evidence Based Medicine Renaissance Group, asked whether the movement was in crisis. It listed the quality mark of evidence co-opted by drug and device companies, more evidence than anyone can read, statistically significant results that mean marginal benefit, and rigid rules that fit badly with patients who have several diseases. Cochrane would not be surprised by the first three. His book names pharmaceutical promotion among the external factors in the inflation, and pressure for new tests. The fourth is nearer to a real disagreement. A patient with five illnesses and eight drugs falls between trials on every line, and a box system for single treatments is no help. Cochrane did see the gap between what a trial shows in selected patients and what happens in the community, and kept the word effective for the second, so that objection lands more on what came after him than on him.

Then there is the certainty about people who disagreed. His pages on psychiatry are the weakest in the book. He calls the specialty basically inefficient, would ban the prescription of amphetamines, would restrict many other psychotropic drugs to consultants, and would cut money for therapy to fund evaluation. He reports being accused of logical lunacy by a distinguished practitioner and cheerfully accepts being called logical. His logic against Roth and Shapiro is sound, since a sentence saying that one good trial of an antidepressant is not invalidated by another equally good negative one invites the reversal. The weakness is impatience, applied to a field where the outcome is a person's state of mind, which glides past a difficulty he raised himself in the section on trials: subjective outcomes are hard to measure, even blind.

Social work gets the same strength and the same impatience. He worries that a new profession is determined to repeat medicine's mistakes, citing a book that argued a counsellor's certainty of value need not rest on verified results. Then he tells two stories against himself. A senior consultant refused to enter a trial because the protocol was unethical, since he knew the result already. A headmaster, asked to randomize boys caught smoking to caning or detention, said it was unnecessary because he always knew which boy should be caned. Cochrane checked later, and the method was simple. He caned them all.

Reception is thin. For a book of this age, no contemporary review of any weight turned up in this research, which is itself information: its reputation was made by what was built on it, not by what was said in 1972. The case against it here has had to be assembled from later arguments, not from critics who read it at the time.

## Chapter Eleven: A Wooden Spoon

What did the book do? Almost nothing at once, then a great deal. In 1979, in an essay in a volume called Medicines for the Year 2000, Cochrane put the complaint in the form that stuck. It was a great criticism of the profession, in words I know only as secondary sources report them, that it had not organized a critical summary, by specialty and updated periodically, of all relevant randomized controlled trials. The same essay, according to a later editorial, awarded a wooden spoon, the booby prize of the Cambridge mathematics examinations, to obstetrics and gynaecology for making the least use of randomized evidence. Midwifery is where the 1972 book is at its most exasperated as well.

The insult worked, because the target answered it. Iain Chalmers and colleagues compiled a bibliography of about three and a half thousand reports of controlled trials in the perinatal field, published in 1985, and between 1985 and 1992 an international group produced systematic reviews of those trials, gathered as Effective Care in Pregnancy and Childbirth. The model widened, and in 1993, five years after Cochrane's death in 1988, the Cochrane Collaboration was founded in his name to do for the rest of medicine what that group had done for birth. That is the road from a prisoner-of-war doctor's confession to the organization that still bears his name.

The route shows what the book was. It was not a method. Hill's trial and Fisher's mathematics, which came before him, are taken up in later episodes. It was an inventory of what had not been tested, written by a man with the authority of once not knowing what to do, and a gift for the story that turns an abstraction into a mistake the listener could make. He wears best where he declines to claim more than he can show, and worst in his forecast of how the profession would respond.

The book is free from the Nuffield Trust as a scan, which is how this episode read it, first page to last. It is a single sitting of perhaps three hours, paced by the tables. The evidence chapter is the part to read if nothing else is, with the treatment section and its anaemia, diabetes and coronary unit examples after it. The chapters on equality surprise, and are the least likely to be summarized elsewhere. The postscript is two paragraphs. In it he says he may have been too critical of colleagues he loves, and asks what other profession encourages publication of its own errors, and which judge or headmaster has encouraged trials of their therapeutic actions. He reminds the reader how many judgements are reversed on appeal and how many bridges have fallen down. As a defence it is only partly consoling.

For anyone who runs studies, the use of the book is to carry the six boxes into the routine. Take what a team does every week with confidence and ask which box it sits in. Does it work the way iron raises haemoglobin, through a mechanism everyone trusts and a comparison nobody has made? Is it a tonsillectomy, applied widely because applying it is easy, or a hearing aid, applied rarely because the service that would apply it is dull? And is there a headmaster somewhere in the routine, a person who knows which case needs the treatment that has been given to every case so far? Of the next ten things the team does by habit, which could be moved up a box by an experiment it could run, and what would have to be true of the people in charge for them to accept the answer if it came back the wrong way round?

For the record, here is how this episode was grounded. Cochrane's book was read in full, every page of the scanned copy including the tables, figures and reference list, and every number and anecdote from it is from those pages. The book is in copyright and almost nothing is quoted from it beyond a few words. The Mather results after 1971, the Peto estimate of 2004, the history of the tolbutamide controversy, the 1979 essay and wooden spoon, the Chalmers bibliography, the founding date of the Cochrane Collaboration and the Greenhalgh critique came from search results and journal abstracts, not the full papers, and the 1979 wording in particular is not Cochrane's own as I saw it. No contemporary review of the 1972 book was found. His autobiography, One Man's Medicine, was not read.
