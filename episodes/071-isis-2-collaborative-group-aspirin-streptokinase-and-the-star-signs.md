## Chapter One: Gemini and Libra

Among patients born under Gemini or Libra, a hundred and fifty died in the month after a suspected heart attack on aspirin. On placebo, a hundred and forty-seven died. Aspirin, in these two signs, did nothing. Among everyone else, the other ten signs of the zodiac, six hundred and fifty-four died on aspirin and eight hundred and sixty-nine on placebo. That is about a quarter fewer deaths, and the chance of a gap like it arising by luck is put at less than one in a million.

Those counts are from a table in a short editorial that Richard Peto published in the British Journal of Cancer in 2011, which I read in full. The table is a demonstration, and a deliberate one. The trial behind it is the Second International Study of Infarct Survival, ISIS-2, reported in the Lancet on the thirteenth of August 1988. The astrological subgroups were not found by an investigator who believed in the stars. They were made, by the trial's own side, to show what happens when a large and thoroughly positive trial is cut into pieces and somebody goes looking.

The question for this episode is an old one in a new setting. The last four episodes were about how a comparison is made fair and how its result is judged: the lottery, the significance test, the sealed envelope. This one is about what is done to a fair comparison afterwards. A trial gives you one answer for the people it enrolled. Everyone who reads it wants a second answer, for the particular person in front of them, and the data can be cut a hundred ways to look as though they supply it. How many of those cuts are information, and how many are weather?

I should say how this episode is grounded. The ISIS-2 paper itself is closed to me. I hold its published abstract in full, which carries every headline count, and I hold two later accounts by people who were inside the ISIS collaboration: Peter Sleight, writing in 2000 under a title that can be summarised as subgroups being fun to look at and not to be believed, and Peto in 2011. When a figure below is about the trial's methods or its findings beyond the abstract, it comes from one of those two, and I will say which. The series has moved from a trial of a hundred and seven people to a trial of seventeen thousand, and the scale is part of what the episode is about.

## Chapter Two: Two Drugs, Four Groups

Start with the trial, because the astrology only works as a joke if the trial was good. Seventeen thousand, one hundred and eighty-seven patients entered four hundred and seventeen hospitals with a suspected heart attack. They came up to twenty-four hours after the pain began, and the median was five hours. Each was randomised twice, in effect, and with placebo control both times. One question was an infusion: either one and a half million units of streptokinase over an hour, or a placebo infusion. The other question was a tablet: either a hundred and sixty milligrams a day of enteric-coated aspirin for a month, or a placebo tablet. Put the two together and every patient lands in one of four groups: streptokinase alone, aspirin alone, both, or neither.

That layout is called a factorial design, and the reason to want it is economy. Streptokinase dissolves clots, and in the 1980s, as I understand it, giving it routinely was still contested. Aspirin was cheap, and its role was also unproven. A conventional design would have run two trials. The factorial runs one. Every patient helps answer both questions, because the streptokinase comparison sets the two groups with the infusion against the two without, and the aspirin comparison cuts the other way. Neither drug's comparison is contaminated by the other, provided the two do not interact, and the design also lets you check whether they do.

The abstract gives the counts, and I give them as printed. Take the four groups as two pooled comparisons. Of about eighty-six hundred on the streptokinase infusion, roughly nine percent died of a vascular cause within five weeks, against twelve percent on the placebo infusion. For the aspirin tablet it was about nine and a half percent against nearly twelve on the placebo tablet. The authors summarise each as an odds reduction, which is roughly the percentage fewer deaths: twenty-five for streptokinase and twenty-three for aspirin, with a standard deviation of four. The chance of results that extreme under no effect is put at less than one in a hundred thousand.

Now the two corner groups, both drugs and neither. On both, 8.0 percent died. On neither, 13.2 percent, in about forty-three hundred patients each. The gap between those is 5.2 points. The streptokinase comparison showed a gap of 2.8 points and the aspirin comparison 2.4, and 2.8 and 2.4 make 5.2. The abstract's own wording is that the separate effects appeared to be additive. The two drugs did not fight, and they did not need each other. A patient who got both had an odds reduction of forty-two percent, with limits of thirty-four to fifty, which in plain terms is about fifty-two fewer deaths per thousand patients than the group that got nothing active.

That sum matters for a reason beyond the headline. If the two effects had not added, the pooled comparisons would have averaged over something different in the two halves, and the design's economy would have been a trap. They did add, so each pooled comparison is clean. This is the same logic Fisher gave factorial designs in 1935, which I covered in episode sixty-eight, the claim that you could test several things at once in the same experiment at no loss of precision on any of them. ISIS-2 is that claim cashed out in a cardiology ward, at a scale Fisher's field plots could not have imagined.

A design like this has a price for the people running it. A protocol with four groups and two placebos, in four hundred and seventeen hospitals across, according to Sleight, sixteen countries, has to be simple enough for a junior doctor in a coronary care unit to run at three in the morning. The tablet is one tablet. The infusion is one hour. The entry form is short. Chapter Four returns to why. First, one more thing the design has to get right, because 1948 left it open.

## Chapter Three: The Telephone

The sealed envelope was the best available answer in 1948 to the question of who could know the next assignment. It has a weakness, and Sleight names it in his 2000 piece. With envelope randomisation, a physician who dislikes the allocation in the first envelope can open the next one. He says this happened quite recently in a hypertension trial, the Captopril Prevention Project, and cites it by name. Randomising by the day of the week, or by hospital, fails the same way: the person who decides who is eligible can see the pattern and steer.

The ISIS answer, per Sleight, was to randomise by telephone after the baseline data had been collected at a central office. The doctor rings. A person at the centre asks for the details that make the patient eligible, records them, and only then releases the assignment. By the time anyone knows which arm the patient is in, the entry decision and the baseline are on the central record and cannot be revised. A later trial in the same piece used a fax with computer scanning, the same idea with different hardware. Sleight's claim is that small leaks of this kind can produce large effects, even on the main result, and that they are exaggerated in any subgroup analysis.

That last clause joins the two halves of this episode. A subgroup is a smaller sample, and a small bias in who is enrolled shows up more in a small sample. The central telephone does not make subgroup analysis safe. It removes one reason for subgroup results to be wrong, and leaves the other: chance.

I told you in episode seventy that the sealed envelope's modern descendant is a system that releases the assignment after enrolment is locked. This is the descendant, as described from inside the collaboration. What the descendant adds is that nobody can open the next one.

## Chapter Four: What a Large Trial Is For

Why seventeen thousand? The Medical Research Council needed a hundred and seven people to see fourteen deaths against four, because the effect was enormous. Here the effect is a quarter of the deaths, in a disease where about one patient in nine died within five weeks on placebo. The difference between 9.4 and 11.8 percent is 2.4 points, and for a difference that size to be convincing, the groups have to be large.

The arithmetic is what Peto and his collaborators built their method on. The abstract reports the odds reduction for aspirin as twenty-three percent with a standard deviation of four. With about eighty-six hundred in each arm, that is what the noise allows. Halve the trial and the standard deviation grows by about the square root of two, and a result that was convincing becomes one you could argue with. Everything about the ISIS design is aimed at pushing patients through cheaply: a simple question, a short form, a cheap drug, a quick follow-up. The trial's name for the approach is, I believe, the large simple trial, though the phrase is not in either of the two accounts I held, and I am giving you its usual label from memory.

The bet in this approach is that moderate effects on common outcomes can be found and are the ones missed by trials small enough to be run with a research nurse on each ward. Because the event is common, even a modest proportional cut spares many people across a population. Sleight cites a textbook chapter by Collins, Peto, Gray and Parish on large-scale randomized evidence for the philosophy, and I have only its title.

There is a cost, and the cost leads to the rest of the episode. A trial built this large, with each arm pooled whole against the other, has by construction answered one question very well. The question is whether, on average, across this heterogeneous crowd, the treatment lowers deaths. A trial of seventeen thousand people is not seventeen thousand answers. It is one answer with a small error bar, and what you do with the pieces afterwards is a different exercise, with a much larger error bar, whatever the denominator on the cover says.

## Chapter Five: The Zodiac

Here is what was done, as Sleight and Peto describe it. Every patient had a date of birth entered on the form as an identifier. Sleight says this made it possible to divide the population into twelve groups by astrological sign. Peto adds the step the demonstration depends on: the results in each of the twelve were examined to see which looked least promising, and the least promising ones were then collected together. That gave a subgroup, Gemini and Libra, in which aspirin appeared totally ineffective, with a hundred and fifty deaths against a hundred and forty-seven. Peto reports that, in the other direction, aspirin appeared to halve mortality in one sign, Capricorn. The range across the twelve ran from nothing to a half.

Nothing about the stars is needed to explain this. Each sign holds about a twelfth of the patients, so something like fourteen hundred in each, and roughly a hundred and fifty deaths. That is a small sample, and the proportion of them that fall in the aspirin arm is subject to the sort of swing that coin flips give. If aspirin worked at the trial's overall rate in every sign, you would expect, from the totals, a division of the Gemini and Libra deaths, about three hundred between the two signs, of roughly a hundred and thirty-one on aspirin to a hundred and sixty-six on placebo. The observed split was the one in the table. That is about nineteen more deaths on the aspirin side than an even effect would give. The ordinary spread of a split of that size, by my own calculation from the binomial, is about eight and a half deaths, so the departure is a little over two spreads. That is not rare when there are sixty-six ways to pick two signs and the pair is chosen after looking. It was not drawn from a hat.

I should be clear that these last numbers are my arithmetic, not the authors', and assume an even effect in every sign. The conclusion does not need them. Peto's table of counts already carries it. All the aspirin benefit is in the ten signs that were not picked, six hundred and fifty-four deaths to eight hundred and sixty-nine, and adding the pair back to the ten gives the trial's overall deaths exactly, the numbers in the abstract. Adding the zodiac back in does not change the trial's verdict. It adds a sliver of noise to one arm of it and hands it a story.

The harm that story could do is real. Gemini and Libra would be told aspirin was useless for them. They would be a population in which, by the evidence of the whole trial, aspirin was as useful as in anyone else, and the evidence of the subgroup was a few deaths in one direction. Sleight's piece is blunt about the habit this teaches. Most physicians laughed at the zodiac result, he says, though not all. And when they were given a less ridiculous grouping, by country, by age, by severity, they were likely to believe it, forgetting the astrology, particularly when a pet theory could explain the result.

The joke has a structure, and it is the mechanism by which the demonstration is meant to work. The absurdity of the explanation protects you. No one can construct a reason that aspirin fails in Gemini. When the grouping is by country, or by sex, or by type of infarction, someone can, and the reason lies ready the moment the data come in. A subgroup result you can explain feels like knowledge, and the astrology is built so that the explanation is not available. The demonstration is a test of the reader's own habits, set when failing it costs nothing.

## Chapter Six: The Anterior Infarct

Sleight's piece is not a joke collection. Its middle section lists three occasions, from the field, on which believing a subgroup caused real harm, and the first is a case where a subgroup read as an answer.

Early trials of thrombolytic therapy, the clot-dissolving class that includes streptokinase, found that treatment clearly reduced mortality in patients with anterior infarction, the larger heart attacks in the front of the heart, who were at higher risk. This led, at first, to the conclusion that thrombolysis did not work in inferior infarction. As more data accumulated it became clear that it did work there too. Sleight says that restricting thrombolysis to anterior infarctions was an error that harmed patients, and gives the parallel history for beta-blockers. The early beta-blocker trials found benefit after a heart attack in the anterior group and only a non-significant trend in the inferior group, who had fewer events overall. When the studies were pooled, with more events to analyse, beta-blockers were beneficial in inferior infarction too.

Look at how each of those errors is built, which differs from the astrology error. No one picked the groups afterwards to make a point. Anterior against inferior is a perfectly natural cut, there is a mechanism one can imagine, and the results looked as though they supported it. What went wrong is the other half of the problem that Sleight spells out as power. A subgroup with fewer events has less ability to show an effect that is really there, and a difference between significant and non-significant is not evidence of a difference. The anterior group had many deaths and could show a benefit. The inferior group had few and could not, and the trial read the silence as an answer.

That second error, the false negative in a small subgroup, is the one Peto's table also models. His Libra and Gemini row is a false negative, a place where a real effect appears to vanish. The usual story told about subgroups is the false positive, a pharmaceutical finding in one corner. In practice, it is at least as often the opposite error that does damage, because it removes treatment from people who would have benefited, and the people harmed are not in any table.

Sleight tells two more instances. In a trial of an ACE inhibitor, ramipril, the HOPE study, the drug showed no significant effect in the American participants, and the result was discussed at a Food and Drug Administration hearing in May 2000. In a large trial comparing clot-dissolving regimens, an advantage for one drug was found in North American patients and not in the rest of the world, and Sleight himself, who had a theory about why, says the difference might simply have been chance, though he was concerned enough to argue a mechanism. He is willing to put his own pet theory in the dock beside everyone else's.

Sleight's piece also discloses something about ISIS-2 itself. Sleight reports that the trial was carried out in sixteen countries. For the streptokinase question, two countries had non-significantly negative results, and for aspirin a single different country did. He says the ISIS investigators saw results like this in their trials and did not report them, because of the possibility of harm from misinterpreting a statistical fluke, and a failure to use a useful treatment in that country. There is a plain accounting here. Sixteen countries and two drugs make thirty-two country-by-drug results. Three of the thirty-two came out negative. For a treatment that was truly helping in every country, a few of them being negative by chance is just what a trial would show.

Was it right to leave them out? The argument against is the one the evidence series has built since Fisher: a reader cannot judge what was looked at if some of it is withheld. The argument for is Sleight's, that three loose results would be read as three facts. The better course is the one his own piece takes by disclosing them years later, with the count of looks and a reading attached. The trial paper may have said some of this already, and I cannot open it.

## Chapter Seven: Three Remedies

What should a reader do with a subgroup? Peto's editorial has a short list, which he introduces by calling them partial remedies for an unavoidable conflict between what doctors want and what statistics can supply. He adds that informed judgements differ on how far each helps in a given case. I'll take them in his order and add what I think of each.

The first is to prespecify. Where there is a good prior reason to expect the proportional effect of a treatment to differ, one subgroup analysis can be written into the protocol, along with the direction of the expected difference. His example is time from the onset of symptoms to treatment in clot-dissolving trials: the earlier the treatment, the larger the expected benefit, so some studies wrote that analysis into the protocol. A single prespecified analysis can be taken somewhat more seriously. But protocols that prespecify several subgroup analyses as secondary outcomes, he says, can still yield importantly wrong answers. I think that is right: a promise to look at six subgroups is a promise of six chances at a fluke, which is not much better than twelve.

The abstract of ISIS-2 gives a small test of this remedy, on the time-to-treatment cut. It reports odds reductions in three windows, patients treated within four hours of pain, between five and twelve hours, and between thirteen and twenty-four. For streptokinase alone: thirty-five percent, sixteen, and twenty-one. For aspirin alone: twenty-five, then twenty-one twice. The shape you would predict from the clot-dissolving theory is a falling line, and for the first window it is the case. But the second and third windows do not fall. For streptokinase the middle window sits below the last, the wrong way round, though with standard deviations of seven and twelve it means nothing. Aspirin is nearly flat across all three, and there is no theory in which aspirin should lose power with delay. The authors, in the abstract, draw the sensible conclusion, that each agent showed evidence of benefit even for the late patients. Even the most plausible subgroup in the trial is a noisy line, and it should be read for what it supports, which is a benefit throughout.

The second remedy runs against instinct. Take the proportional risk reduction from the whole trial, or better still from a meta-analysis of all such trials, and treat it as the guide to every subgroup, giving little weight to the subgroup's own apparent result. Then adjust for the subgroup's absolute risk. Peto says this is clearly the right way to read the astrological findings. Sleight says it is more reliable statistically than examining the actual result in the subgroup, though possibly counter-intuitive. The reasoning is that the whole-trial estimate is far steadier than any one sign's, and that real differences in proportional effect between subgroups are usually smaller than the noise in measuring them. What does change between subgroups is the absolute benefit, because a treatment that halves a tiny risk helps almost nobody, and one that trims a large risk helps a lot. Peto says the classification of patients as high or low risk is often a sensible guide to which patients expect a small or a large gain, and may not need formal subgroup analysis at all.

The third remedy is the least portable: look at a more frequent outcome, such as recurrence in cancer, where the numbers are bigger and the pattern more stable, to see whether subgroup differences hold up on an outcome with more events. That is a tool for oncology more than for heart attacks, and I mention it only to show that the three remedies are not rival theories. Each is a way to buy more events or a stronger prior.

## Chapter Eight: The Other Demonstration

The astrology demonstration shows one failure, multiplicity: cut enough ways and something will look different. A paper from 1980 shows a second failure, and it sits beside the first because the zodiac cannot reach it.

Kerry Lee and colleagues at Duke published a simulated randomised trial in Circulation. They took one thousand and seventy-three consecutive patients from the Duke database, all with coronary artery disease and all treated medically, and randomised them into two groups. Both groups got the same care, so there was no treatment difference to find, and no overall difference in survival appeared. But in a subgroup of three hundred and ninety-seven patients, those with three-vessel disease and an abnormally contracting left ventricle, survival in group one was significantly different from group two. The authors then adjusted for prognostic factors, and the difference turned out to be the combined result of small imbalances in several of them. The groups had been randomised, so any imbalance was by chance, but in a subgroup with a steep prognosis, slight shifts in the factors that predict death add up to a visible difference. A second subgroup they found had a significant difference that the multivariable methods could not explain. I hold this paper only as its abstract, and I am giving you its account of itself.

Two mechanisms, then, and they ask for different habits. The zodiac is about how many cuts you made, and the remedy is to count them: twelve signs, sixteen countries, and the arithmetic of how many fluke findings that many looks will yield. Sleight gives the sum: carve a negative trial into forty pieces and at the 0.05 level you expect positive results in two. Lee is about how small the cut is, and the remedy is to distrust a subgroup, however few cuts were made, in which the groups were never given a chance to balance. Randomisation promises balance on average, across all the patients. It makes no promise inside a slice.

There is a link back to Fisher's argument in episode sixty-eight. He said the lottery guarantees the validity of the test, not the equality of the groups. In the whole trial, the test can take account of the possibility of imbalance, since it is built into the reference distribution. Inside a subgroup you chose after seeing the data, the test's reference distribution no longer describes what you did. The p-value in a subgroup is the answer to a question about a procedure you did not follow. And from episode sixty-nine, if the 0.05 is an error rate, it is the error rate of a whole procedure including the choice to look. Twelve signs at 0.05 each gives, by my arithmetic, a chance of about forty-six percent that at least one sign crosses the line when nothing is happening. The number on the printout is the same, and the procedure behind it is different.

## Chapter Nine: The Case Against the Joke

The demonstration has a case against it, in three parts.

First, it proves less than it seems. Peto and Sleight had a trial that was spectacularly positive, with an overall result far past the usual line. A huge overall effect is the best case for the proportional-effect rule: if aspirin works at all, the prior that it stops working in Gemini is about as low as priors go. The demonstration shows that a false subgroup can be manufactured. It says nothing about how often real heterogeneity exists in the treatments we study, and the people who run trials do not usually have the luxury of a signal as loud as this one for their overall result. When the overall effect is small or borderline, the reasoning gets harder, since there is no strong overall estimate to import into the subgroups.

Second, the remedy has a hidden assumption. Using the overall proportional reduction for every subgroup assumes the effect is the same in proportion everywhere. Sleight says it is unlikely that results in a subgroup are qualitatively different from the main result, though they may be quantitatively different, in young against old or high risk against low. That is a statement of belief about how the world is, and it is probably right for aspirin and heart attacks, where the mechanism is shared across all bodies. It may be wrong for a treatment with a mechanism that depends on a patient characteristic, a drug targeting a receptor some patients lack. Peto himself calls the remedies matters on which informed judgements differ. They are the habits of a field in which most effects are moderate and broadly shared, and I do not know that they transfer unchanged to every field.

Third, there is a quieter objection about what the demonstration does to a reader. If the lesson learned is that all subgroup analysis is noise, the reader is left with a rule that is simple, memorable and wrong in a different direction. Sleight does not say that. He gives credit to prespecified subgroups, to absolute-risk reasoning, to pooled estimates. But the mnemonic of the zodiac is stronger than the caveats, and a reader who takes away only the joke will throw away the anterior-versus-inferior question, which was a good question badly answered.

And a further fact about my own position: I read no dissents. Both accounts are from within the collaboration whose trial is the example, and a reception pass on a paper this standard would have turned up people who think the demonstration oversells the case. I did not find them in what I could reach, and I am saying so rather than inventing a critic.

## Chapter Ten: The Plan Written First

The listener to this show writes statistical analysis plans, and the zodiac gives a test for one section of them.

Every subgroup in a plan needs three things written down before the data exist: why it is there, which direction the difference is expected to go, and what will be done with the result if it goes the other way. If you cannot supply a reason and a direction, the analysis does not belong in the plan as a question. It belongs in a labelled exploratory section, and the sentence reporting it should carry the number of cuts that were made alongside it, as a denominator.

Imagine a trial of a school supplement across forty schools in eight districts, with the made-up numbers there only to show the arithmetic. The plan lists subgroups by sex, by age band, by baseline height-for-age, and by district, which comes to a dozen or so separate cuts. The sentence that matters is the one that says how the district results will be read. By Peto's second remedy, the pooled proportional effect is the default guide to each district, and a district that comes out flat is reported as a district where chance had room, unless the plan said in advance that this one would differ and why. If the plan did say so, it is the single prespecified analysis that may be taken somewhat more seriously. If it listed all eight districts as secondary outcomes, it has made the error Peto names.

The unit-of-randomisation test from episode sixty-eight applies as well, and it makes this worse. In a trial randomised by school, the schools are the units, and a district-level subgroup holds only five of them. The Gemini and Libra subgroup in ISIS-2 had about three hundred deaths to work with. Five schools are five coin tosses, and no number of children inside them rescues the effective sample size. The noise Sleight and Peto describe in a subgroup of seventeen thousand patients is a mild version of the noise in a handful of schools.

Finally, the sentence in the paper's discussion that mentions a subgroup should do the thing ISIS did not do with its sixteen countries. If the plan promised to look at them, the paper should say what was seen, including the flat ones, and the pooled estimate beside them. A reader can do something with a flat result, if she is also told how many results there were.

The trial's finding is the plain one: two cheap treatments, given early, each spare about twenty-five patients in a thousand, and together about fifty-two. Among those born under Libra and Gemini, a hundred and fifty on aspirin died and a hundred and forty-seven on placebo, and nothing in the protocol gave a reason to read anything into three deaths.
