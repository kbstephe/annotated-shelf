## Chapter One: Two Ways to Hurt a Patient With a Trial

In the early trials of clot-dissolving drugs for heart attack, streptokinase clearly saved lives when the infarct was in the front wall of the heart. Where it was in the lower wall, the benefit was smaller and not significant. For a while, Peter Sleight recalled in 2000, that was read as a finding: thrombolysis works for anterior infarction and not for inferior. As more trials came in and were pooled, the inferior patients turned out to benefit too. They had simply had fewer deaths to prevent, so their slice of each trial held too few events to show it. The same thing happened, by his account, with beta-blockers after infarction, where an early non-significant trend in inferior infarcts was read as no effect until a meta-analysis of all the trials showed otherwise. Sleight, an Oxford cardiologist who had helped run the ISIS trials, listed both as subgroup readings that had harmed patients, by withholding a treatment that worked.

Now the other direction. In 1999 the RALES trial showed that spironolactone, added to standard care in selected patients with severe heart failure, cut the risk of death and readmission by thirty to thirty-five percent. Guidelines followed, and use of the drug in heart failure rose fourfold. Richard Kravitz, Naihua Duan and Joel Braslow, writing in the Milbank Quarterly in 2004, retold what came next from a study by Juurlink and colleagues: hospital admissions for dangerously high potassium, a known side effect, rose more than fourfold, and deaths from all causes did not fall. The authors suggested that part of the explanation was who got the drug: patients in the community were selected differently from those in the trial, and monitored to different local standards. The average effect, applied to people it did not describe, did harm.

So there are two ways to hurt a patient with a trial. You can believe a subgroup, and deny a treatment to people who would have gained from it. Or trust the average, and prescribe it to patients for whom its balance of benefit and harm is different from the one the trial reported. The first error is the one statisticians have warned about for forty years. The second is the one clinicians have always suspected, and the one evidence-based medicine was accused of ignoring.

This episode is a duel between those two warnings. On one side is Salim Yusuf, then at the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, with Janet Wittes, John Probstfield and Herman Tyroler, whose 1991 paper in JAMA on analysing subgroups in trials became the standard text of the sceptical position, and Sleight's short essay of 2000, whose title tells you to enjoy subgroup analyses but not to believe them. On the other is Kravitz's 2004 paper on what its title calls the trouble with averages, and the programme that David Kent, at Tufts, built on it: a 2010 proposal with Peter Rothwell, John Ioannidis, Douglas Altman and Rodney Hayward, a 2018 review in the BMJ, and, in 2020, a consensus statement that gave the approach a name, predictive approaches to treatment effect heterogeneity, or PATH for short.

The question running through all of it is simple to ask. A trial reports one number for thousands of people. Whom does that number describe?

## Chapter Two: The Case for the Overall Result

Start with the sceptics, because their case has the longer record, and because most trialists were trained in it.

Yusuf's 1991 paper sits behind a paywall, and what I hold of it is the abstract, together with the many papers that restate it. The abstract carries the argument in compressed form. Interactions come in two kinds. A quantitative interaction is a difference in degree: the treatment helps everyone, but some more than others. A qualitative interaction is a difference in kind: one group gains while another is harmed, or gains nothing. Yusuf and his colleagues held that quantitative interactions are much more likely than qualitative ones, and are probably present whether or not a trial can see them. Apparent qualitative interactions, by contrast, should generally be disbelieved, because they have usually failed to replicate. From that follows the working rule. The overall trial result is usually a better guide to the direction of effect in a subgroup than the effect observed within that subgroup.

That rule sounds like a refusal to look. It is better described as a bet about where noise lives. A subgroup is a smaller sample, so its estimate is noisier than the whole trial's. If you look at enough subgroups, some will point the wrong way by chance alone. Sleight put the arithmetic in its plainest form: divide a large trial with a negative result into forty subgroups, by country, age, sex, blood pressure and the rest, and at the usual five percent threshold you expect two of them to come out positive. The astrological subgroups of ISIS-2, in which aspirin appeared to do nothing for patients born under Gemini or Libra, are the famous demonstration, and this show spent an episode on them. Sleight's point in 2000 was what happens next. Most physicians laughed at the zodiac. Shown a subgroup that came with a plausible story attached, the same physicians tended to believe it.

How often would a perfectly uniform treatment produce such a story? Here is an invented case, worked with a normal approximation only to show the arithmetic. Suppose a trial has eighty percent power for its overall effect, and the effect really is the same in men and women. Split the trial in half by sex. Each half has about half the information, and its power drops to roughly fifty percent. The chance that exactly one half reaches significance, so that the paper can say the drug works in men but not in women, or the reverse, is then also about a half. Half the time, a treatment with no difference between the sexes produces a forest plot that seems to show one.

Sara Brookes and her colleagues at Bristol ran this kind of exercise properly, by simulation, for a Health Technology Assessment report in 2001 and a paper in the Journal of Clinical Epidemiology in 2004. With no real difference between subgroups, a formal test of interaction came out significant five percent of the time, as it should. Testing each subgroup separately did much worse: a significant effect in one subgroup only turned up in anywhere from seven to sixty-four percent of their simulations, depending on the trial. And the honest test was weak. A trial with eighty percent power for its overall effect had only twenty-nine percent power to detect an interaction of the same size as that effect. To detect it with the power planned for the main result, the sample would have to be four times as large, and for an interaction a fifth the size of the overall effect, the inflation factor exceeded a hundred.

There is one more cost, and the previous episode supplied it. A subgroup reported because it passed a significance test is a filtered estimate. In the invented trial above, a significant half, at roughly fifty percent power, overstates its true effect by about forty percent on average. The story attached to it, that the drug is stronger in men, is built on an exaggerated number before anyone has asked whether the difference is real.

Put together, the sceptics' case runs like this. Real differences in kind are rare. Apparent ones are common and mostly chance. The tests that could detect real ones are weak, and the subgroup estimates that survive them are inflated. So apply the overall result to everyone the trial enrolled, and treat every subgroup finding as a hypothesis until another trial confirms it.

Sleight added one more sentence. If you want the benefit in a subgroup, he wrote, take the overall proportional reduction from the whole trial and apply it to the absolute risk of the subgroup you care about. That is more reliable, he said, though possibly counter-intuitive, than reading off the result the trial observed in that subgroup. So the sceptics never held that all patients gain equally. Their claim was that each gains a similar proportion of his or her own risk. I will come back to how much that concedes.

## Chapter Three: The Trouble With Averages

Kravitz, Duan and Braslow began from the clinician's side of the room. A trial's average effect, they argued, can be a mixture: substantial benefit for some, little benefit for many, and harm for a few. A modest average is what you would see from that mixture, and it would describe almost nobody.

They named four sources of the mixture. Patients differ in their risk of the bad outcome without treatment, in how strongly they respond to the drug, in their vulnerability to its side effects, and in how much they value the outcomes in play. Each of the four can vary a great deal. The paper's examples run from rheumatic heart disease, which multiplies the stroke risk in atrial fibrillation about eighteenfold and so the benefit of anticoagulation with it, to an inherited enzyme deficiency that makes a leukaemia drug ten to twenty times more toxic, to men facing prostate surgery whose valuations of impotence and incontinence spread across the whole scale from nearly worthless to no loss at all.

The first of the four carries most of the argument, and it is arithmetic rather than biology. Kravitz quoted Paul Glasziou and Les Irwig, writing in the BMJ in 1995: the patients at greatest risk of a disease will have the greatest net benefit from treatment, since benefit usually rises with risk while harm stays comparatively fixed. A drug that cuts the risk of stroke by a fixed proportion removes many strokes from people likely to have one and few from people unlikely to. Its bleeding risk does not care which group you are in. Somewhere along the scale of baseline risk the two lines cross, and below the crossing the treatment does more harm than good. The paper's case from atrial fibrillation is warfarin, which reduced stroke by an average of about two thirds but not, in a 1994 analysis of the trials, in patients under sixty-five without hypertension, diabetes or a previous stroke, whose annual risk was near one percent and for whom the bleeding cost could exceed any benefit.

So far this is compatible with Sleight's advice: constant proportional benefit, varying absolute benefit. Kravitz pushed past it with two further claims. The first is that responsiveness and vulnerability vary too, so the proportional benefit itself need not be constant, and that little effort had gone into assessing all four dimensions together. The second borrows an image from Nicholas Longford: a trial is an experiment and a survey rolled into one, and usually a good experiment and a poor survey. Narrow inclusion criteria can make the effect look uniform in the sample while it varies in the population the drug is then sold to. The spironolactone story is that failure at scale.

It would be easy to read Kravitz as the clinician's revenge on the statisticians. The paper is more careful than that, and its concessions belong in the steelman. Without good data on a patient's risk, responsiveness and vulnerability, the authors wrote, the average effect from trials gives the best guidance available, all the more since the results of ad hoc subgroup analyses are often spurious; they cited Yusuf for that. They wanted subgroups chosen with judgement and tested with interaction terms, not many small ones assembled for representativeness. And they made one point that still unsettles trial policy. The American requirement that women, minorities and children be included in federally funded trials, they argued, may in a single trial do nothing except guarantee that the estimate for each group is unreliable, because there are too few of each to say anything.

What Kravitz did not supply was a method. The paper ended on the hope that a clinician with a handheld computer might one day calculate a patient's risk, responsiveness and vulnerability, and talk it through. That is where Kent came in.

## Chapter Four: One Score Instead of Twenty Splits

Kent's 2010 paper in the journal Trials starts from an observation about the standard forest plot. Each row splits the trial on one characteristic: men and women, old and young, diabetic and not. But patients carry all their characteristics at once. A diabetic seventy-year-old woman with kidney disease and a large infarct is not described by any single row. When you split a trial by sex alone, the men and the women each contain every mix of age, comorbidity and severity, so the two halves are more alike in risk than they look. One-variable-at-a-time analysis compares groups that differ on one thing, which is why, the authors argued, it so often finds the consistency of effect it reports. They called that conclusion false: the groups compared are more similar than dissimilar.

Their alternative is to combine the characteristics that predict the outcome into a single score of baseline risk, preferably one developed outside the trial, then sort the trial's patients by that score and report the treatment effect in each band of it, on both the relative and the absolute scale. One prespecified analysis replaces twenty, so the multiplicity problem shrinks. And the comparison now sets patients who differ on many risk factors at once against each other, so the analysis has more power to find variation that matters.

How much does risk vary inside a trial? More than the forest plot suggests. Kent's group reported that in large trials the event rate in the highest-risk quarter of patients is often five to twenty times that in the lowest. And they made the point that a mediocre risk model is enough to see it: a score with an area under the curve of 0.6 to 0.65, modest at telling one patient from another, still gave more power to detect risk-based differences in effect than the strongest single risk factor examined alone. Scores clinicians already use, such as the TIMI score in acute coronary syndromes and the CHADS2 score in atrial fibrillation, sit in that range.

Then they showed why it matters, with a table of numbers they invented to make the arithmetic plain. Suppose a treatment reduces everyone's risk of the outcome by a quarter. Most patients, three in four, have a four percent risk over the trial, and the rest a twenty percent risk. Across the whole trial the risk falls from eight percent to six. For the typical patient, though, the fall is from four to three, and for the riskier group from twenty to fifteen. Now add a small harm, two serious adverse events per thousand patients per year, and run the trial for five years. That adds one percentage point of harm to everyone. The riskier patients still gain four points. The typical patient gains exactly nothing, since benefit and harm are each a single point. The trial's summary reports a moderate benefit, a relative reduction of about an eighth, while most of its patients received no net benefit at all. Widen the gap, so the typical patient's risk is two percent and the others' thirty, and the summary still shows a benefit while that same majority is net harmed.

Nothing in that table requires the treatment's biology to differ between patients. The relative effect of the drug on the disease is the same in every patient, and so is the harm. The heterogeneity comes from the denominator: how much disease each patient had to lose.

The 2010 paper made five recommendations, and two of them change what a trial report looks like. Report the distribution of predicted risk in the first table of the paper, by arm, so readers can see how spread out the population is. And report the treatment effect, relative and absolute, across bands of that risk. Other subgroup analyses should be labelled as primary, few in number, prespecified with their expected direction and justified by biology or previous evidence, or as secondary, explicitly exploratory and published in an appendix. The paper called itself provisional. The 2020 statement was the consensus version, built with a panel that included Steven Goodman, Michael Pencina, Andrew Vickers and Ewout Steyerberg, and it divided the field into risk modelling, the score-based approach, and effect modelling, which tries to predict each patient's benefit directly from treatment-by-covariate interactions. It gave criteria for when the first is likely to find differences that matter clinically, and attached caveats to the second.

## Chapter Five: Thirty Thousand Heart Attacks, Sorted by Risk

The cleanest place to see the risk-based approach run to its end is a trial everyone already knows. GUSTO-I, reported in 1993, randomised some forty thousand patients with acute myocardial infarction among four thrombolytic strategies. In 2020 Steyerberg, a Leiden methodologist who served on the PATH panel, used it for a worked tutorial on Frank Harrell's blog, Statistical Thinking, comparing the accelerated tissue plasminogen activator regimen, tPA, against streptokinase in the thirty thousand five hundred and ten patients assigned to one or the other with known thirty-day survival.

The headline is a modest one. Thirty-day mortality was 6.3 percent on tPA and 7.3 percent on streptokinase, an absolute difference of one percentage point. On the odds ratio scale that is 0.85.

Steyerberg then fitted a standard prediction model from a handful of standard characteristics, among them age, blood pressure, heart rate and the location of the infarct. Applied to each patient, it gives a predicted risk of death without tPA. He asked first whether the relative effect of tPA varied. Testing treatment against all the model's variables together gave a p-value for interaction of 0.57; testing it against the predicted risk itself gave 0.35. Neither gives any evidence that tPA's proportional effect differed across patients.

Then he sorted the patients into quarters by predicted risk and reported the death rates in each. In the lowest quarter, 0.7 percent of patients died on tPA and 0.9 percent on streptokinase. In the second quarter, 1.9 and 2.6. In the third, 5.0 and 6.0. In the highest, 17.4 and 20.0.

Work those through. Expressed as a proportion of each quarter's own death rate, tPA's benefit is roughly similar across the four, somewhere between a seventh and a quarter, which is what the interaction tests said. In absolute terms the picture changes completely. In the lowest quarter, tPA prevents about one death for every six hundred patients treated instead of streptokinase. In the second, roughly one per hundred and sixty; in the third, one in a hundred; and among the sickest patients, one for every thirty-nine.

Now ask where the lives were saved. Multiply each quarter's absolute benefit by its number of patients and add them up. The top quarter accounts for about three fifths of all the deaths tPA prevented in the trial. The bottom one contributes less than a twentieth. The sickest quarter's risk of death was about twenty times the lowest quarter's.

Hold that result against the two camps. Yusuf's camp would say, correctly, that there was no evidence of a subgroup effect: the relative benefit was consistent, and the overall result was the right guide to the direction of effect for everyone. Kent's camp would answer, also correctly, that the trial's summary number described almost nobody. The summary's one point matched the third quarter only; the bottom half of the trial gained far less, and the top quarter more than twice as much. For a drug that, as Sleight noted, caused significantly more cerebral haemorrhage than streptokinase, especially in the elderly, the decision for a patient in the lowest quarter is a different decision from the one for a patient in the highest, even though the drug's relative effect was the same in both.

The GUSTO example is the gentle case, where risk varies and the relative effect does not. Kent's 2018 review in the BMJ, with Steyerberg and David van Klaveren, showed the harsher one. DANAMI-2, a Danish trial of primary angioplasty against clot-dissolving drugs in ST-elevation infarction, reported its subgroups in the conventional way and found the effect consistent across all of them. When the patients were later stratified by the TIMI risk score, the roughly three quarters at low risk turned out to have received no mortality benefit, with a non-significant trend toward harm, while the high-risk remainder gained about ten percentage points. This time the interaction on the relative scale was significant as well. The forest plot had reported consistency; the risk score found a population in which most patients gained nothing.

## Chapter Six: The Duel Was About Scales

Set those two results beside each other and the duel looks different from the way either camp described it.

Yusuf's principle was about the direction of effect, and about differences in kind. Read on the relative scale, it has held up remarkably well. In GUSTO-I, the relative effect of tPA was consistent across the risk spectrum, and across the conventional subgroups Steyerberg also plotted. Harrell, as I will come to, thinks credible evidence of relative effects varying between patients has been minimal outside cancers defined by tumour markers. On the relative scale, the sceptics were mostly right.

Kent's principle was about the size of benefit for a patient, and that lives on the absolute scale. There, heterogeneity is not a hypothesis to be tested. It is guaranteed whenever risk varies and the treatment works at all, because a patient with almost no risk has almost nothing to lose. Kent's 2018 review put it in one line of algebra: every measure of treatment effect depends on the control event rate, and when that rate varies, at most one of those measures can stay constant. If the relative effect is constant, the absolute one cannot be. On the absolute scale, the clinicians' suspicion was right, and it was right for a reason that needs no biology.

And here Sleight's extra sentence comes back. Apply the trial's overall proportional reduction to the absolute risk of the subgroup you care about, he wrote. That is risk modelling done by hand. Kent's programme takes the sceptics' best estimate of relative effect, the pooled one, and asks the one question the pooled number cannot answer: how much risk does this patient have for it to act on? The two camps were not offering rival estimates of the same quantity. One was protecting the relative effect from noise. The other was refusing to let a protected relative effect stand in for an absolute one. Both rejected the same thing, which was the forest plot read row by row.

That forest plot fails in both directions at once, and the two camps had each catalogued one of its failures. Read for differences, it produces false positives: Gemini and Libra, the subgroup that works in men only, the half that passed. Read for sameness, it gives false reassurance: DANAMI-2's consistency of effect across rows that were too alike in risk to show anything. Each row splits on one variable, so it is both too noisy to trust when it differs and too blunt to find what differs.

Kent's 2018 review ran the false-positive side as a short calculation. Suppose that among the subgroup variables a trial examines, five percent truly modify the effect, and that the trial has thirty percent power to detect each one, which is roughly Brookes's figure. Out of a hundred variables tested, the five real ones yield one and a half significant results. The ninety-five null ones yield nearly five false ones. So among about six significant subgroup findings, only one and a half are real, a quarter. Raise the prior to a quarter of the variables, by testing only well-motivated ones, and about two thirds of significant findings are real. Even then, Kent's group noted, the real ones are overestimated, because the test selects the exaggerated draws; they used the name testimation bias. That is the previous episode's significance filter, applied to the rows of a forest plot instead of to a pilot study.

There is a connection backwards too, to the episode on adjusting for baseline. The risk score Kent asks for is built from the same prognostic variables that Lin and Freedman were arguing about adjusting for. Adjustment uses them to sharpen the estimate of one relative effect. Risk modelling spreads that relative effect across the patients and read off what each band can gain. The covariates are the same. The question has moved from how well we know the effect to whom it is large for.

## Chapter Seven: The Case Against the Score

If the risk score reconciles the two camps, why is there still an argument? Because the score has critics of its own, and the most outspoken is Frank Harrell, the Vanderbilt biostatistician whose blog hosted Steyerberg's tutorial.

Harrell's first objection is to the word heterogeneity. After the 2018 meeting on the evidence and the individual patient that Kent organised, Harrell wrote that he would call the absolute-scale phenomenon risk magnification: a low-risk patient cannot have a large absolute risk reduction, because there is no room to move a probability that is already near zero. He thought risk magnification the single most useful idea for medical decisions, more useful than genomic precision medicine and far cheaper. But he denied it was heterogeneity of treatment effect in any interesting sense. Defined on the absolute scale, heterogeneity is always present, so the word stops meaning anything; only on a relative scale can a treatment's effect be constant across patients, and only there can differences be related to how a drug works. Researchers who find that older patients gain more one week and patients with comorbidities gain more the next, he wrote, are labelling one fact twice. Whatever raises risk raises absolute benefit.

That is a fair objection to a vocabulary, and Kent's camp half-accepts it. The PATH statement defines heterogeneity on a selected scale and says plainly that the absolute scale is the one for decisions. The disagreement left over is about emphasis. Harrell wants trials to estimate the relative effect well and leave the absolute calculation to the point of care, where a clinician can multiply it by an estimate of this patient's risk from any good source, even a cohort study. Kent wants the risk-stratified table in the trial paper itself, because otherwise, in his phrase, the trial's results are incompletely disclosed. Harrell is right that the arithmetic needs no trial data once the relative effect is known. Kent is right that almost nobody does it, and that a guideline panel reading a summary number of one point will write one recommendation for everyone.

The second objection is to the quarters. Harrell was worried, at the same meeting, to see speakers cutting predicted risk into quantile groups. Risk is continuous, he pointed out, and the boundaries of a quartile are set by who happened to enrol, so changing the inclusion criteria moves them; they reflect demographics, not medicine. Steyerberg's tutorial concedes the point in its own text: the quarters are for illustration, and better estimation of benefit avoids grouping. GUSTO-I's quarters were a way of making a smooth curve audible. A trial that took them as decision thresholds would be repeating, in a new form, the cutting the method was meant to replace.

The third objection is technical. Some researchers build the risk model on the control arm only, reasoning that baseline risk is what patients would have had without treatment. Harrell and Kent both warn against it. A model fitted to untreated patients alone learns their idiosyncrasies, and when it is applied to both arms it can make an inert treatment look beneficial in high-risk patients and harmful in low-risk ones. Kent's review says even very modest overfitting can bias the estimates substantially, because the absolute treatment effect is small compared with the risk being predicted. The safeguard is a model developed outside the trial, or one fitted to both arms blind to assignment. External models are the honest choice and are often missing: Kent's 2010 paper admits that a registry of validated prediction tools for every major trial outcome did not exist, and that the modelling literature fell short of that ideal even in fields with a long tradition of it.

The fourth objection is to the ambition beyond risk. Effect modelling, which tries to predict each patient's benefit from interactions selected in the trial's own data, runs straight back into Yusuf's problem. Kent's own review says so: selecting significant interaction terms is in many respects identical to one-at-a-time subgroup analysis, with the same false positives and the same exaggeration, and needs many times more events per term than a prognostic model. Harrell's arithmetic is blunter. Estimating an interaction, under the best conditions, takes about four times the sample needed for the average effect, which matches Brookes's inflation factor. And he made a prediction about where all this leads: that researchers will be surprised by how little differential treatment effects matter compared with patient risk factors and risk magnification. He also gave a reason to be suspicious of any claim of heterogeneity in a trial whose overall effect is near zero. To say a treatment with a null average helps some patients is to say it harms others, and that assumption should be questioned before it is believed.

Against Yusuf's side, the case is just as hard, and it comes from Kent's invented table. The rule that qualitative interactions should generally be disbelieved was stated for differences in the drug's effect on the disease. But a treatment with a fixed harm and a proportional benefit produces a qualitative interaction on the net absolute scale by arithmetic alone: the patients below the crossing point are net harmed. Kent's 2010 table lists a real example, activated protein C for severe sepsis, whose main trial showed a significant overall reduction in mortality, but whose benefit was confined to the half of patients at high baseline risk, while the lower-risk half bore the bleeding risk without the survival gain. A reader who applied the overall result to everyone, as Yusuf's working rule advised, would have treated those patients for nothing but the chance of a bleed. Yusuf's abstract itself points the way out: it recommends interpreting subgroups in the context of known biologic mechanisms and patient prognosis. Prognosis is the risk score. The sceptics had the ingredient and stopped short of the recipe.

## Chapter Eight: What the Subgroup Section Should Say

So the verdict is less a win than a division of the field. Yusuf and Sleight win on the relative scale: a difference in kind between patient groups, found by splitting a trial one variable at a time, should be disbelieved until it replicates, and the overall relative effect is the best estimate for every row of the forest plot. Kravitz and Kent win on the absolute scale: the summary number describes the patient at average risk, who is often not a typical patient at all, and the benefit for most of the trial can be much smaller than reported, or absent. Harrell wins on vocabulary and loses, I think, on where the table belongs. A calculation everyone could do and almost nobody does belongs in the paper.

In an analysis plan, the division becomes a short paragraph. Name the risk model before unblinding, external if one exists and otherwise fitted to both arms blind to assignment, and commit to reporting predicted risk by arm and the treatment effect across it, relative and absolute, with the interaction tested on the continuous score rather than on bands. Let that be the trial's primary look at heterogeneity. Any other subgroup is then either primary, with its biology, its expected direction and a penalty for how many there are, or exploratory, labelled so and kept out of the abstract. And no subgroup claim rests on one row being significant while another is not, which is how the invented trial split by sex manufactured a difference half the time.

Picture a journal club next month. The paper's forest plot shows a hazard ratio of 0.70 in men, with an interval from 0.55 to 0.89, and 0.95 in women, with an interval from 0.70 to 1.29. The discussion says the treatment works in men. The arithmetic to test that claim fits on the back of the handout. Take the logarithm of each hazard ratio; the difference between them is about 0.31. Each interval's width on the log scale, divided by four, gives roughly its standard error, about 0.12 for the men and 0.16 for the women. Square those, add, take the square root: about 0.20. The difference is about one and a half standard errors, a p-value near 0.12, and the paper's own numbers give no evidence that the sexes differ. Then ask the second question, the one the forest plot cannot answer: what were the event rates in each group, and in each band of risk? If the women in the trial had half the men's event rate, the absolute benefit for them would be smaller even if the relative effect were identical, and that, not a difference in the drug, may be the clinical fact the authors have half seen.

For reading, take Sleight's essay first, three pages in a small open journal, Current Controlled Trials in Cardiovascular Medicine, which has the inferior infarcts, the zodiac and the advice about applying the overall result to subgroup risk. Then Kent's 2010 proposal, which is short and whose two invented tables are the argument. Kravitz's Milbank paper is longer and more discursive, best for its four sources of difference between patients and its section on guidelines. Kent's 2018 review in the BMJ is the fullest statement, and its figures on DANAMI-2 and the Diabetes Prevention Program are what to look at. For the case against, read Harrell's post on heterogeneity and precision medicine from 2018, and Steyerberg's GUSTO-I tutorial on the same blog if you want to see the method run in code. Yusuf's 1991 paper is paywalled; its abstract holds the principle, and the rest reaches most readers through the papers that cite it.
