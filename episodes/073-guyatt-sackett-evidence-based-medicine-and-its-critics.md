description: "A satirical parachute trial opens the question of what the evidence-based medicine movement actually claimed in 1992. The episode follows the claim, the hierarchy built on it, and the five problems its own admirers listed in 2014."

## Chapter One: The parachute that nobody could test

In December 2003, two researchers named Gordon Smith and Jill Pell published a systematic review in the British Medical Journal. They searched Medline, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library for randomised trials of parachutes, with death or major trauma after a fall as the outcome, and they found none. Their conclusion, as the abstract gives it, is that parachutes have never been put through rigorous evaluation, that advocates of evidence-based medicine have criticised the adoption of treatments judged on observational data alone, and that everyone might benefit if the most radical of those advocates organised a double blind, randomised, placebo controlled, crossover trial of the parachute.

Fifteen years later somebody did one. In December 2018 a group of researchers published the PARACHUTE trial, also in the BMJ. Over about a year the investigators approached ninety-two passengers on commercial and private aircraft and asked whether they would agree to be randomised to jump with a parachute or with an empty backpack. Sixty-nine either refused or were judged ineligible. Twenty-three agreed, and the paper says plainly that most of the people randomised were members of the study team. Nobody in either group died or was seriously hurt, the difference was nil, and the P value was above 0.9.

The reason is in the next sentence of the results. The twenty-three jumped from an average height of 0.6 metres, with the aircraft at a standstill: aeroplanes and helicopters parked on the ground. The ninety-two people they had screened were, on average, at more than nine thousand metres and moving at eight hundred. The passengers who stood to benefit most from the parachute had declined, and the paper's conclusion is that when a community already believes strongly in a treatment, a randomised trial can end up enrolling the people least likely to need it.

This is a joke, and the authors say so in the discussion. They tried to register the trial with a clinical trials registry in Sri Lanka, which refused on the grounds that the question lacked scientific validity, and the authors write that they agree with the decision.

But the two parachute papers are jokes with a target, and they have different targets. The first mocks a reading of evidence-based medicine in which anything not randomised is not known. The second mocks the opposite failure, in which a trial is perfectly randomised and the people who would have shown the effect never entered it. Both assume that the movement laid down a specific rule about how to know a treatment works. This episode is about what it actually claimed, who made the claim, what it built, and which of the criticisms of its first quarter century are aimed at the idea and which at what institutions did with the idea. The place to start is the thing the jokes assume, a claim made in 1992.

## Chapter Two: A paradigm for teaching residents

The founding paper, "Evidence-Based Medicine: A New Approach to Teaching the Practice of Medicine," appeared in the Journal of the American Medical Association in November 1992 under the byline of a working group, led, in the usual telling, by Gordon Guyatt at McMaster University in Canada. I could not open the paper from here. What I hold is its title, its place in the journal, and the way PubMed indexes it, under teaching, internship and residency, and educational models. So the first fact about the movement is a modest one. It began as a proposal about how to train residents, not as a theory of knowledge.

The ambition was in the word the authors chose, which a later paper by the movement's own supporters recalls. Trisha Greenhalgh, Jeremy Howick and Neal Maskrey, writing for a group that called itself the Renaissance Group, say that the 1992 authors announced a new paradigm for teaching and practising clinical medicine. Tradition, anecdote and theoretical reasoning from the basic sciences were to be replaced by evidence from high quality randomised trials and observational studies, in combination with clinical expertise and the needs and wishes of the patient.

A listener who has been through the Kuhn episodes will hear what the word promises. A paradigm in Kuhn's sense is a set of exemplars that tells a field what counts as a problem and what counts as a solution, and a paradigm shift replaces the exemplars. The 1992 authors were claiming that an internist's exemplar of good practice had been a respected teacher's pattern recognition backed up by pathophysiology, and that it should become a reading skill: formulate the question, find the study, ask whether its comparison was fair, and apply the answer to this patient. The Users' Guides to the Medical Literature, a long-running series that I believe began in the same journal, packaged that skill as checklists for each type of paper. The Cochrane Collaboration, which Greenhalgh's paper lists among the achievements, supplied the pooled evidence to read. Archie Cochrane, in episode sixty-six, had supplied the diagnosis, that medicine did not know which of its treatments worked, and the 1992 paper supplied what a clinician might do about it on a Tuesday.

Four years later the movement's senior figures answered the first wave of objections in a two-page BMJ editorial, by David Sackett and four colleagues, titled "Evidence based medicine: what it is and what it isn't." That text did not open from here either, so what follows is from memory and the title. As I recall it, the editorial defines the practice as the integration of individual clinical expertise with the best available external evidence, and goes out of its way to say that the practice is not restricted to randomised trials and is not cookbook medicine. A movement that has to publish "what it isn't" four years in has been misread already, and the misreadings are the subject of the parachute jokes.

There were, then, three parts in the original claim, and keeping them apart is most of what it takes to argue about it sensibly. The first part is a skill, appraising studies. The second is a ranking, in which some designs are less open to bias than others. The third is a decision rule that combines the evidence with the clinician's judgement and the patient's wishes. Most of the later quarrels attack one part while pretending to attack all three.

## Chapter Three: What it replaced, and what the ranking ranks

What did the movement replace? Greenhalgh's list is tradition, anecdote and theoretical reasoning. The third is the one that surprises people, because reasoning from physiology sounds like science. The hormone therapy story from episode seventy-two shows why it was a target. A plausible mechanism said that estrogen should protect the arteries, large cohort studies agreed, and a treatment taken by a large share of postmenopausal women for prevention had a mechanism, a literature and expert endorsement. Then a randomised trial found a higher risk of coronary heart disease. Mechanism, literature and endorsement all agreed with one another, and none of the three was a test of the claim. Only the trial was.

Tradition has a signature too, and Greenhalgh's paper provides one. Arthroscopic washout of the knee, a procedure whose benefit, the authors write, is unproved except when there is a known loose body in the joint, was done at a rate that varied from three to forty-eight per hundred thousand across England. That is a sixteen-fold spread in how often an English patient with a painful knee gets the operation, depending on where the patient lives, for a procedure whose benefit the authors call unproved. Variation of that size is what practice looks like when it is steered by habit and local custom, and the movement's strongest argument was that the habits had never been tested.

To read evidence for this purpose, the movement used a ranking. As I know it from memory, the hierarchy runs from systematic reviews of randomised trials at the top, through single trials, cohort studies and case-control studies, to case series and expert opinion at the bottom. The ranking is usually drawn as a pyramid and often misread as a ranking of importance, the first error in how it is taught. The pyramid ranks susceptibility to bias, and each step up removes a specific way of being fooled.

A cohort study compares people who chose or were given a treatment with people who were not, and the differences in who got the treatment are what the earlier episodes in this series kept finding. Fisher's randomisation, in episode sixty-eight, was designed to make the groups alike on everything, including what nobody measured. The sealed envelope from the streptomycin trial, in episode seventy, kept the person enrolling patients from steering them, which is a different protection. A systematic review at the top addresses a third way of being fooled, the reader who finds only the studies that came out the way she hoped. Every step answers a named mistake, and the ranking was never meant as a verdict on persons or papers.

That reading also explains what the hierarchy cannot do, and episode seventy-two supplied the example. The Nurses' Health Study sits on a lower rung than the trial, and the trial turned out to be right about hormones. But the re-analysis that Miguel Hernán and colleagues published in 2008 rebuilt the trial's protocol from the cohort's own records, and the estimate moved a long way once the clock started where a trial's would. The cohort's mistake was a clock that started in the wrong place, a fault of execution, and the rung it sat on was not the cause. A ranking by design label tells you the average reliability of studies carrying that label. It cannot tell you whether this one, on your desk, was done well.

And the label can fail the other way. The satirical review of 2003 depends on the observation that some effects are so large and so fast that nobody needs a trial to believe in them, and no ranking exists for that property. Hierarchies are for the middle of the range, where treatments help a little and harm a little and the observation is noisy. In that range the movement was right, and the hormone reversal shows it. Outside it, the hierarchy needs a judge to say when it applies, and the judge is the clinical expertise the third part of the claim keeps.

## Chapter Four: The movement's own scorecard

By 2014, twenty-two years after the working group's paper, the movement had enough history to be audited, and the audit was done by people who wanted it to continue. Greenhalgh, Howick and Maskrey open by listing what had been achieved: the Cochrane Collaboration; methodological and publication standards for primary and secondary research; national and international systems for producing and updating clinical guidelines; courses in critical appraisal; and a research base on getting evidence into practice.

Two examples of success are given in the paper, and they are modest in the right way. The British Thoracic Society's asthma guidelines of 1990 were built from a combination of randomised trials and observational studies, and the authors report that after them personal care plans and stepwise prescribing of inhaled steroids increased, and illness and death from asthma fell. A second case is the guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence on preventing blood clots after surgery, whose uptake the authors say produced significant reductions in thromboembolic complications. They are the kind of result the movement promised, a recommendation derived from tested interventions, followed widely, and followed by fewer bad outcomes.

Then comes a sentence that turns the paper. Despite these successes, the authors write, many who support the idea in principle have argued that the movement is now in crisis. Kuhn's account of crisis, for listeners who remember it, is a field whose exemplars keep working on the puzzles they were built for while the anomalies pile up on the side. That description fits the paper's own list. It names five problems, which I will call the quality mark, volume, marginal gains, rules, and the patient with several illnesses. The first says the evidence-based label has been captured by vested interests. The second says there is more evidence, guidelines especially, than anyone can read. The third says statistically significant benefits may be small in practice. The fourth says inflexible rules and prompts steer care toward management and away from the patient. The fifth says guidelines map badly onto people with several diseases at once.

Episode sixty-six mentioned this list in a paragraph, to ask what Cochrane would have made of it. Here the list is the subject, so each of the five gets a worked example before they are sorted.

## Chapter Five: Five problems, from the inside

Take the quality mark first. The authors argue that drug and device companies increasingly set the research agenda: they define what counts as disease, they define predisease risk states such as low bone density treatable with alendronate, and they choose which comparisons are run and which outcomes count as efficacy, often a surrogate. Then they describe the methods by which a sponsor can produce an apparently unbiased paper. Trials are overpowered so that small differences become significant. Inclusion criteria select those most likely to respond. Doses of the intervention and of the comparison drug are manipulated. Surrogate endpoints stand in for outcomes patients feel. Positive studies are published selectively. As an illustration the authors report that studies of psychiatric drugs, each sponsored by its drug's maker, showed drug A beating drug B, which beat drug C, which in turn beat drug A. And they cite a review of industry-sponsored antidepressant trials in which thirty-seven of thirty-eight with positive findings were published, against fourteen of thirty-six with negative findings.

That is a loop, and it should be walked slowly. The movement taught a generation of clinicians to ask whether a study was randomised, blinded, and analysed as intended. A sponsor that wants a favourable finding learns to supply studies that pass those questions. The appraisal checklist, the authors write, may be unable to detect the increasingly subtle biases in industry-sponsored work. The skill the movement taught turned out to be the specification that the sponsors needed to meet.

The second problem is volume, which the authors call a measure of the movement's success. They cite a 2005 audit of one twenty-four-hour medical take in an acute hospital, in which the national guidelines relevant to the patients' immediate care ran to three thousand, six hundred and seventy-nine pages, which the authors estimate at a hundred and twenty-two hours of reading. One day's admissions came with about three working weeks of reading, and the movement that taught people to consult the evidence had produced more of it than a person can consult.

The third problem is marginal gains. The authors argue that the big improvements in many conditions were found early, and cite antiretroviral therapy for HIV and triple therapy for ulcers caused by Helicobacter pylori. What remains are questions such as whether two drugs should be given in series or in parallel. Large trials in a field like that, they write, typically overestimate benefit, because trial samples are unrepresentative and an overpowered trial can show effects that are statistically significant and clinically trivial, and underestimate harm, because adverse events are under-detected or under-reported. Their example is a seventy-four-year-old woman put on a high dose statin because her clinician applied a fragment of a guideline uncritically. She develops muscle pains that interfere with her hobbies and her exercise. The authors' phrase for what has happened is that the evidence-based tail wags the clinical dog, and their diagnosis is that attention has moved from the patient to the subgroup of women aged seventy to seventy-five, and from the patient's goals to the question of how to get everyone in a denominator onto the drug.

The fourth is rules. Decision support, templates and prompts built to carry evidence to the point of care can crowd out the individual patient's concerns. The authors give a diabetes check-up that follows a template, during which a serious symptom the patient mentions in passing may not be recorded. They note that inexperienced clinicians may use prompts mechanically and defensively, partly through fear of litigation, which stunts the judgement the prompts were meant to supplement. They quote Harrison and Checkland on the way the language of the movement spreads through practice: as bureaucratic rules become the accepted way of implementing the best evidence, the evidential requirement is, in effect, worn down in favour of an emphasis on rules. The example is the Quality and Outcomes Framework in British general practice, which pays practices points for recalling patients for structured checks. The authors concede that it has been associated with significant improvements in blood pressure control, especially in deprived populations. Its cost is a consultation interrupted by pop-up prompts, in which few patients are offered a conversation with a senior clinician before the recommended tests and treatments.

The fifth is the patient with several illnesses. As populations age, a person whose only condition maps onto one guideline is becoming rare. Even when trials enrolled people with multiple conditions, applying findings to a given combination is difficult, and following the guideline for each disease in turn can worsen another condition, typically through the drugs themselves. Anyone who has reviewed an older patient's medication list knows that each drug's recommendation was written for someone else.

What the authors ask for in return is concrete in places. Findings should come as numbers a patient can use: how many people must be treated for one to benefit, how many for one to be hurt, how many screened to find a single case. Journals should raise the bar on usability as they once raised it on statistics, when editors moved authors from P values to confidence intervals by requiring them, a change that the null ritual of episode sixty-nine survived. Independent funders should shape which evidence gets produced. The James Lind Alliance, they note, already brings patients, carers and clinicians together to rank research questions, so the people who live with a condition help decide which questions get a trial. And training should stop teaching with tidy vignettes in which the sick person is reduced to a handful of facts that fit a decision tree, and teach instead with real cases, intuition first and formal methods used to check it.

## Chapter Six: Sorting the complaints

Five complaints in a row can sound like a collapse. Two older sources help in weighing them. Both predate the 2014 paper by more than a decade, so the objections are old and the movement had time to answer them.

In 1998 Mark Tonelli of the University of Washington published "The philosophical limits of evidence-based medicine" in Academic Medicine. I hold only the abstract. It accepts that the benefits are enormous and well described, and then argues that there is an intrinsic gap between clinical research and clinical practice. Under the current understanding, he writes, the individuality of patients tends to be devalued, the focus shifts away from individuals toward populations, and the complex nature of clinical judgement is not fully appreciated. That is a complaint about the idea, and it is the stronger of the two older claims: the average effect in a trial population is evidence about a population, and moving from it to this patient is an inference that no study can make for the clinician.

In 2000 Sharon Straus and Finlay McAlister published a commentary in the Canadian Medical Association Journal on the common criticisms, and sorted them into three bins: limitations universal to the practice of medicine, limitations unique to evidence-based medicine, and misperceptions of it. I hold the abstract of this one as well. Applied to Greenhalgh's five, with the bins theirs and the assignments mine, it separates what belongs to the idea from what belongs to its handling.

Marginal gains belongs in the bin of limits universal to medicine. Every mature field finds the easy discoveries first, and a trial of a small effect will give a noisy estimate whoever runs it. The authors are right that the movement's tools do not tell a clinician how to treat a small statistical win, but no alternative would either. Multimorbidity is real and sits mostly in trial design: exclusion criteria produce populations unlike the people treated, and Tonelli's gap lives there. It is unique to the evidence-based method only in that the method asks the clinician to rely on those trials.

The rules problem is the mixed case. Sackett's editorial, as I recall it, disowned cookbook medicine, so the idea is not what the charge hits. The institutions are, and the charge is accurate there, and the cause is measurement. Once a guideline exists, someone wants to know whether it is being followed, and the percentage of patients on the recommended drug is easy to count. Greenhalgh's paper cites the American College of Cardiology's cholesterol guideline and a JAMA article that followed with advice on when to override it, whose authors wrote, in the paper's quotation, that the target for performance measures should be the proportion of eligible patients who take part in shared decision making about statins, and not the percentage who are prescribed them. That is the Goodhart pattern from episodes twenty-seven and twenty-eight. A number chosen to stand for good care is turned into the target, and the people measured respond to the number. Nobody at McMaster in 1992 intended compliance rates. They are what an organisation does to a skill when it needs something it can count.

Volume and the quality mark are the two that belong to the movement most closely, and they are the same problem seen twice. A brand that guarantees trustworthiness is valuable. Valuable brands get captured. A hospital with a guideline folder, a regulator with a standard, and a drug company with a trial portfolio all have reasons to be able to say that their work is evidence-based, and the cheapest way to say it is to produce documents that meet the checklist. The authors say the movement's quality tools may not detect subtle bias, which extends the worry from sponsors to the whole method of reading: if appraisal is done on the paper, then anyone who controls the paper controls the appraisal.

What the 1992 claim did, underneath the language of paradigms, was move epistemic authority. Before it, a clinician's authority came from a person: the teacher, the senior colleague, the eminent author of the textbook. After it, authority was meant to come from a document, a trial or a pooled analysis that any trained reader could check. Every earlier episode in this series, from Snow's pump to the streptomycin envelopes, was an instance of a document beating a person. But a document has an author, and the person who writes it can be an interested party. Moving authority from persons to documents does not remove the need to trust someone. It moves the trust from the bedside to the sponsor, the journal and the guideline committee, and the movement's skills were designed for a reader who could assume the documents were honestly made. What failed in the first twenty years was the assumption, and the reading skills were never wrong about the thing they were built to detect.

## Chapter Seven: Objections to the critics

The critics are owed the same scrutiny they asked others to accept, and Greenhalgh's paper meets four objections of its own.

The first is that its remedy sits at a level where it is hard to be wrong. It asks for real evidence-based medicine, defined as ethical care first, individualised evidence, expert judgement over mechanical rules, shared decisions and a strong relationship between clinician and patient. Nobody disputes any of those, and the paper itself says most of them are not new. A prescription that no one opposes needs a mechanism to be a programme. The paper has several concrete suggestions, among them that publishers should require findings to be presented in a usable form, such as numbers needed to treat, and that independent funders should shape the production of evidence. Those are testable. The paragraphs on judgement and relationships are not.

The second is the appeal to expertise. The paper cites the Dreyfus brothers' five stages of learning, in which the novice follows rules mechanically and the expert reasons rapidly and intuitively, and concludes that equating quality with strict adherence to guidelines overlooks the evidence on advanced expertise. That is a fair description of how good clinicians work. It is also what the 1992 paradigm was set up to replace: pattern recognition by respected seniors, defended by calling it expertise. The historical record the paper itself cites, the sixteen-fold variation in knee washouts, is what unexamined expert judgement looks like at scale. The honest reading is that both are true, that judgement is indispensable and that it needs auditing, and the paper is much stronger where it proposes training and tools to do the auditing than where it praises intuition.

The third concerns the marginal-gains argument, which says trials in saturated fields overestimate benefit. As a statement about published trials it may well be accurate, and the reliability of published findings is where next episode starts. As a criticism of the method it overshoots. A trial that is too large and finds a significant difference of trivial size is reporting something true, and a reader who asks about absolute risk and the size of the effect, which the movement's own appraisal guides teach, is not fooled. The statin patient in the example is the failure of a clinician who applied a fragment of a guideline without asking how large the benefit was for her. That failure is exactly what the appraisal skill is meant to prevent, and it argues for more of that teaching.

The fourth concerns counting. The paper never counts. It describes a crisis in terms of a quality mark, a pile of guidelines, and a set of tendencies, without a measure of how much worse care became, or in what share of patients, from any of the five problems. The successes it does report come with outcomes, falls in asthma illness and in clotting complications. The harms are given as illustrations. In an essay that calls itself a preliminary agenda that is allowed, but a movement that asks everyone else for the denominator should supply its own. Without one, nobody can say whether the average patient is better off with the five problems than she was before the working group's paper. The evidence in this episode, a sixteen-fold variation in an unproved operation and the reversal of a treatment used by millions, suggests she is, and I did not find a paper that measures it.

One reception note. The papers I found are insiders arguing with insiders: Tonelli, a physician at a medical school; Straus and McAlister, whose commentary sorts the objections from a position of sympathy; and Greenhalgh's group, who describe themselves as supporters. I did not find in the material I held a serious critic from outside medicine, and the philosophers of science who have written on the hierarchy of evidence are not represented here. The shape of the argument above is the shape of the dispute among those who agree the movement was needed.

## Chapter Eight: What a trialist does with this

For a person who writes analysis plans and reviews papers, the working use of the ranking is a two-column habit. Beside any study he is about to rely on, he writes which specific mistake its design protects against and which it leaves open. For a randomised trial the protected mistakes are confounding by who got the treatment and, with concealment, steering of enrolment. The open ones are selection of who entered, loss afterwards, the choice of outcome, and who paid. The PARACHUTE trial is a cautionary tale about the first open column. Randomisation protects the comparison among those randomised and says nothing about whether they are the people the question was about.

The cheapest place to see that is the participant flow diagram, which every trial reporting the CONSORT standard includes and few readers look at first. In the parachute paper it reads ninety-two screened and twenty-three randomised. Ask the same of any trial, in either direction. How many were screened, how many met the criteria, how many agreed, and are those who left more or less likely to benefit than those who stayed? When the answer is that a treatment already in routine use was tested only on people whose doctors were willing to withhold it, the trial is the PARACHUTE trial, with less comedy and a less obvious altitude.

Smith and Pell take five minutes, and the PARACHUTE paper's limitations paragraph is a better lesson in external validity than most textbook chapters. Greenhalgh's essay is free and long, an evening's reading. Sackett's two pages from 1996 are where I would start if I could have opened them, because the title says what the rest of the debate has been about.

I found no measurement of whether the five problems cost more than the practices the movement displaced. The sponsors, on the 2014 paper's account, learned the checklist at least as fast as the people who read their studies.
