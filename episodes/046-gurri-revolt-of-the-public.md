title: "Martin Gurri, The Revolt of the Public: what happens to authority once anyone can watch the institution work"
description: "A CIA analyst who spent twenty-nine years reading foreign newspapers for a living argues that the internet did not radicalize the public so much as let it see what elites had always been doing behind a monopoly on the story."

## Chapter One: An Anonymous Page for a Dead Man

In June of two thousand ten, a marketing executive at Google named Wael Ghonim, working out of Dubai and known to almost nobody in Egyptian public life, opened an anonymous Facebook page named after a young man he had never met. Khaled Said was a twenty-eight-year-old from Alexandria who had been dragged out of an internet café by two plainclothes police officers weeks earlier and beaten to death on the street, and a cellphone photograph of his disfigured face, taken by his own brother at the morgue, had already begun circulating past whatever the Egyptian state media planned to say about it. Ghonim built the page around that photograph and a name, "We Are All Khaled Said," and told almost no one that he was the person running it. By January of the following year it had passed three hundred fifty thousand followers, and an event Ghonim posted to it calling for a national day of protest drew ninety thousand people who clicked a button promising to show up in person.

Egyptian state security found him two days into the protests his own event page had called for and disappeared him into a cell for eleven days, the standard treatment for anyone the regime wanted permanently out of circulation. What the regime had not accounted for was that the disappearance of an anonymous page's anonymous author had itself become news the moment his family and colleagues started asking a television network to ask where he was. Ghonim came out of that cell and gave a live interview so raw that the anchor had to stop the broadcast partway through to let him compose himself, and the clip moved through the same networks the page itself had used to organize the original protest. Within two days the crowds in Cairo's Tahrir Square, already large, swelled past anything the government's own intelligence services had modeled. On the eleventh of February, eighteen days after the protests began, Hosni Mubarak, president of Egypt for thirty years, resigned and handed power to the military.

A government built to survive coup plots, foreign pressure, and a state of emergency it had kept in continuous force for three decades had no answer for a marketing executive's side project. The question this hour asks is not really about Egypt, though Egypt supplies its clearest single case: what changed, in the years just before two thousand eleven, that let an anonymous Facebook page do something armies and opposition parties had failed to do for a generation? A former intelligence analyst who had spent nearly three decades paid to read this kind of material professionally thought he had an answer, and he had been watching the pattern repeat in country after country from a chair very few people outside his own agency knew existed.

## Chapter Two: A Reader of Other People's Newspapers

Martin Gurri was born in Havana in nineteen forty-nine and left Cuba with his family as a child in the wave of departures that followed the revolution there. He spent twenty-nine years at the Central Intelligence Agency's Open Source Center, the unit whose entire job was reading foreign newspapers, watching foreign television broadcasts, and monitoring foreign radio and, later, foreign websites and social media, to tell Washington what the rest of the world's own information environment was actually saying about itself. He rose to director of research inside that unit, which meant his working life consisted of watching, professionally and at scale, the same kind of material Ghonim's page was made of, in dozens of countries at once, for the better part of three decades.

What Gurri noticed, sometime in the years after two thousand and one, was a change in the shape of the information itself rather than in any single story it carried. He built his explanation around an old etymological fact: the word authority descends from the Latin auctoritas, itself built on auctor, an author, the person whose account of events other people could be expected to trust. A government, a newspaper, a scientific body, he argued, holds authority for as long as it holds something close to a monopoly on the account of its own actions, the position of being the only author whose version of a story reaches the public at any real scale. Once that monopoly breaks, and a thousand other authors can publish an account of the same event at the same moment, authority does not merely weaken. It has lost the single condition it was built on, whether or not the institution holding it has done anything differently at all.

That claim is the whole argument in one sentence, and the rest of the book is Gurri testing it against a run of events that, when he first drafted the manuscript in two thousand fourteen, had not yet been given the name history would eventually settle on. He self-published the book that year rather than wait for a press to catch up with events still unfolding around him, and Stripe reissued it in twenty eighteen with a substantial new final section reckoning with two results the original edition had arrived just too early to cover: the British vote to leave the European Union and the election of Donald Trump. He kept writing after both editions were in print, later naming his own ongoing commentary The Fifth Wave, a title borrowed straight from the argument's own central term rather than a new one, as if to say the diagnosis, once made, did not need updating so much as continued application.

## Chapter Three: Five Revolutions in How Information Moves

Gurri frames the argument that got him there as a sequence of five waves, each one a change in the technology of communication large enough to rearrange who could speak with authority to how many people. Writing itself was the first wave, producing a scribal class in Bronze Age Mesopotamia and Egypt whose monopoly on the physical skill of making marks that meant something let temples and palaces run empires no one else could audit. The alphabet was the second, stripping writing down from thousands of pictographic signs to a few dozen letters and putting literacy within reach of merchants and citizens rather than only priests, one of the conditions that made a public debate culture like classical Athens's possible at all. The printing press was the third, turning a hand-copied manuscript into a pamphlet that could be run off by the thousand, cheaply enough that Martin Luther's ninety-five theses reached most of German-speaking Europe within weeks of being nailed to a church door, faster than any bishop could organize a response.

The fourth wave is the one whose end Gurri actually lived through, and it is the one his argument leans on hardest, because it produced the twentieth century's peculiar and, in hindsight, temporary arrangement: a small number of national newspapers, and then radio networks, and then three or four television channels, broadcasting one account of events outward to an audience with no equivalent channel to broadcast anything back. Gurri calls it an I-talk-you-listen structure, and its power to protect a favored account from a damaging one is easiest to see in a case with no digital technology anywhere near it. In April of nineteen sixty-one, President Kennedy authorized an invasion of Cuba that collapsed within three days, the prisoners captured, the plan an open embarrassment to the new administration that had approved it. Kennedy's approval rating afterward rose, not fell, reaching eighty-three percent within weeks, because the same handful of networks and papers that broke the story also carried Kennedy's own account of taking responsibility, and no rival account competed for the same audience's attention at anything like the same scale. A president could survive a fiasco that clear because the fourth wave gave him something closer to the only author's chair in the room.

## Chapter Four: A Preview at Abu Ghraib

The fifth wave is the one Gurri's title is actually about, the collapse of that arrangement under cheap digital publishing and networked social media, and its opening scene arrived a few years before Facebook or Twitter existed to carry Ghonim's page at all. In the spring of two thousand four, photographs taken on ordinary digital cameras by American soldiers at Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq, documenting abuse of detainees, reached news organizations and then the wider internet within days of being taken. Thirty years earlier, journalists covering the war in Vietnam had filed stories and film reels through a chain of editors, wire services, and network broadcast schedules that took days or weeks to move a story from a rice paddy to an American living room, time enough for official accounts to reach the same audience first or alongside it. The Abu Ghraib photographs skipped that entire chain. They existed as files that could be copied, emailed, and republished by anyone who had them, and no editor, general, or press secretary controlled the one channel wide enough to get ahead of them.

WikiLeaks turned that same mechanism into a publishing operation on purpose rather than by accident. In April of two thousand ten the organization released a video, recorded from the gun-camera of a helicopter over Baghdad during a strike carried out in July of oh-seven, showing a dozen or more people killed in an attack later shown to have included two Reuters journalists; it titled the video Collateral Murder and it moved across the internet at a speed no official transcript of the incident had ever moved at. The source of that video and of a much larger cache that followed, an Army intelligence analyst named Chelsea Manning, had been arrested by May; by autumn WikiLeaks had also published the Afghan and Iraq war logs, and in November of that year it released a quarter million diplomatic cables at once, an amount of formerly internal government correspondence larger than any newspaper's investigative staff could have produced through its own reporting in a decade. Washington's response, a formal diplomatic apology tour to allied governments named unflatteringly in the cables and a criminal prosecution of Manning that ended in a thirty-five-year sentence, treated the leak as a security failure to be contained rather than a story to be out-argued, an admission in itself that no version of events the State Department could offer was going to outrun the documents already loose online. None of it needed a nation's television infrastructure to reach an audience. A server was enough, aimed at readers already primed to read past whatever any single government spokesperson said next.

## Chapter Five: A Crowd That Never Physically Meets

Gurri's technical term for the actor on the other side of this collapse is not the people, a word he treats as carrying a false promise of unity and permanence a real crowd never earns. He calls it the public, a temporary, self-organizing swarm of people who share nothing but a point of reference and, once they have looked at it together, an opinion, and who dissolve again once the event that gathered them has passed. A public forms around a shared piece of information rather than a shared identity, which is why it can include people who agree on almost nothing else, and its most consistent behavior, in Gurri's account, is negation: it is extremely good at showing an institution's claim to be false, and it is under no obligation whatsoever to propose anything to replace what it has just discredited.

An American case from before the Arab Spring shows the same pattern on a much smaller and more mundane trigger. On the nineteenth of February in two thousand nine, a CNBC commentator named Rick Santelli, broadcasting live from the floor of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, delivered an unscripted rant against a federal mortgage relief plan, floating the idea of a Chicago Tea Party where traders would dump derivative securities into the harbor. The clip needed no newspaper's endorsement to travel; within ten days, organizers who had never worked together before held the first Tea Party rallies in Washington and Chicago, coordinated almost entirely online. Santelli held no elected office, led no party, and had proposed no actual policy beyond his own outrage, yet a movement organized itself around his ninety seconds of video faster than any single legacy institution could have convened a response. Two years and one ocean away, an Italian comedian named Beppe Grillo and a web strategist named Gianroberto Casaleggio launched a movement of their own in October of two thousand nine built on the identical premise, that citizens organized through a blog could serve as a watchdog on a professional political class assumed to be self-dealing by default. They called it the Five Star Movement, and within a decade it would be pulling more votes than either of Italy's old governing parties.

## Chapter Six: The Year the Asymmetry Broke

Two thousand eleven is the year Gurri's argument treats as the moment the asymmetry actually flipped rather than merely cracked, and the run of events inside that single calendar year does most of the persuading. On the seventeenth of December in two thousand ten, a Tunisian street vendor named Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire after a market inspector confiscated his cart, an act of protest against a specific, local humiliation that had nothing to do with any Facebook page. He died on the fourth of January, and the footage and photographs of the demonstrations his death triggered spread through Tunisia and then the wider Arabic-speaking internet fast enough that Ghonim's own Egyptian page began organizing around the same energy weeks later, one public's negation feeding directly into another's. By the time Mubarak resigned in February, protests using the identical toolkit, a shared grievance, a leaderless organizing page, a date set by consensus rather than by any party's central committee, had already spread to Libya, Yemen, Bahrain, and Syria.

Europe's own version arrived that May. On the fifteenth, in Madrid's Puerta del Sol square, roughly fifty thousand people gathered under a banner reading, in translation, that they were not commodities in the hands of politicians and bankers, and rather than disperse after the demonstration's scheduled end, several hundred stayed to sleep in the square. The occupation held for twenty-eight days, spread to more than sixty other Spanish cities, and drew, by some counts, over a million participants across the country before it wound down, all of it organized through the same kind of ad hoc digital coordination Ghonim's page and Santelli's clip had already demonstrated an ocean apart. Israel had its own tent encampments that July, begun after a young film editor named Daphni Leef, unable to find an apartment she could afford in Tel Aviv, posted a single message calling for others to pitch tents in protest; the camps spread to cities across the country within weeks. Adbusters, a Canadian magazine, issued its own open call that September for people to occupy Wall Street, and protesters camped in New York's Zuccotti Park through the autumn under a banner borrowed almost verbatim from Madrid's, that the system served the few at the expense of the many. Five countries, five unrelated grievances, and one identical organizing shape, arriving inside a single calendar year with no coordination between any of them beyond the fact that each had watched the one before it happen in something close to real time. Spain's own encampment did not simply disperse into nothing once the tents came down. Three years later, in January of twenty fourteen, a group of Madrid academics who had cut their teeth arguing inside those same assemblies launched a new political party, Podemos, built explicitly to carry the square's grievances into parliament rather than leave them to dissolve with the crowd; within a year it was polling competitively with Spain's two governing parties, a rare case of Gurri's negation actually producing an institution rather than only tearing one down.

## Chapter Seven: The Charge of Nihilism

What none of those movements did, and this is the half of Gurri's argument that reads as an accusation rather than a description, is produce a program. The Indignados dissolved their encampments without agreeing on a policy platform beyond opposition to what already existed; Occupy Wall Street never settled on a single demand its own general assemblies could ratify; the Tea Party elected candidates but organized around what government should stop doing rather than any shared account of what it should do instead. Gurri calls this negation without a positive alternative a kind of nihilism, careful to specify that he means it in something close to its original philosophical sense, a conviction that an existing order is comprehensively corrupt and cannot be reformed piece by piece, rather than the popular sense of pure destructiveness for its own sake. The public, in his account, holds elites to a standard of performance no government in history has ever actually met, and having proven, correctly, that elites fall short of that standard, it treats the proof as grounds for total rejection rather than as an argument for a specific, achievable fix.

The charge would be easier to accept without qualification if the failures the public was reacting to were imaginary, and Gurri does not pretend they were. He names the Iraq war's failure to find the weapons of mass destruction its own planners had promised as a real failure, not a perceived one; he names the two thousand eight financial crisis, in which the institutions charged with managing risk on behalf of the public turned out not to have been managing it at all, as a second. Elites, in his account, did not merely look untrustworthy through some new digital distortion. A meaningful share of what fed the revolt he describes was elites behaving exactly as badly as the public eventually decided they had, and the fifth wave's real function was not to manufacture that judgment but to make it impossible to keep contained inside a room the rest of the public could not see into.

## Chapter Eight: Two Referendums the First Edition Missed

Gurri's two thousand fourteen manuscript went to press before the two results that would end up supplying the clearest test of everything it predicted, and the twenty eighteen edition's new material walks straight into both. In June of twenty sixteen, British voters chose by a narrow margin to leave the European Union, against the recommendation of essentially every major party leader, most of the country's press, and the near-unanimous judgment of professional economists who had warned the choice would carry a real cost. Five months later American voters elected as president a candidate who had never held office, ran explicitly against the fitness of the people already running both parties, and treated hostility to the same television networks and newspapers Kennedy had once relied on as a campaign asset rather than a liability to be managed.

Gurri reads both results through a frame he calls center against border, in which a shrinking cluster of credentialed decision-makers, clustered in the same handful of capital cities and elite universities regardless of which country they belong to, faces an outer ring of citizens who no longer accept that cluster's account of its own competence. Brexit and Trump, in that reading, are not two unrelated national stories but the same negation the Arab Spring and the Indignados had already rehearsed, now returning a verdict decisive enough to change who actually governs, rather than only who gets to camp in a public square for a month. What the border coalition offered instead of the center's account, in both cases, remained closer to a rejection than a program, a criticism Gurri had already leveled at Cairo and Madrid five years earlier, applied now to two countries that had spent the intervening years assuming their own institutions were somehow more durable than Mubarak's had been.

## Chapter Nine: Three Objections

Scott Alexander's review of the book, published on his Astral Codex Ten newsletter, grants the diagnosis of two thousand eleven almost without argument. Leaderless, social-media-organized, largely middle-class protest movements really did erupt across a strikingly wide set of countries in the same narrow window, and Gurri's account of why explains the pattern better than any rival he considers. Alexander's harder objection is that the book explains the years right around its own publication so well that it has become difficult to falsify: subsequent politics did not resolve into the pure center-versus-border collapse the framework predicts, but instead split along familiar left-right lines, each side rebuilding its own trusted authorities, Fox News for one coalition, university-credentialed experts for the other, rather than abandoning trust in institutions altogether. That looks less like the public's terminal negation of all authority and more like authority itself re-forming along a new, tribal boundary, a possibility the original framework has less room for than its cleanest cases suggest.

Noah Smith's review raises a second, more historical objection. Spontaneous, leaderless popular uprisings did not begin in two thousand eleven; Europe saw wave after wave of them between seventeen eighty-nine and eighteen forty-eight, and the United States lived through a comparable run of them in the nineteen sixties and seventies, all without a Facebook page organizing any of it. If a nearly identical pattern shows up in eras with none of the fifth wave's technology anywhere in sight, digital communication may be one accelerant among several rather than the discovery Gurri presents it as, and Smith adds that the book undercounts what elites, and the public working through elite channels, have actually accomplished across the same decades: civil rights legislation, Medicare, and the policing reforms several American cities adopted after the Black Lives Matter protests Gurri's own framework would predict should have produced nothing but negation.

A third objection sits inside Gurri's own choice of the word nihilism, rather than in any external counter-example. Calling the public's reaction nihilistic, even hedged the way Gurri hedges it, frames what was in large part a correct diagnosis of real institutional failure, Iraq's missing weapons, a financial system that had not managed the risk it claimed to be managing, as a psychological disorder belonging to the public rather than a judgment the public had simply gotten right. An institution that failed and was caught failing is not the same event as a public that lost its mind, and treating them as one event risks handing elites the more flattering half of the very asymmetry the book claims to be describing, a trade in which they get called merely disappointing while the people who noticed get called nihilists.

## Chapter Ten: A Preview Nobody Wrote Yet

Neither edition of the book, published in two thousand fourteen and twenty eighteen, had reason to mention a pandemic, but the mechanism it describes reached its clearest public-health test two years after the second edition went to print. On the fifteenth of February, twenty twenty, the World Health Organization's director-general, Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, told a security conference in Munich that the agency was fighting something beyond the virus itself: we're not just fighting an epidemic, he said, we're fighting an infodemic. The word itself was not new, a columnist had coined it back in two thousand three during an earlier health scare, but the World Health Organization's decision to adopt it as an official term in its own COVID-19 situation reports marked the first time a major international health authority had conceded, in its own official language, that competing accounts of a single set of facts had become a problem the institution needed a name for.

The agency's own conduct that year supplied some of the material feeding the infodemic it was busy naming. In late February twenty twenty the United States Centers for Disease Control, echoing guidance the World Health Organization itself held at the time, told the public not to bother with masks; by early April both had reversed course and were recommending them, a six-week swing public-health officials later defended as a reasonable response to evolving evidence and a shortage of medical-grade supply, and a public already primed by a decade of cases like the ones this hour has just walked through read it instead as confirmation that the officials had not actually known what they were doing the first time. Whether the reversal was prudent caution or a real failure of the kind that fed the two thousand eight crisis, it reached a public that no longer needed the agency's own account to catch the contradiction, because the tweets announcing both positions were still sitting online, time-stamped, for anyone to set side by side.

What made an infodemic possible was the same monopoly-breaking mechanism Gurri had already traced through Cairo and Madrid, applied now to an authority that had spent the whole of the fourth wave era as close to unchallengeable as any institution gets: national health ministries and the doctors who spoke for them. A vaccination campaign run out of a district health office anywhere in the world used to compete with almost nothing for the story of what a given vaccine did and did not do; a parent skeptical of a shot had, at most, a neighbor's account or a newspaper clipping to weigh against the nurse's own word. That same parent now carries, on the same phone the district's own dashboard runs from, an unlimited supply of competing publishers, some careful, many not, none of them accountable to the local chain of supervision at all. A programme that spent a decade building trust the old way, through repeated visits and a health worker whose word had never yet been contradicted, discovers that the contradiction no longer needs to arrive locally. It can arrive from anywhere, aimed at no one district in particular, and land anyway. An earlier show on this shelf described institutions building what it called accountability sinks, structures engineered so that no single person inside them can ever quite be blamed for a bad outcome. Gurri's fifth wave does not widen those sinks. It builds a channel that bypasses them entirely, reaching a public that no longer needs the institution's own account to know something went wrong, or to decide, not always correctly, that it did.

## Chapter Eleven: Prescriptions, and What Happened to Them

The book's own answer to the crisis it diagnoses is comparatively modest given the scale of the diagnosis. Gurri asks elites to hold themselves to a standard of transparency good enough to survive an audience that will find the gap between claim and reality anyway, whether or not it is shown that gap voluntarily; he asks institutions to build channels that let a public participate in a decision rather than merely receive an announcement of one already made; and he asks the public itself to trade the standard of total institutional perfection it has been measuring elites against for a standard elites might conceivably meet, on the theory that expecting an achievable good is what keeps negation from calcifying into permanent rejection of anything a government could ever offer.

Writing a decade after the book's first publication, Gurri argued the crisis he had described was no longer producing pure negation in its newest phase, pointing to Argentina's Javier Milei and a second Trump administration's Department of Government Efficiency as cases where border politics had converted itself into an actual governing program aimed at shrinking the very institutions it distrusted, rather than merely refusing to engage with them. He read Europe the same way: France's National Rally and Germany's Alternative for Germany, both still formally shut out of governing coalitions by the very parties they now outpoll in parts of both countries, and a Romanian presidential election in which the first-round result was thrown out entirely rather than let a border candidate take office, as evidence that the center's own instinct, faced with a public it can no longer out-argue, is increasingly just to withhold the office rather than contest the vote.

Whether that counts as Gurri's own prescription finally being adopted, elites disciplined by a public no longer willing to fund what it cannot verify, or as negation simply finding a second life inside the machinery of government instead of outside it, is a question his own later writing does not fully settle, and it is close to the same open question this shelf's last six episodes have been circling from the funding side rather than the information side: whether an institution that has lost the public's presumption of competence can rebuild it by demonstration, the way Vannevar Bush embedded trust in a peer-review structure earlier on this shelf, or whether, once the monopoly on the story is gone, no amount of good behavior buys it back, because the public that would have to grant that trust no longer exists as a single audience capable of granting anything at all.

## Chapter Twelve: What a District Office Can and Cannot Control

A programme built to deliver something as trust-dependent as a vaccine, a therapeutic food, or a course of counselling to people who have never met the researchers designing it does not get to opt out of the fifth wave by staying small or staying local. It only gets to choose what its own account looks like on the day someone outside the programme decides to check it against a claim of their own, favorable or not, informed or not. Gurri's own diagnosis suggests the choice that survives contact with an audience it cannot control is not a more persuasive story, since a skeptical reader already assumes any persuasive story was dressed up for the visit, but a number a district officer would be willing to publish as it actually stands, on the theory that whatever gap exists between the claim and the record will surface on someone else's timeline if it does not surface on the programme's own.
