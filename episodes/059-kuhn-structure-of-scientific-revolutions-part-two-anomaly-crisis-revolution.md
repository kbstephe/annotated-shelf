## Chapter One: When Was Oxygen Discovered

Who discovered oxygen, and on what day? Kuhn takes the question up in the sixth chapter of his book and declines to answer it, and the refusal is the argument. Three people have a claim. The Swedish apothecary Scheele made a fairly pure sample first, but he did not publish until others had announced the gas, so Kuhn sets him aside. Joseph Priestley, a minister and a chemist, heated red oxide of mercury, collected what came off, and in seventeen seventy-four identified it as nitrous oxide, a gas he already knew. The next year, after further tests, he called it common air with less than its usual share of phlogiston, the period's name for whatever left a body when it burned. Lavoisier, working in Paris after Priestley's experiments and possibly on a hint from Priestley, reported early in seventeen seventy-five that the gas was air itself entire, only purer. By seventeen seventy-seven he held it to be a distinct species, one of the two main constituents of the atmosphere. Priestley never accepted that, to the end of a long life.

Now try to award the prize. If holding impure oxygen counts, everyone who ever bottled atmospheric air discovered it. If Priestley discovered it, the date cannot be seventeen seventy-four, when he thought he had nitrous oxide, and seventeen seventy-five gives him dephlogisticated air, a different gas in a different chemistry. Lavoisier's claim has the same trouble in quieter form. To the end of his life he insisted that oxygen was the carrier of acidity, and that the gas existed only when that principle combined with caloric, the supposed matter of heat. Neither idea left chemistry until well into the nineteenth century, and oxygen had become a standard substance long before. Kuhn's ruling is that the question assumes discovery is a single act, like seeing, when it is a process with two parts: finding out that something is there and finding out what it is. The two arrive together in an instant only when the categories are ready in advance, and then the thing found is not a new sort of thing.

The apparatus teaches more than the priority fight does. Both men tested the quality of a gas the standard way: two volumes of the gas mixed with one volume of nitric oxide, shaken over water, and the leftover gas measured. Atmospheric air had always left one volume. Anything worse left more. Their new gas left about one volume, and both filed it as a kind of air. Priestley found, much later and partly by accident, that with four times the nitric oxide almost nothing was left. Kuhn's gloss is that commitment to a test with a long record of success was a commitment to the nonexistence of gases that behaved as oxygen did. The instrument had a theory built into its proportions.

Lavoisier saw something in the same jar that Priestley could not, for a reason that predates the jar. He had been uneasy about phlogiston for years, and in late seventeen seventy-two he deposited a sealed note with the secretary of the French Academy saying that burning bodies took up part of the atmosphere. The oxygen work gave that unease a shape. It told him what he was already prepared to discover.

A puzzle follows. Normal science, as episode fifty-eight described it, is a game with settled rules and instruments built to confirm what the rules predict. If that is all it does, where do surprises come from? The odd answer is that they come out of the same machinery, and the instrument that hid oxygen from Priestley is the kind of instrument that makes trouble visible to Lavoisier.

## Chapter Two: The Screen That Glowed

Kuhn's second example is Roentgen, chosen because it looks nothing like oxygen. In November of eighteen ninety-five, Roentgen was in the middle of a routine investigation of cathode rays when he noticed that a screen coated with barium platinocyanide, sitting some distance from his shielded tube, glowed whenever the tube discharged. He spent seven hectic weeks, rarely leaving the laboratory, finding that whatever caused the glow traveled in straight lines, cast shadows, and could not be bent by a magnet. Kuhn will say only that X-rays emerged in Würzburg between the eighth of November and the twenty-eighth of December. At least one other investigator had seen the same glow and, to his later chagrin, found nothing. Crookes had noticed unaccountably fogged photographic plates.

What interests Kuhn is the reaction. No theory of the day forbade a new radiation, since light, infrared and ultraviolet were all accepted, and a new element filling a gap in the periodic table would have drawn congratulations and nothing more. X-rays drew shock, and Kelvin at first pronounced them a hoax. Kuhn's explanation is about equipment. By the eighteen nineties cathode ray tubes sat in laboratories all over Europe. If Roentgen's tube made these rays, many people had been making them for years without knowing, and the rays might have been contaminating work already published and filed as understood. Familiar apparatus would need lead shielding. Finished experiments would need redoing, because earlier scientists had failed to control a variable nobody knew existed. The discovery did more than add a field. It stripped certain instruments of their standing as the standard way of looking.

Kuhn then reaches for an experiment from psychology. In nineteen forty-nine, Bruner and Postman flashed playing cards at subjects for controlled short intervals, and some of the cards had been tampered with: a red six of spades, a black four of hearts. At the shortest exposures, subjects named the trick cards without hesitation and without any awareness of trouble, taking the odd card for an ordinary one. With longer exposures they began to hesitate and to say that something was off, that the spade had a red border. Then, sometimes suddenly, they got it, and after two or three trick cards the rest were easy. A few never did. At forty times the exposure needed for a normal card, more than a tenth of the anomalous cards were still misnamed, and some of those subjects were distressed. One said he was no longer sure what a spade looked like.

The same shape turns up in the sky. On at least seventeen occasions between sixteen ninety and seventeen eighty-one, astronomers, some of them Europe's best, recorded a star where Uranus then was. One looked on four successive nights in seventeen sixty-nine without registering that it moved. William Herschel, observing through a telescope he had built himself, saw a disc where a star should be a point, postponed identification, found that the object moved, and announced a comet. Only months later, when nobody could fit the motion to a cometary orbit, did Lexell suggest a planet. Or take nuclear chemistry. In nineteen thirty-nine, Hahn and Strassmann reported that their products from irradiated uranium behaved chemically like barium, and wrote that as chemists they should relabel the products but as nuclear chemists they could not bring themselves to make that leap, and perhaps strange accidents had deceived them. Kuhn notes that the reaction had been under study for almost five years, with chemical tests chosen for the heavy end of the periodic table.

This is not a gloomy picture of scientists. Anomaly is detectable only against a precise background of expectation, and the more exact the paradigm, the more sensitive an indicator it is. Kuhn adds that resistance, which looks like stubbornness, keeps a field from being distracted by every blip, so that the anomalies that break through have penetrated to the core. It is also why novelties so often appear in several laboratories at once. The tradition prepares the ground for its own undoing in many places.

If that is right, a prediction follows that can be checked against something other than Kuhn's own cases. Whatever overturns an established view should have been in view, loosely, before, and absorbed into old categories at every earlier sighting. I know a case that fits the prediction almost embarrassingly well, and Kuhn could not have known it, because it happened after he wrote.

## Chapter Three: Spirals in the Stomach, Seen Before

In nineteen eighty-two the cause of peptic ulcer was known. Barry Marshall, in his Nobel lecture of two thousand five, gave the received view in one sentence: ulcers came from excess acid, itself the product of personality, stress, smoking, or an inherited tendency. Five years earlier the first drugs that blocked acid secretion had arrived, cimetidine and then ranitidine, and nearly every ulcer healed on them. By Marshall's account the cimetidine maker was earning a billion dollars a year by nineteen eighty-three, and ranitidine went on to sell more than three billion a year through the decade. I am relying on his lecture for all of this, the telling of a man collecting a prize, and I have not checked its figures against the original Lancet papers. I will mark where the argument leans on his memory.

The anomaly was a spiral bacterium on the lining of the stomach, which a Perth pathologist, Robin Warren, had been noticing in biopsies. Marshall, a trainee in internal medicine, took over the clinical side of the study in August of nineteen eighty-one. Here the Roentgen pattern appears, because the lecture is full of earlier sightings. Bizzozero described spiral organisms in the stomach in eighteen ninety-two. In nineteen forty, Freedberg at Harvard saw them in forty percent of stomachs removed for ulcer or cancer. About ten years later the leading American gastroenterologist of the day, Palmer, did blind suction biopsies on more than a thousand patients, found none, and concluded that the bacteria were contaminants that appeared after death. The best-known sighting is a photograph. Susumu Ito, an anatomist in Boston, had swallowed a suction instrument to sample his own stomach, and his electron micrograph of Helicobacter pylori was reproduced in a handbook of physiology. He took the organism to be a harmless resident, because cats and dogs carry spiral bacteria without disease, and because nearly every Japanese adult of his generation was infected, so that infection looked like the normal state of the stomach.

That is the card experiment run on a century of pathologists. The bacterium was seen, filed as a commensal, and forgotten. Then comes the instrument problem, which Marshall tells with the faint rage of a man who still remembers it. He and his colleagues could see masses of bacteria on stained smears and could not grow them. For six months the cultures failed. The first that worked came from a patient biopsied on the Thursday before Easter in nineteen eighty-two. The hospital laboratory was swamped by a drug-resistant staphylococcus scare, and nobody looked at the plates on the Saturday, so they stayed in the incubator until Tuesday, five days. Small colonies had grown. Only then did the microbiologist confess that his staff had been handling the research biopsies like throat swabs, which are overgrown with mouth bacteria after two days and are therefore thrown away at forty-eight hours. This bacterium needed longer. It is Priestley's nitric oxide test with an incubator standing in for the shaken flask: a standard procedure with a long record of success, written for a world in which the thing sought does not exist.

The study that followed was a hundred consecutive endoscopy patients, a number Marshall chose because it made percentages easy in the days before spreadsheets. The pathologist read the slides without knowing the endoscopy findings, and the biopsies were taken from stomach wall away from any ulcer, so inflammation could not be blamed on proximity. When the table came back, fifty-eight of the hundred carried the bacterium. Of thirteen patients with duodenal ulcer, all thirteen did. Of twenty-two with gastric ulcer, eighteen did. The chance of the duodenal result arising by luck was about four in ten thousand.

Nobody was going to accept that, and the reason is in the table. Put the skeptics' case at its strongest, because it is the one a clinical researcher would make in the same room. Half of the patients with no ulcer also carried the bacterium. Run the arithmetic from Marshall's own counts: of fifty-eight infected patients, twenty-seven had an ulcer, about forty-seven percent, against four of forty-two uninfected, about ten. A risk ratio near five is large. It is also what one would see if ulcer-prone stomachs were simply good places for a harmless bacterium to live, which was the commensal reading that colleagues put to him for years. In nineteen eighty-four he tested the blood of healthy donors in the port of Fremantle and found that forty-three percent had antibodies, so the organism was common in the well. A cross-sectional table of this kind cannot separate cause from habitat. Only an intervention can, and in nineteen eighty-two nobody knew how to remove the bacterium.

So the anomaly existed, had been seen, and the field had every reason to look away. Why did anyone look? Kuhn's seventh and eighth chapters ask that question of every case he has.

## Chapter Four: When Failure Becomes a Counterinstance

Kuhn's claim in the seventh chapter is that no theory of consequence has been replaced without a prior period in which its own practitioners stopped being sure what it said. He calls that period crisis, and his three examples run from slow to slower.

Ptolemy's astronomy, he says, was admirable. For the stars it is still used as an engineering approximation, and for the planets its predictions matched Copernicus's. But it never quite conformed to the best observations, and reducing the discrepancies became the standing work of Ptolemy's successors. Each could be fixed by a small adjustment to the compounded circles. As time went on, a person looking at the net result of all that labor could see that the system's complexity was growing faster than its accuracy, and that a discrepancy corrected in one place showed up in another. In the thirteenth century Alfonso the Tenth said that if God had consulted him during creation he would have given better advice. Copernicus's preface complains that astronomers had assembled the heavens like an artist who draws hands and feet from different models, and ends with a monster rather than a man. Kuhn adds a point that deflates the heroic version: Copernicus's system was no more accurate than Ptolemy's and did not improve the calendar. People turned to it because the old system had failed and it was time to give a competitor a chance.

The chemistry case connects to chapter one. By the time Lavoisier began his experiments, the pneumatic chemists had produced so many gases with so many properties that there were almost as many versions of the phlogiston theory as there were chemists. A theory that must be revised for each laboratory has stopped being one theory. And the problem of weight, that metals gain weight when roasted, which phlogiston could only handle by giving it negative weight or some other adjustment, had sat at the edge of the field's attention for generations. Once the balance became a routine instrument and gases could be retained, it moved to the middle.

Here is the part of Kuhn's argument with the largest consequences for anyone whose job is reading data. Anomalies are not counterinstances. Scientists do not reject a theory that has the status of a paradigm because it meets anomalies, and they reject it only if an alternative is available. Kuhn then adds that there is no research without counterinstances. Every puzzle that normal science treats as a puzzle can, from another vantage, be seen as a failure of the theory, and what separates ordinary science from science in crisis is not the absence of such failures. It is how the field has decided to see them. Copernicus read as counterinstances what Ptolemy's other successors had read as puzzles. Lavoisier read as a counterinstance what Priestley took for a solved puzzle. Kuhn goes further than I would, saying that either no theory ever confronts a counterinstance or all of them do, at all times.

His evidence for patience is the moon. For about sixty years after Newton's original computation, the predicted motion of the moon's perigee was half the observed one, and the best mathematical physicists in Europe could not close the gap. There were occasional proposals to modify the inverse square law, and nobody took them seriously. In seventeen fifty Clairaut showed that the mathematics of the application had been wrong, and Newton's theory stood. Two other discrepancies, in the speed of sound and in the orbit of Mercury, sat for generations. Experiments on heat done for another purpose resolved the first, and a theory born of a crisis it had no part in creating resolved the second. If anomaly alone sufficed, Newtonian physics would have collapsed in the seventeen forties. If patience were always right, nothing would ever change. Kuhn says there is probably no fully general answer to what makes an anomaly worth concerted scrutiny, and offers three conditions from his cases. It may strike an explicit fundamental generalization. It may block an application of practical importance, as the calendar did for Copernicus. Or normal work may have turned an old vexation into a pressing problem, as the balance did to weight gain.

What does a field in crisis look like from inside? The rules of normal research blur. People try every articulation of the paradigm they can imagine, and Kuhn lists the symptoms: proliferating versions of the theory, willingness to try anything, open discontent, recourse to philosophy and to argument about fundamentals. Pauli, in the months before Heisenberg's paper on matrix mechanics, wrote to a friend that physics was terribly confused and that he wished he had been a movie comedian. Less than five months later he wrote that the new mechanics had given him hope and joy in life again. Kuhn notes that whoever finally builds the new frame has almost always been very young or very new to the field, and adds that the generalization is a cliché in need of systematic study.

Now put the ulcer case to this test, and the result is ambiguous. In nineteen eighty-two, ulcer medicine was not in crisis. It was in a boom. The acid-blocking drugs worked, the market was enormous, and nobody was writing prefaces about monsters. Marshall's name for the problem is an illusion of knowledge, a phrase he borrows from the historian Boorstin. It fits Kuhn's account of why earlier sightings were ignored: solutions anticipated outside a crisis are not taken up, as Aristarchus's sun-centered cosmos was not, and as the seventeenth-century theories of combustion by absorption from the air failed to get a hearing. Warren's bacteria were the same kind of thing, right, early, and unheard, because they made no contact with a recognized trouble spot.

But there was a trouble spot, a small one, and Marshall found it. The acid-blocking drugs did not cure ulcers. Marshall's lecture says they relapsed once the drug was stopped, which made cimetidine a treatment and not a cure. A trial published in nineteen eighty-one by Martin and Hollanders compared cimetidine with bismuth, an old remedy, and found that at two years about ninety percent of the cimetidine patients had relapsed against about sixty percent of the bismuth group. The authors could not say why. Marshall noticed that the curves had flattened by two years in both arms, took the thirty-point difference to be permanent cure, and wondered whether bismuth was acting as an antibiotic. That reading was his, and the data were already in the literature. In the historian Butterfield's phrase, which Kuhn borrows, he had picked up the other end of the stick. He tested the idea on a plate: paper discs soaked in bismuth, laid on a lawn of the bacterium, left from a Friday to a Tuesday. A clear ring surrounded each disc. Marshall says it was probably the most exciting day of his life, and I believe him, because the experiment was four days of waiting for an answer to a question he had assembled from other people's tables.

## Chapter Five: Two Words for One Solution

If two groups see different things in the same data, can an experiment decide between them? Kuhn's ninth and tenth chapters say not by itself, and the argument has layers that get harder to accept as they go down.

The first is political. Political revolutions aim to change institutions in ways the institutions prohibit, and in the interim society is not fully governed by them. The parties can appeal to no authority above the institutions at issue, so they resort to persuasion, and sometimes force. Kuhn says the choice between paradigms is a choice between incompatible modes of community life, so the arguments are necessarily circular, each side using its own standards to defend itself. Such a circle can persuade. It cannot compel anyone who refuses to step into it, and there is no standard higher than the assent of the relevant community.

The second layer is that the two sides disagree about what counts as a problem. Kuhn's example is Newton. The seventeenth century had banished explanations in terms of occult qualities, and Moliere's doctor, who explains opium's power to put people to sleep by its dormitive potency, had become a joke. Then Newton's gravity, an innate attraction between every pair of particles, looked like exactly that sort of quality, and for decades it was a scandal to those who still held the mechanical standard. Eventually the community accepted that attraction was simply a property of matter, a reversion, Kuhn says, to a scholastic standard, which is not the same as a retrogression. By the seventeen forties electricians could speak of the attractive virtue of the electric fluid without being laughed at. A change in what questions were permitted had changed what could be seen.

The third layer is that vocabulary shifts under the speakers, and Kuhn's best demonstration is chemical. In the eighteenth century, chemists treated salt dissolved in water as a compound, because the affinity that held salt together seemed to be the same force that held it in solution, and because the solution was homogeneous. Proust argued at the end of the century that all chemical reactions proceed in fixed proportions, and Berthollet that they do not. Each had good experimental evidence, and the two could not move each other, because where Berthollet saw a compound whose proportions varied, Proust saw a physical mixture. Experiment could not settle that, and neither could agreement on a definition, since the line between mixture and compound was part of how each man saw chemistry.

Then came Dalton, a meteorologist working on how water takes up gases, who treated dissolving as a physical process and assumed that atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios. That turned the law of fixed proportions from an occasional finding into a rule of definition: a reaction with varying proportions was, by definition, not purely chemical. Old data now read differently. The two oxides of carbon, which chemists had written as holding fifty-six and seventy-two percent oxygen by weight, were rewritten as one part of carbon to one point three or two point six parts of oxygen, and a simple doubling leaped to the eye. Kuhn then adds a detail I find unsettling. Proust's own measurements on the two oxides of copper gave a ratio of one point four seven to one, not two to one, and Proust was just the careful experimentalist who would have been expected to land on Dalton's number. It is hard to make nature fit a paradigm, Kuhn says, and after Dalton's theory was accepted, chemists spent almost another generation beating nature into line. The percentage composition of well-known compounds changed. Accepting the theory had revised the numbers.

I cannot tell whether this is deep or merely true of stoichiometry. But it is the version of incommensurability that Kuhn can support with documents, and I would sooner defend it than the tenth chapter's claim that scientists after a revolution work in a different world. That chapter's showpiece is Galileo's pendulum. Where Aristotelians saw a stone falling with difficulty, Galileo saw a body that nearly repeated its motion forever, a view the impetus theory of the fourteenth century had made available. Kuhn adds that Galileo reported the period to be independent of amplitude up to ninety degrees, far more regularity than anyone can find in a pendulum today. A regularity that one observer finds in the swing and another cannot find at all is better evidence than any lens metaphor. I would set one limit on my approval. Kuhn compares scientists to subjects wearing inverting goggles, whose visual field, after a period of confusion, flips over. That is a good picture of learning a field. It is a poor picture of two groups arguing, because both still have the old vision available and can choose what to attend to.

In the Perth case, the vocabulary did shift a little. The word ulcer carried a theory of the person: the ulcer personality, the stress. Marshall's later trial included a psychometric scale, and he reports that patients' scores improved when their infection was cleared, which suggested to him that the so-called ulcer personality was a mark of chronic infection. The same data, rearranged, turned a concept that had seemed to describe a kind of person into a symptom. That is a Dalton-sized reclassification, though I have only his word for the effect.

## Chapter Six: What Persuades a Field

Kuhn's twelfth chapter takes up the philosophers' question of how theories are tested, and his answer is that they are not, in the sense the philosophers meant. An ordinary researcher solving a puzzle is like a chess player trying alternative moves, who tries out moves and not the rules of the game. Paradigm testing begins only when persistent failure has brought on a crisis, and even then only once the crisis has thrown up a rival. The test is a competition between two paradigms for a community's allegiance, never a comparison of one paradigm with nature. Falsification occurs, he says, but not because an anomaly turns up. It is a second, separate process, which might as well be called verification, since it consists in one paradigm triumphing over another. That is a correction aimed at Popper, and the argument continues next episode.

What moves people? Kuhn gives a short, unembarrassed list. The commonest claim is that the new paradigm solves the problem that provoked the crisis: Copernicus on the length of the year, Newton on joining terrestrial and celestial mechanics, Lavoisier on weight. The claim is stronger if the new system is quantitatively better, as Kepler's tables were for Copernicanism. It is sometimes made falsely: for some years the wave theory of light handled polarization worse than its corpuscular rival. A third kind is the prediction of something nobody suspected. Poisson, an opponent of Fresnel, deduced from Fresnel's account of light that a circular disc should cast a shadow with a bright spot at its center, and when Fresnel showed the spot, French resistance collapsed. Last come aesthetic arguments about neatness and simplicity, which attract few, though the few may be the ones who develop the theory far enough for harder arguments to exist.

The deepest claim in the chapter comes next. A paradigm debate, Kuhn says, is not really about relative problem-solving ability, because the new candidate has usually solved little and imperfectly, and its opponents can point to problems it has not touched. The debate is about which paradigm should guide future research on problems neither has solved, so the decision rests on promise more than achievement. Whoever adopts a new paradigm early does so against the evidence of problem-solving, on faith, a word Kuhn uses without apology. Crisis matters because without it nobody gives up hard evidence for a will-o'-the-wisp.

Then he turns to the famous remark. Resistance can last a lifetime, and he takes it to be an index of something real: the assurance that the old paradigm will eventually solve all its problems is the same assurance that makes normal science possible. He quotes Darwin on the last pages of the Origin, saying he did not expect to convince experienced naturalists but looked to the young. He gives Planck's remark from the Scientific Autobiography, that a new truth wins not by convincing opponents but because they die and a generation grows up familiar with it. Kuhn rejects the usual moral. The holdouts are not cheating, neither proof nor error is at issue, and he finds no point at which resistance becomes unscientific, short of the case where someone goes on resisting after the whole profession has changed.

This claim has been tested, which is unusual among Kuhn's, and two results are at hand, both through secondary summaries that I have not checked against the papers. In nineteen seventy-eight, Hull, Tessner and Diamond published a study in Science of whether older British scientists resisted Darwin more than younger ones, using the written opinions of about seventy-eight of them from eighteen fifty-nine to eighteen sixty-nine, and as summarized found that age did not predict acceptance. In two thousand nineteen, Azoulay, Fons-Rosen and Graff Zivin published a study in the American Economic Review of what happens to a subfield when an eminent life scientist dies young. Papers by the dead scientist's collaborators decline. Papers by non-collaborators increase, drawing on a different literature and disproportionately often highly cited. The subfields grew by about seven percent. The authors read this as outsiders staying away while the leader lives, without anyone telling them to.

Together the two make Kuhn's remark half right. As a rule about individuals, that the old die and the young are persuaded, it has weak support, at least for Darwin. As a claim that fields are shaped around living leaders, whose departure lets in different ideas, it has stronger support, and it describes what a community does, which was Kuhn's real subject. It also concerns who gets through the door, a matter for a funding system more than for conversion.

How did the ulcer story end? Not with a funeral. Marshall had the one experiment that was available. By the middle of nineteen eighty-four he had tried to infect four piglets, which failed and went unpublished, as he says, like most failed experiments, and he had decided that the open question was Koch's third postulate: does a pure culture reproduce the disease in a healthy host? He took a baseline endoscopy from a senior colleague, an acid blocker two hours ahead, and a beaker about a quarter full of cloudy broth containing the bacteria. He skipped an ethics committee on purpose, reasoning that a refusal would have left him either doing it anyway and unable to publish, or not doing it at all. He drank it in one gulp. For a few days nothing happened. Then, on about the fifth to the eighth day, he woke nauseated, vomited clear fluid without acid, and had halitosis that his wife remarked on and his colleagues politely did not. On the tenth day an endoscopy showed spiral bacteria and a biopsy full of inflammatory cells. Four days later the bacteria had gone without antibiotics, a mystery he says remains one. The popular story is that his wife made him take antibiotics. In his account, he took tinidazole only after the day-fourteen endoscopy, when the infection had already cleared.

It is an extraordinary act and a weak study. One person, infected deliberately, developed gastritis and not an ulcer, and the bacterium went away by itself. What it answered was the objection that the organism merely lived in people who already had ulcers, because a healthy stomach was made sick. It did not touch the question of whether eradication cures ulcers, which was the question the field cared about. After the Lancet and Medical Journal of Australia papers, Marshall says, he held his breath and waited for sparks, and nothing happened. A Texan gastroenterologist who phoned him had called him a real cowboy.

The evidence that moved gastroenterology was dull and arrived in a different currency. Marshall designed it for his skeptics. It was a four-arm trial: cimetidine or bismuth, each with antibiotic or placebo, because bismuth blackens the stool and would have unblinded patients, so an arm of cimetidine plus antibiotic was needed to keep the blinding honest. He recruited smokers and severe cases deliberately, so that relapses would come early. He was not allowed to speak to patients, in case they mentioned side effects, and he hired his brother and sister-in-law to administer the personality scale. His funding came after an interview in which the panel opened by refusing to pay for the serology test as too far-fetched, and agreed to fund the trial for one year, at about fifty thousand Australian dollars, salary included, no computer. He had his hundred patients within four months, after screening about three hundred. In his words, curing ten patients with the old treatment required treating a hundred and four, against fourteen with the new.

Three more trials followed from Amsterdam, Houston and Vienna: Rauws and Tytgat's in nineteen ninety, Graham's a year later and Hentschel's two years after that. Marshall says Hentschel's was the most convincing to the extreme skeptics, because it used no bismuth and so could not be accused of broken blinding. The year after Hentschel's, in February, a consensus conference at the National Institutes of Health concluded that in all cases of peptic ulcer the essential first step was to identify and eradicate the bacterium. That is twelve years from the first culture and ten from the main Lancet paper.

## Chapter Seven: What Survives, and One Proposal

Hold the ulcer story against Kuhn's claims one at a time, and keep three things apart: what he says, what the case supports, and what I conclude.

On discovery as a process, with an anomaly, an instrument that hides it, and earlier sightings absorbed into old categories, Kuhn's account fits so well it reads like a script. The incubator that discarded plates at forty-eight hours is the nitric oxide test. For anyone who runs a laboratory, the sixth chapter's practical question is what the standard procedure was built to find, and whether anything lives outside it.

On the newcomer, the fit is good. A trainee on a rotation due to end in December of nineteen eighty-one stayed on because colleagues backed a prospective study. Kuhn's reason is that the newcomer is less committed to the old rules, and the Perth case does not contradict it.

On crisis as precondition, the fit is poor but instructive. The field was not in trouble, the ignored sightings of the previous ninety years fit Kuhn's prediction that anticipations outside a crisis go unheard, and the one real trouble spot was relapse, a small one that someone noticed in other people's data.

On incommensurability and the claim that no standard exists above the community, the case runs against Kuhn. The argument was won in the incumbents' currency: healing and relapse of duodenal ulcer at twelve months, in a blinded comparison against cimetidine. The skeptics never had to adopt a new world. They had to accept a result from a trial they might have designed, with an arm added to answer the flaw they would have named. Kuhn's circularity is real when the camps lack a shared test, and Proust and Berthollet had none. It looks much weaker in medicine, where randomized comparison on outcomes a patient can feel is a test both sides have already signed. Anyone trying to shift a field would do well to find the instrument the other side will accept as decisive, before looking for the one that is decisive to herself. That is my addition and not a claim from the book.

On conversion by generations, the case is mildly against, and the available tests of Planck's principle give it little support. I have no information on who the holdouts were. Twelve years is slow by the standard of a trial and fast beside Copernicus, whose theory made few converts for almost a century.

Two cautions go with all this. The ulcer case is one I chose because it fit the first half of the argument so well, and a case picked for fit illustrates and does not test. And the people on the other side had better reasons than Kuhn's chapter allows. Half of the uninfected stomachs in the study were fine, and so were nearly half of healthy blood donors, and an association of that kind is not a cause. The skeptics were applying a sound rule, that a common organism in the diseased is suspect until an intervention shows otherwise, and the rule was in the end satisfied. Compare episodes forty-nine and fifty, where the Imperial Japanese Army resisted the barley ration and the American South resisted Goldberger's diet for about twenty years each, with pride and a regional economy at stake. Kuhn's resistance has nothing in it but assurance. The ulcer story has one place where money was in the room, since the acid blockers were selling in the billions and Smith Kline and French, the maker of cimetidine, gave Marshall about twelve thousand Australian dollars in nineteen eighty-four, as Pfizer and Abbott did. Marshall's lecture ascribes the resistance to belief and not to commerce. I do not know how to weigh that and would not claim more than he does.

Part one of this series ended on a study section. Part two can end on something smaller. A program director who decides how a fellow's protected time is spent has two kinds of fellow in front of her: the one who is productive inside the field's puzzles, and the one who keeps returning to a result that will not go away. Marshall's rotation was due to end in December. What kept him in the laboratory was a protocol, a hundred patients, and a nurse who reminded the endoscopists to take two extra biopsies from stomach wall away from the ulcer. The question to carry into the next conference is what the standard procedure in your best assay was built to discard, and who in the building has been told, politely, that their result is subtle.
