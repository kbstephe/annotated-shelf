## Chapter One: A Hexagonal Room in Santiago

In nineteen seventy-two, in a building in downtown Santiago, Chile, workers finished construction of a hexagonal room, seventy-two square meters, containing seven white fiberglass chairs arranged in a circle. The chairs had buttons in the armrests that controlled screens on the walls. Seven chairs, deliberately an odd number, so that a disagreement among the people sitting in them could always be settled by a vote. The screens were fed by a network of telex machines connecting the room to factories across the country, because this room was intended to be something no government had ever possessed: a place where the managers of a national economy could watch it behave, in something close to real time, and respond while responding still mattered. The project was called Cybersyn. It was commissioned by Salvador Allende's socialist government and designed under the direction of a British management consultant named Stafford Beer – a large, bearded, cigar-smoking former steel executive who had come to believe that the sciences of communication and control could make organizations genuinely intelligent. The room never fully ran. On the eleventh of September, nineteen seventy-three, Pinochet's coup ended the government, Allende died in the presidential palace, and soldiers who found the operations room destroyed it. For fifty years the whole episode has been a curiosity – techno-utopian kitsch, a set from a film never made. The strangest choice in Dan Davies's book about British bureaucratic failure is that its entire second half is a rescue mission for the man who built that room. Why would a book that begins with shredded squirrels need a dead cybernetician? Part one ended with the claim that organizations are machines that make decisions no human makes. If you take that claim seriously, you need a science of deciding machines. Beer had one. This episode is what it says.

## Chapter Two: The Amount of World a Thing Can See

The foundation stone of that science is a law with an unglamorous name: Ashby's law of requisite variety. Variety, in this dialect, means the number of distinct states a thing can be in, or can recognize, or can respond to. And the law says: a controller can only control what it has the variety to match. A thermostat has a variety of two – too hot, too cold – and so it can regulate exactly one thing, temperature, and nothing else about your house. The law sounds abstract until you watch what it forces every organization to do all day, every day. The world has effectively infinite variety. An organization has a fixed, finite amount. So every organization is, before it is anything else, an engine for throwing away information – compressing the roaring particularity of reality into the small number of categories it can act on. Beer's term for that compression is attenuation, and once you have the word, part one of this series re-describes itself. The squirrels arrived at Schiphol with enormous variety: healthy animals, a solvable paperwork gap, a recipient in Athens, a warehouse that could hold them for a week. The system that received them had a variety of two: documented, undocumented. Everything about the situation that did not survive that compression – which was everything that mattered – was attenuated away, and the shredder was what remained. The Post Office helpline compressed hundreds of postmasters' reports, each one specific and alarmed, into a category called individual till discrepancy, and the pattern that any human reading fifty of them would have seen in an afternoon never existed at the level where decisions were made. Attenuation is not a failure. It is mandatory; Ashby's law gives no choice. The only real question about any organization – and Beer thought it was the deepest question of institutional design – is which information gets thrown away, and whether anything is watching what the discarding does.

## Chapter Three: The Purpose of a System Is What It Does

Beer compressed his second idea into a slogan, and the slogan has outlived everything else he wrote: the purpose of a system is what it does. Spelled as an acronym, POSIWID. It sounds like a truism and functions like a scalpel. The claim is a discipline of description: when a system reliably, repeatedly produces an outcome, treat that outcome as the system's purpose for analytical purposes – regardless of the mission statement, regardless of anyone's sincere intentions. A hospital billing office that reliably generates surprise bills is, for analysis, a surprise-bill generator. The prior-authorization apparatus from part one reliably produces abandoned appeals; POSIWID says stop calling that a side effect. The discipline matters because the alternative frame – the system is failing to achieve its true purpose, year after year, decade after decade – is unfalsifiable and consoling and leads precisely nowhere. If a school system produces credential sorting more reliably than learning, forty years running, at what point does failing to teach stop being an accident? A caution belongs next to the scalpel, though, because the slogan can cut badly. Taken as metaphysics rather than method, POSIWID slides into paranoia – every bad outcome becomes somebody's secret intention, and the analyst becomes a conspiracy theorist with a vocabulary. The sober use is diagnostic. It redirects the investigator's attention from what the system says to what the system's feedback loops actually reward, and there it earns its keep. This series' own dosage rule applies to acronyms too.

## Chapter Four: Five Systems, Spoken Slowly

Beer's centerpiece is a model of what any organization needs in order to stay alive, and it can be built in the ear, without a diagram, if it is built slowly. He called it the Viable System Model. Picture a hospital, since this listener will. System One is everything that does the actual work: the wards, the operating theatres, the clinics. Each unit runs itself; each has its own local management, its own rhythm. System Two is coordination – the scheduling office, the shared protocols, the bed managers – whose whole job is to stop the System One units from colliding with each other over the same nurses and the same beds. System Three is internal control: the chief operating officer's world, allocating resources across the units, auditing, squeezing synergies, holding the whole present-tense operation to account. Most organizations that call themselves well-managed are Systems One through Three and nothing else, and here is why that is fatal: everything so far faces inward and faces now. System Four faces outward and faces the future. It is the function that scans the environment – epidemiology, demographics, technologies, the strange new thing the clinic across town is doing – and asks what the organization must become. And System Five is identity: the level that holds the values, arbitrates when Three and Four fight – as they structurally must, since one wants efficiency now and the other wants investment in later – and answers the question what are we actually for. Now the model pays for the minutes it took, because it hands over a diagnostic vocabulary. An organization with weak System Four is efficient and doomed; it will be blindsided by a future it had no organ for perceiving. An organization with weak System Five is a sink factory: with no one holding the question of what it is for, POSIWID takes over by default, and what it does – whatever that has drifted into – becomes what it is for. Run the model over the Post Office and the diagnosis writes itself. Systems One through Three, muscular: prosecutions efficient, liabilities managed. System Four, blind by construction – the helpline that should have been its sensory organ was scripted to deny the pattern. System Five, worse than absent: an identity so captured by we do not make mistakes that hundreds of independent alarms could not compete with it. Beer's claim, and Davies's, is that this is not a metaphor for what went wrong. It is the anatomy of it. And Beer built one more organ into the model, which lands differently once you have heard the Post Office story. He required every viable system to carry what he called an algedonic channel – from the Greek words for pain and pleasure – an alarm wire that runs from the working level straight to the top, bypassing every intervening layer, and that fires when something crosses a threshold no routine report would surface in time. The layers in between exist to attenuate; that is their function; and so, Beer reasoned, a signal that must not be attenuated needs a wire that never touches them. Cybersyn's design had these alarms wired into the factory network. Hold the idea of a pain signal that management cannot filter, because part three will meet an industry that actually built one.

## Chapter Five: Artificial Intelligences, Running on People

Which brings the book to its most startling move, the one that updates a nineteen-seventies science for this decade. Beer insisted that organizations are not like decision-making entities; they are decision-making entities. Davies restates it in the vocabulary that has colonized the last ten years: a corporation is an artificial intelligence. It runs on people the way software runs on silicon – no individual component understands or intends the output, and the output is nonetheless computed, reliably, by the whole. The corporation optimizes an objective function; it perceives through attenuated channels; it acts through procedures; and – the step where the analogy stops being cute – its goals are not the goals of any human inside it. Every employee of the insurer from part one might privately wish the prior-authorization process were kinder, and the process grinds on, because the machine's objective is not the average of its components' wishes. We spent the twenty-tens holding conferences about the alignment problem – the fear that a powerful artificial optimizer might pursue its given objective in ways that destroy what its creators actually value. Davies's observation is that this is not a hypothetical about the future. We have lived among misaligned optimizers for a century and a half. They are called limited-liability companies and government ministries, and the accountability sink is simply what misalignment looks like from the inside: the mechanism by which the machine insulates its objective from the humans who might, if any feedback reached them, want to change it. The AI-safety literature even has a name for a system that resists having its goals corrected – it calls the missing property corrigibility, and a company that cannot be steered by the evidence of its own harms is failing it in exactly the technical sense. The squirrel question from part one – if no one decided, what decided? – now has its answer stated plainly. The machine decided. The unsettling part is not that the answer is exotic. It is that once heard, it stops sounding exotic at all.

## Chapter Six: The Objective Function Somebody Chose

But a machine optimizes whatever it is built to optimize, which forces one more question, and it is the question that gives Davies his villain. Who chose the objective function? Here the book names names, and the name is economics. In nineteen seventy, Milton Friedman published the essay that became the charter of the shareholder-value era: the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits. Davies reads that doctrine cybernetically, and the reading is colder than the usual moral complaint about greed. Handing a corporation the single objective maximize shareholder returns is an act of radical attenuation – it compresses every signal the machine might attend to, from worker safety to product quality to the long-term survival of its own suppliers, into one number, and then wires the whole control system to that number. In Beer's anatomy, it amputates System Five and solders a metric where identity used to be. A machine so configured does not need wicked executives to behave wickedly; it needs only to run. Davies pairs the doctrine with a second economic export, Goodhart's law – when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure – and notes the combination: modern institutions were instructed to govern themselves by targets, by a profession whose own law says targets rot. The rot is not hypothetical; it has a case file. When England's emergency departments were set a four-hour maximum wait, hospitals discovered patients could be held in ambulances outside the door, where the clock had not yet started, or admitted to a ward at hour three fifty-nine whether admission served them or not. The target was met; the thing the target stood for was not; and the information the measure once carried – how long sick people actually wait – was destroyed by the act of managing to it. A machine wired to a metric does not merely pursue the metric. It consumes it. Now, this is the point in the book where a listener should feel the ground tilt, because the argument is at its most satisfying here, and most-satisfying is exactly when this series applies the brakes. Ed Smith, reviewing the book in the New Statesman, pushed back on precisely this chapter: Davies, he argued, gives economics too much of the blame – the hollowing-out of responsibility has sources older and deeper than Friedman, and a discipline is a convenient defendant because it cannot take the stand. I think the objection lands about halfway. Davies does not claim economists invented the machine; he claims they wrote the objective function that the machine's most powerful instances now run – and for corporations since nineteen eighty, that claim seems to me simply true, and Smith does not really contest it. Where Smith wins is scope: the squirrels died, and the Post Office prosecuted, inside institutions where shareholder value explains nothing. The sink predates the doctrine. Friedman supplied one master objective; the tendency of large systems to shed responsibility needed no instruction. Keep both halves – the machine is old, its most aggressive current programming is recent – and you hold the book's argument at its honest strength, which is where an argument should be held before you decide how much of it to keep.

## Chapter Seven: The Man Who Was Refused

Return, before closing, to Santiago, because the biography now reads differently than it did in chapter one. Beer was not a crank at the margins. He ran operations research for one of Britain's largest steel companies, commanded enormous consulting fees, wrote books that were reviewed and read. In the mid nineteen-seventies, at the height of his earning power, he gave most of it up – resigned the directorships, shed the possessions, and moved to a stone cottage in rural Wales with no telephone, where he wrote, painted, and taught for the rest of his life; whatever else can be said of him, he was not optimizing shareholder value. And across the nineteen seventies and eighties, his science died anyway – not refuted, Davies emphasizes, but defunded, unfashionable, orphaned between departments; too mathematical for the management schools, too managerial for the mathematicians, and, after the coup, politically radioactive by association. The discipline that asked how should an organization be wired so that it can perceive and correct itself lost the century to the discipline that asked how do rational agents maximize – and part of Davies's project is the claim that this was a wrong turn taken by Western thought as a whole, with the squirrels and the postmasters among its eventual invoices. It is a grand claim, and its grandness is worth noticing, because a book that began as a sharp essay about phone trees is now asserting that a forgotten Welshman's consultant had the correct theory of institutions and civilization declined to hear it. Ed Smith, in the same review, caught this too, from the other side: having introduced Beer, he complained, Davies does not actually give us much of him as a solution – the exhumation is loving, the reanimation thin. Whether that is a flaw in the book or an honest report on the state of the science is the question part three exists to answer. Because everything so far has been diagnosis, and diagnosis was the easy half. The machine decides; the machine is misaligned; the machine's own structure routes the evidence of its failures away from anyone who could act. The question a clinician asks next is the only one left. What is the treatment – and who, exactly, would administer it? Next time, Part Three: repairing the feedback. Thanks for listening.
