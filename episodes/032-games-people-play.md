## Chapter One: The Dance Lessons She Never Took

A married woman comes to see a psychiatrist because her husband has spent years controlling what she can and cannot do. He decides where they go, who they see, how she spends her afternoons. She has wanted, for a long time, to learn to dance, and he will not permit it. Treatment proceeds. Under a psychiatrist's steady attention the husband, over months, loosens his grip, stops objecting to outings, stops vetoing her plans, and the wife, freed at last of the restriction she has been naming as the whole problem of her marriage, signs up for dance classes. She discovers, the first time she has to walk onto a dance floor in front of other people, that she cannot do it. Not won't. Cannot. A genuine, crippling phobia, one that had apparently been there the entire time, sitting underneath a marriage she had spent years describing entirely in terms of what her husband refused to let her do.

Read that case the way most people would read it and the husband looks like the villain who finally got exposed, a controlling man whose wife's world opened the moment he stepped back. Eric Berne, the psychiatrist who published this exact case in nineteen sixty-four, read it the other way around. The husband's restriction had been doing his wife an enormous, unacknowledged favor. As long as he refused to let her dance, she never had to find out that she could not bear to. She got to spend years complaining, sincerely, about a man stopping her from doing something she would have been unable to do regardless, and the complaint itself, not the dancing, was the actual point of the arrangement: a stable, renewable grievance that let her feel thwarted rather than afraid. Berne named this pattern If It Weren't For You, gave it that title in a thesaurus he was compiling of dozens more like it, and called the whole category of behavior a game, a word he meant with none of the lightness the word usually carries. A Berne game is not fun for the people playing it. It has rules, roles, and a predictable ending, run over and over by two people who both, at some level neither will admit to the other, need the ending to keep happening.

## Chapter Two: The Man Who Failed His Own Analysis

Eric Berne was born Eric Lennard Bernstein in Montreal in nineteen ten, the son of a doctor who died of tuberculosis when Berne was a boy and a mother who worked as a professional writer and editor. He took his medical degree at McGill, trained in psychiatry at Yale, became an American citizen at the end of the nineteen thirties, and somewhere around then anglicized his name to the one the book would make famous. His actual training was orthodox almost to a fault: analysis under Paul Federn, a direct disciple of Freud's, at the New York institute in the early nineteen forties, then, after the war, a second training analysis under Erik Erikson himself at the San Francisco Psychoanalytic Institute. By any reasonable measure, Berne spent the better part of fifteen years becoming the very psychoanalyst the profession was built to produce.

In nineteen fifty-six, after all of that training, he applied to that same institute for full membership, and the credentialing committee turned him down. The record is a shade more careful than the story that gets told about it: the committee did not close the door outright, so much as tell him he wasn't ready and could reapply after three or four more years of additional personal analysis, a deferral rather than a flat rejection. Berne, by every account of what happened next, heard it as a rejection anyway, and history has mostly agreed with him rather than with the committee's own paperwork. Before that year was out he had published his first papers on what he was calling ego states, and within two years he had a name for the whole approach: transactional analysis, built explicitly apart from the psychoanalytic establishment that had just told him to go back and do more years of the thing he had already spent fifteen doing. The timing is hard to read as coincidence. A man who had just been told his understanding of the human mind wasn't yet credentialed enough to practice under Freud's banner went and built an entirely separate banner, in under two years, and never looked back toward the institute that had made him wait.

## Chapter Three: Three People Living in One Skull

The framework Berne built starts from a deliberately different kind of division than Freud's. Freud's id, ego, and superego are theoretical structures, forces inferred from behavior but never directly observed doing anything. Berne's three states, capitalized because he means them as something closer to distinct, recognizable modes a person actually and visibly occupies, are Parent, Adult, and Child. The Parent is a recording, borrowed wholesale from the actual parents and parental figures a person grew up watching, replayed as attitudes and behaviors that were never personally reasoned through, just copied. The Adult is the part of a person actually processing the present moment, weighing evidence, deciding what to do next based on what is currently true rather than what was once true or what should always be true. The Child is older than either, in one sense: it is the set of feelings and behaviors a person laid down in actual childhood and never fully retired, joy and tantrum and helpless dependence alike, available to resurface at forty the same way they operated at four.

None of the three attaches to age. A sixty-year-old snapping at a subordinate with a parent's disappointed tone is operating from Parent. A six-year-old carefully weighing whether to tell a parent about a broken vase, gathering evidence before deciding, is operating from Adult. The framework's use is not classification for its own sake. It is diagnostic: watch which of the three states is actually driving a given exchange, and you can often predict, before either party consciously decides anything, where the exchange is headed.

## Chapter Four: The Vitamin Called Attention

Berne's second building block starts from an observation borrowed from outside psychiatry entirely, from research on infants who were fed and kept warm and medically monitored and who nonetheless, deprived of being touched and handled, sometimes failed to thrive and occasionally died. From that evidence Berne argued that human beings carry a genuine biological hunger for physical and social contact, as real in its way as hunger for food, and that as an infant grows into a talking, socialized adult, that hunger for touch gets sublimated into a hunger for recognition: a nod, a greeting, a compliment, an argument, anything that registers one person's existence to another. He called the smallest unit of that recognition a stroke, and his shorthand for the whole theory, deliberately built to be as sticky as the fable it echoes, was that a person needs a stroke a day roughly the way a body needs a vitamin.

The harder claim, and the one that ends up doing real work later in the book, is what Berne found people willing to trade for a stroke when a warm one wasn't on offer. A cold stroke, an insult, a public correction, an argument that ends in humiliation, still registers as recognition, and Berne's contention is that most people, most of the time, will take a negative stroke over none at all rather than risk the total silence of being unseen. That preference, a bad stroke over no stroke, is the hinge the whole games catalogue swings on: if getting hurt on a schedule beats getting ignored, then an arrangement that reliably produces a hurt feeling, on cue, twice a week, forever, stops looking irrational and starts looking like a very efficient way to make sure the vitamin keeps arriving.

## Chapter Five: Six Ways to Spend an Afternoon

Berne sorts everything two or more people can do with shared time into six categories, arranged, not coincidentally, in order of rising stroke intensity and rising risk. Withdrawal is contact declined outright, a person alone with their own thoughts, drawing on no strokes but the ones memory and imagination can supply internally. Rituals are the safest possible exchange with another person, a greeting, a holiday custom, an exchange so scripted that both parties know exactly what is coming and exactly how much recognition it is worth, cheap and reliable, like small change. Pastimes are looser, semi-scripted conversations organized around a shared, low-stakes topic, the kind of talk that fills a waiting room or a cocktail hour, useful mainly for sizing up whether a stranger might be worth a closer, riskier kind of time later. Activities are what happens when two or more people turn toward a shared task in the world, building something, solving something, and draw their recognition from the work itself rather than from each other directly.

Then come the two categories that cost the most and pay the most: games, which manufacture a strong, reliable, if painful feeling through an entirely scripted exchange, and intimacy, the only mode on the list with no script at all, two people risking an honest exchange of feeling with no guaranteed outcome and no fallback position if it goes badly. Berne's order is also, not by accident, an order of increasing courage required. A ritual asks nothing of anyone. Intimacy asks everything, and that gap in nerve is why, on Berne's account, so much of adult social life gets rerouted into the second-to-last category instead of the last one.

## Chapter Six: A Machine With Four Moving Parts

Berne's own definition of a game, stripped of its clinical hedging, runs close to this: an ongoing exchange between two people that looks, on its surface, like one kind of transaction, while actually running a second, hidden transaction underneath, aimed at a predictable and specific outcome neither player will admit to wanting in advance. Underneath that definition sits a repeatable shape. One player opens with a move that looks innocent, a request, a complaint, an invitation, engineered without either party quite admitting it to exploit something in the other player, some unmet need or vulnerability the opener has correctly sized up. The second player takes the bait, and the two run through a series of exchanges that look, to anyone watching, like an ordinary conversation. Then comes a switch: one player abruptly shifts position, and a beat of confusion follows as both register that something other than the surface conversation was going on the whole time. Last is the outcome itself: a specific bad feeling that both parties end up holding, and Berne's argument is that this final feeling was never an accident. It was the entire reason either player sat down for the conversation.

The game Berne says he identified first, the one that gave him the whole idea, is called Why Don't You, Yes But. Someone presents a problem to a group, a stalled decision, a difficult situation, something that sounds like a genuine request for help. The group obliges, offering solution after solution, and every one gets turned down the same way: yes, but I already tried that; yes, but that wouldn't work either. The exchange can run for a surprisingly long time, suggestion after suggestion shot down, until the group runs out of ideas and falls silent, at which point someone offers a consoling remark and the round ends. Read as a request for help, the whole exchange is baffling, since every offered solution gets refused. Read as a game, it makes complete sense: the person presenting the problem was never actually looking for a solution. She was looking to demonstrate, to a room full of apparently capable people, that her problem could not be solved, and every rejected suggestion was one more piece of evidence for a conclusion she had already reached before she opened her mouth.

## Chapter Seven: The Son of a Bitch and the Man Wearing the Sign

Two of Berne's games make a matched pair worth holding side by side, because they show the same mechanism running from opposite ends of a relationship. Now I've Got You, You Son of a Bitch is a game one player runs by conducting what looks like ordinary business, a transaction, a favor, a shared project, all the while watching for the other party to make some small, almost inevitable mistake, an overcharge, a missed detail, a broken promise. The moment it happens, the watching player erupts, and the eruption feels, to everyone involved including the person having it, entirely justified. That justification is what the entire exchange was built to produce. A grievance earned fair and square lets a person unload months of stored anger with a clean conscience, and Berne's point is that the anger was never really about the overcharge. The overcharge was simply the first legitimate excuse to arrive.

Kick Me runs the identical exchange from the other chair. A person behaves, without quite meaning to, in ways that invite rejection or mistreatment, and when the world is slow to oblige, escalates the behavior until someone finally does. When the blow lands, the player reacts with real, apparently genuine bewilderment: why does this always happen to me. But the bewilderment sits on top of a payoff just as real as the other player's righteous anger, a confirmation of a belief about oneself that predates the specific incident by years, the belief that one is simply the kind of person life treats this way. Put the two games next to each other and the shared structure gets hard to miss: one player needs a legitimate reason to be angry, the other needs an equally legitimate confirmation of being wronged, and between the two of them, in a marriage or a friendship or an office, they can supply each other's need indefinitely, each one certain the other one started it.

## Chapter Eight: The Position You Decided On Before You Could Talk

Every game, Berne argues, is in service of something set earlier and held more stubbornly than either player realizes: one of four life positions a person settles into, according to Berne, in early childhood, long before there is vocabulary to argue about it. The rare, healthy baseline holds both sides of a relationship in equal standing, self and other each fundamentally fine, and it is the only one of the four from which real intimacy becomes possible, since neither party needs the other to be wrong. Tip the balance one way and the persecutors result, secure in their own standing and endlessly on watch for a target who deserves the blame. Tip it the other way and out come the victims, sure only of their own inadequacy and endlessly available to be wronged by whoever they have already decided holds the upper hand. And when a person loses faith in both sides of the ledger at once, certain that neither they nor anyone around them is fundamentally fine, the result sits closer to despair than to any game at all.

Here is the claim the whole book has been circling since the dance-floor case in the first chapter, and it is worth stating as plainly as Berne eventually does: the bad feeling at the end of a game is not a cost the players are stuck paying for something else they actually wanted. It is the product. A person who settled, at five or six years old, into the belief that she was fundamentally unworthy of love does not spend the rest of her life trying to disprove that belief. She spends it, often without a shred of conscious intent, arranging situations that will confirm it on schedule, because a confirmed, familiar, expected bad feeling is more bearable than an unfamiliar good one that the earlier decision has no way to file. Berne calls the substitute feeling itself, the specific flavor of misery or righteousness or self-pity a person's games are built to manufacture, a racket, on the theory that somewhere in early childhood the real feeling underneath it, often something as simple as anger or fear, got punished or ignored until the child learned to produce an approved substitute instead. Games are not a failure to get what a person wants. They are a highly efficient, entirely unconscious machine for getting what an earlier version of that person decided, once, was the most that could be expected.

## Chapter Nine: The Story Written Before You Could Read

A racket explains why a single game keeps recurring. It does not explain why the games a given person favors, If It Weren't For You in one relationship, Kick Me in the next, tend to belong to a single coherent plot rather than to an unrelated bag of habits. Berne spent his last years building out exactly that larger claim, in the book he was still correcting in proof when he died: What Do You Say After You Say Hello, a title poking fun at how little social theory ever has to offer past a first greeting. His argument there is that a person does not simply collect favorite games across a lifetime the way a hobbyist collects stamps. Sometime in early childhood, often before there is language to reason about it, a person settles on something closer to a plot: a life script, an unconscious story about how things are generally going to go, complete with a recurring cast and, in Berne's more unsettling phrase, an ending already decided.

A script does not run on its own; it needs games the way a plot needs scenes, each one a small, self-contained episode that pushes the story toward the ending the script already assumes. A person whose script casts her as someone doomed to be let down does not have to go looking for disappointing partners. Playing enough rounds of If It Weren't For You across a few different relationships supplies the disappointment on schedule, each round one more scene confirming a plot some much younger version of her decided on, with none of the standing to argue against it. Berne's aim in this harder, later book was to help a patient read her own script back to herself clearly enough to see the familiar ending coming before she walked into it one more time, a far more ambitious task than naming a single game in a single afternoon, and one his own death left considerably less finished than the games catalogue that had already made him famous.

## Chapter Ten: Five Roles for One Bottle

Nowhere does Berne build a fuller cast than in the game he calls simply Alcoholic, and his opening move in that chapter is worth keeping in mind before the roles arrive: there is no such thing, in his analysis, as an alcoholic as a fixed type of person. There is a role called the Alcoholic, available to be played inside a particular game, and the game needs a full supporting cast to run at all. The player at the center takes the Alcoholic's role. A Persecutor, typically a spouse, supplies the anger and the accusations. A Rescuer, often a doctor, invests real professional pride in fixing the problem, and Berne's own dry aside about this role, a physician and patient congratulating each other after six sober months only for the patient to turn up in the gutter the following day, is close to the book's bleakest joke. A Patsy, often a parent, supplies unconditional sympathy and practical help with no strings and no confrontation attached. And a Connection, the supplier, the bartender who understands the particular kind of talk an active drinker needs to hear, rounds out a fifth role Berne calls, without much exaggeration, the most meaningful relationship in an addict's life.

What makes this game more than a catalogue of five job descriptions is what it does to the actual substance at its center. The drinking itself, on Berne's reading, is almost beside the point. The real payoff is the cycle, transgression, persecution, rescue, and relapse, running again and again, because it lets every single person in the cast confirm something about themselves they arrived already believing: the Persecutor gets to be righteously furious, the Rescuer gets to be needed, the Patsy gets to be endlessly forgiving, the Connection gets to be indispensable, and the Alcoholic, the player everyone assumes is the loser in the arrangement, gets what Berne argues is actually the deepest payoff of all, a self-directed misery that confirms whatever the earliest, most stubborn belief about himself already was.

## Chapter Eleven: A Triangle With No Fixed Corners

Four years after Berne published this five-role cast, a psychiatrist working in the same San Francisco circles, Stephen Karpman, noticed that three of the five roles, Persecutor, Rescuer, and the player Berne calls the Victim, kept reappearing across every game in the thesaurus, not just Alcoholic, and that the three of them behaved less like fixed jobs than like corners of a triangle people kept sliding around. Karpman's insight, published in nineteen sixty-eight, is that nobody stays in one corner. A Rescuer whose help goes unappreciated flips, often within a single conversation, into a Persecutor, furious at the ingratitude. A Persecutor who oversteps can suddenly present as a Victim, wounded by the very person they were just attacking. Karpman's triangle strips away Berne's more specific five-role cast and leaves something more portable: a general description of any relationship where the same handful of people keep trading three roles, none of them stable, all of them capable of switching the instant it serves whatever racket feeling is due next.

The triangle has outgrown its parent, with barely anyone noticing the theft. Walk into a modern coaching session, a mediation training, a corporate conflict-resolution workshop, and you are considerably more likely to hear Karpman's three corners named than any of Berne's original games, a strange inversion for a concept that started as one detail buried inside somebody else's more famous book. I recognize the shape from my own work: a distance programme, ten districts, lay counsellors reporting up through supervisors who report up through me, and every so often a supervisor who has taken on, without ever saying so, a struggling counsellor's whole caseload out of genuine concern, playing Rescuer without naming it that way, flips within a single difficult week into Persecutor, resentful that the counsellor never asked for the extra load and never thanked her for it, while the counsellor, blindsided by an anger she didn't see building, lands squarely in Victim. Nobody in that sequence set out to run a game on anyone. The triangle just describes, with more precision than a management manual usually manages, how an ordinary act of goodwill can curdle into three overlapping grievances without a single person deciding to start one.

## Chapter Twelve: The Door That Swings the Other Way

The last chapter of this shelf spent its length arguing that a self is not something underneath a performance, waiting to be revealed, but something produced by a performance successfully staged in front of an audience, front region and back region running as two equally real, equally rehearsed versions of the same person. Berne, working independently and arriving five years later at a book with almost none of Goffman's vocabulary in it, describes something that rhymes with that claim and then outdoes it in one respect Goffman's own book never reaches. Goffman's performer chooses, consciously or half-consciously, which sign vehicles to send an audience and which to conceal, aware, at some level, that a performance is under way. Berne's game runs on a transaction that operates on two levels at once, an open, sociable surface message and a second message riding underneath it, and the players are not merely managing an audience's impression. Both are half-aware, at most, that the second message even exists, and each one, and this is the harder part to sit with, is getting something actually needed out of a script neither would admit to reading from if you asked directly.

Put the two books next to each other and the difference in what they are trying to explain comes into focus. Goffman is asking how a person manages to look consistent to an audience across many different rooms. Berne is asking why two specific people, alone with each other, keep having the identical argument, with the identical ending, for years, when a single honest sentence from either one of them could apparently end it in a minute. Goffman's answer is sociological: a front, once built, is expensive to abandon, and a team needs its members loyal to the performance. Berne's answer is developmental: the ending was decided on by somebody much younger than either current participant, and the argument, run correctly, one more time, is simply how that decision gets kept.

## Chapter Thirteen: Five Million Copies and a Rival Bestseller

Grove Press printed roughly three thousand copies of Games People Play in nineteen sixty-four and had gone back to press eight more times within a year, on the way to a run that eventually cleared five million copies and kept the book on the New York Times bestseller list for more than two years, an extraordinary run for a work built, in its first sixty pages, out of the same dense clinical vocabulary that fills any psychiatric textbook. Kurt Vonnegut, reviewing it for Life magazine in nineteen sixty-five, caught exactly why the second half of the book, the games thesaurus itself, was the part that actually sold: he called the games catalogue story lines that hacks will not exhaust in the next ten thousand years, a novelist recognizing, correctly, that Berne had accidentally produced a plot generator disguised as a diagnosis.

The book's own runaway success then produced an odd complication for its author's legacy. In nineteen sixty-seven, a personal friend and professional colleague of Berne's, the psychiatrist Thomas Harris, published I'm Okay, You're Okay, a more accessible restatement of transactional analysis built around Berne's own life-position framework, and Harris's book went on to sell seven million copies and spend nearly two years on the same bestseller list, numbers that edge out Berne's own. Ask most people today which four words they associate with transactional analysis and a fair number will say I'm okay, you're okay before they can name a single one of Berne's actual games, which means the theory's own popularizer, using material Berne had built and published first, ended up more famous in the culture at large than the man who invented the framework Harris was popularizing. Berne did not live to watch this play out at any length. He died of a heart attack in July of nineteen seventy, at sixty, three weeks after a first one, correcting galley proofs for his next book from a hospital bed when the second attack killed him.

## Chapter Fourteen: Party-Time Psychoanalysis

The same accessibility that sold five million copies is what the book's serious critics used against it, and the book paid for it twice over: once in its own reviews, and once in a cost that had nothing to do with rigor at all. Robert Coles, reviewing it in the New York Times in nineteen sixty-seven, found the vernacular itself distasteful, calling it, in three words that stuck to the book for years afterward, racy, vulgar, slangy, and singling out named games, Kick Me and Now I've Got You You Son of a Bitch among them, as evidence of a framework more interested in memorable insult than clinical seriousness. Academic critics pressed a harder version of the same charge. Ben Glancy dismissed the whole apparatus as parlor psychiatry and party-time psychoanalysis, and Roger Hite, writing some years later, found little research or scientific support behind Berne's claims at all, a version of a complaint this shelf has met before: a vocabulary flexible enough to file any observed behavior as some game or another, with no controlled study anywhere establishing how often games actually happen, how reliably a given payoff follows a given opening move, or what a disconfirming case would even look like.

The other cost is the one that had nothing to do with rigor: the book helped kick off the very kind of diffusion that erodes a serious framework's standing even as it multiplies its readership. Transactional analysis rode the same nineteen seventies wave that filled growth centers like Esalen with encounter groups and primal-scream workshops, one modality among several on offer to anyone looking for quick insight into why people behave the way they do, and mainstream academic psychology largely moved on from it by the nineteen eighties, drawn toward frameworks with actual controlled trials behind them. What survived was not the clinical theory so much as its most portable pieces: Karpman's triangle in every conflict-resolution seminar, I'm okay, you're okay as a phrase almost anyone can produce with no idea where it came from, and Berne's own name, attached to a book most people know by title only, sitting closer to trivia than to a live clinical tradition.

## Chapter Fifteen: The Dose and the Poison

Take game analysis as a diagnosis of a specific interaction and the framework proves its worth. Watching an argument for a scripted, repeating shape, an opening that looks milder than what follows, a switch nobody quite saw coming, a bad feeling both people leave with that neither one can fully explain, is a sharper way of reading a room than most people walk around with by default, and it explains something the language of simple disagreement cannot: why two intelligent, well-meaning people can have the identical fight, ending the identical way, every few weeks, for years, without either one seeming to learn anything from the last time.

Taken as an identity, the same vocabulary turns corrosive fast, and here the dosage this shelf has applied to every cynical framework it has picked up matters as much as it ever has. A person who starts filing every disagreement as somebody's game, every kindness as a Rescuer's opening move, every complaint as a Kick Me looking for its kick, has traded the discomfort of not knowing what is actually happening between two people for the false comfort of a label that always fits, and a tool this accommodating has stopped doing diagnostic work and started doing the opposite of intimacy, the very thing games exist, on Berne's own account, to avoid. Use it the way Berne himself seems, at his best, to have meant it: as a way to catch the fifth or sixth time an argument lands exactly where it landed before, and to ask, honestly, what feeling both people are actually leaving with, and whether either one of them has ever tried simply not delivering their scripted half of it.

The question worth carrying past this episode is not which of your own recurring arguments is a game, though most people can name one before they finish the thought. It is what would actually happen the next time you felt the pull to deliver your own familiar half of it, and chose, once, to answer from whichever of your three states was actually looking at what was true in that room instead.

Next time, the shelf stays with the same small, scripted stage a while longer, for a director who spent a career teaching actors to unlearn the very self-consciousness Berne and Goffman both spent their books describing: Keith Johnstone's Impro, and the argument that most people are not blocked because they lack ideas, but because they have spent a lifetime learning to reject the ones that arrive. Thanks for listening.
