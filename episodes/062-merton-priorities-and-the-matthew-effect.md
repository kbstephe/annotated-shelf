## Chapter One: A Trumpery Letter

In June 1858, Charles Darwin opened a parcel from the Malay Archipelago. Inside was a manuscript by Alfred Russel Wallace, a collector of beetles and birds whom Darwin knew mainly as a reliable correspondent, and Wallace asked him to pass it to the geologist Charles Lyell if it seemed of interest. Darwin read it at once and wrote to Lyell the same day, and what he wrote is the most naked page in the history of scientific credit.

Two years earlier Lyell had warned him, more than once, to publish before somebody else did. Darwin had answered with a sentence that sits well in a drawer of unsent letters. He rather hated the idea of writing for priority, he said, yet he would certainly be vexed if anyone published his doctrines before him. Now it had happened. He told Lyell that your words have come true with a vengeance, that he had never seen a more complete coincidence, and that if Wallace had been handed his own sketch of 1842, he could not have produced a better short abstract. Even the terms Wallace used stood as chapter heads in Darwin's manuscript. Then came the sentence about originality: all of it would be smashed.

What follows in the correspondence is a man arguing with himself in both directions inside a single week. First he resolves to step aside altogether and let Wallace have it. A few days later he wonders whether he might publish a short version, a dozen pages, and in the same letter says he cannot persuade himself that he can do so honourably. He ends by begging Lyell's forgiveness for what he calls a trumpery letter, influenced by trumpery feelings. The next day he writes again to take back the postscript in which he had promised never to trouble Lyell on the subject. He sends Joseph Hooker the Wallace manuscript and his own sketch of 1844, so that Hooker can see in his own handwriting that he had read it, and adds that it is miserable in him to care at all about priority. Within days, a young child of his was dead of scarlet fever, and the correspondence is a record of a man trying to do the decent thing while the house fell apart.

Lyell and Hooker did the thing Darwin could not do for himself. They arranged for both men's papers to be read together at the Linnean Society on the first of July, and their covering letter said they were considering not only the relative claims of the two men, but the interests of science generally. Wallace, for his part, spent the rest of his life declining to claim more than a junior share. Fifty years later he was still insisting on the contrast between his own hurried week and Darwin's twenty years of patient collecting.

This is the version of the story where everybody behaves well, and it is the only version in which the history of priority fights is short. The sociologist Robert Merton opened a paper in 1957 by asking why that version is so rare. He had a candidate answer, and the answer tells you what a funder or a panel is actually distributing when it distributes credit. The episode follows the answer through two papers, eleven years apart, and then to the three studies in recent years that tried to measure what the second paper claimed.

Everything quoted in this chapter about the Darwin letters comes from Merton's own account in the 1957 paper, which quotes the Life and Letters at length. I read that paper from a scan of the American Sociological Review text, and will say at the end which parts of the two papers I read and which I met only through other people's quotations.

## Chapter Two: Property That Cannot Be Locked

Merton was the American sociologist who gave the field of science studies its first working vocabulary. He wrote on the norms of science in the 1940s, he coined the term self-fulfilling prophecy in a paper of 1948, and by the mid-1950s he had a position at Columbia from which to ask empirical questions about how scientists reward one another. The 1957 paper, Priorities in Scientific Discovery, was his presidential address to the American Sociological Society that summer. It has an air of a man who has finished his main work and is enjoying the footnotes.

He opens with a procession of quarrels, and the procession is the argument, since the length of the list is what his question needs. Galileo, in The Assayer, going after Father Grassi for the telescope, Scheiner for the sunspots, and Simon Mayr for having the gall, Galileo says, to claim he saw the moons of Jupiter first. Newton against Hooke over optics and gravitation, then Newton against Leibniz over the calculus. Hooke against Huygens over the balance spring of a watch, so that Hooke had priority fights with the two most distinguished men of his age. Cavendish, Watt and Lavoisier over the composition of water. Adams and Le Verrier over Neptune. Jenner, Lister, Faraday, whose onetime mentor Davy opposed his election to the Royal Society on the ground that his discovery was not original. The German language, Merton notes, has a single compound word for the whole phenomenon, Prioritätsstreit.

The obvious explanation is simultaneous discovery. Two people reach the same place at the same time, and they fight. Merton says this is a trigger and not a cause, because scientists know perfectly well that independent discovery happens, and fear it, and are therefore driven to publish early. The next obvious explanation is human nature, and he sets it aside in a sentence with a lovely bit of dryness, remarking that the history of social thought is strewn with the corpses of those who tried to leap from human nature to particular forms of social conduct. The third explanation says science attracts the egotistical. He allows this might be partly so and offers reasons to doubt it explains much.

His reasons are two observations, and each is a small piece of detective work. The first is that these fights keep dragging in the meek. Cavendish was so shy that his notebooks, once opened, turned out to contain discoveries nobody had known he had made, and he never published them. Yet Cavendish was pulled into the Water Controversy. Davy asked at the time how it was possible that a man so unambitious and so fearful of the voice of fame should be engaged in such a conflict. Cavendish's biographer called the situation a perplexing dilemma, two unusually modest and unambitious men suddenly standing in a hostile position towards each other.

The second observation is who actually does the fighting. Very often it is not the principals. It was Wollaston's friends, not Wollaston, who insinuated that the young Faraday had usurped credit for electromagnetic rotation. It was Priestley, De Luc and Blagden, all men eminent in science and unblemished in character, who stirred Cavendish and Watt into their quarrel. When Neptune was found, it was Arago and a crowd of astronomers across France and England who stirred the pot, and not the shy and gentle Adams. These bystanders stand to gain nothing. Their personal status is not at risk. And they express a great moral indignation, which Merton, borrowing from Durkheim, reads as a signpost: disinterested moral indignation announces that a norm has been violated.

So which norm? His answer is that science has made originality the one thing it pays for. A result is valuable to the extent that it is new, and a discoverer's only claim to the thing he has found is that he found it first. Once he publishes, it belongs to everyone. He cannot withhold it, cannot license it, cannot demand that it be used on his terms. Merton lists the rights that ordinary property carries and shows that science whittles them down to a single one, the recognition by others that the result was his. That is the entire property, and it is the one asset that cannot be locked in a drawer. It exists only in other people's heads.

Merton draws two consequences from this, and the second is less obvious than the first. The first is that the intensity of priority fights stops looking like vanity. If recognition is the only title deed, a scientist who sees his deed questioned is in the position of a landowner whose boundary is being moved, and the surprising thing would be a man who did not protest. The second is that the institution has made modesty and originality compete, and has given the contest a referee that always rules for originality. Modesty cannot be observed by anyone. Nobody sees the scientist who had the same idea and did not report it. Originality can be seen by the whole community, and it is fully rewarded. In his phrase, the social organisation of science allocates honour in a way that tends to vitiate the institutional emphasis on modesty. Darwin's letters then read as a transcript of a man in whom the two norms were both live and both loud.

The part of the 1957 paper that has aged best is the machinery for paying the recognition out. Merton catalogues the forms: eponymy at the top, so that a law, a constant, a comet or an epoch carries the name, then the prizes, then the honorific titles, among which he gives a list of the Fathers of this and that, from Morgagni, Father of Pathology, down to Johann Dzierzon, Father of Modern Rational Beekeeping. The scale runs from epochs named for Newton at one end to the beekeeper at the other, and it is built like a Guttman scale, in which the person with the top honour is also awarded all the lesser ones. Remember that detail. The honours stack, and it will matter in a few minutes.

Merton adds a small coda about generosity, because a fair account needs it. Euler had solved the problem of the calculus of variations and sat on the solution until the twenty-three-year-old Lagrange, who had a new method that reached the same result, could publish first, so as not to deprive the young man of any part of the glory that was his due. Legendre did the same for Abel. Darwin and Wallace, in his telling, are the supreme case. But the acts of generosity go into the record as exceptions, and that bookkeeping tells you which way the norm normally points.

A reader who stops at 1957 comes away with a tidy picture. Credit is the currency of science, it has the great virtue of being given for something real, and fights over it are the immune system of the institution. Then comes the harder question the paper leaves standing. What happens when the credit has to be divided between two people, or two papers, or two applicants, and the community cannot tell their work apart? Who gets it, and what decides?

## Chapter Three: The Forty-First Chair

Eleven years later, in January 1968, Merton published a paper in Science with a title that has since become a phrase in ordinary speech. The Matthew Effect in Science took its name from the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-five, verse twenty-nine, in which unto every one that hath shall be given, and from him that hath not shall be taken away even that which he hath. The paper's evidence was a set of interviews with Nobel laureates, nearly all of them conducted by Merton's student and later colleague Harriet Zuckerman for her doctoral research. Merton said later that he had drawn so heavily on her work that the paper should have carried both names. That acknowledgement reaches me through a biographical summary and I have not seen the original wording, but the point it makes stands: the founding data of this field were collected by someone who was not listed on the title page, and that is itself a small specimen of the thing the paper describes.

The effect, as he defines it, concerns shares of recognition. Famous scientists receive larger shares for particular contributions than unknown scientists do, and the unknown get the recognition withheld until they have made their mark. The definition has been quoted so often that it is easy to overlook how strong the word particular is. He is not saying famous people do more or better work, nor that their fame was earned or unearned. He is saying that for the same piece of work, the credit goes differently depending on whose name is on it.

The evidence from the laureates is conversational and has the force of confession. A laureate describing a paper written with junior collaborators says that people see his name and remember it and forget the others. Another says that you usually pick out the name you already know, and that even if it is last, it will be the one that sticks. A third, a physicist, describes the independent-discovery version from both sides of his own career. Early on, he remembers the irritation of watching someone famous work out the obvious, which he had also worked out, and collect the credit for it. Later he suspects, and says so, that he is probably getting credit now for things other people figured out, because he is notorious. In a sentence Merton himself wrote about this man, the later overestimate and the earlier underestimate compounded into a sort of rough-hewn justice, made of two compensating injustices.

The laureates also acted on all this. Merton reports that eminent scientists tend to give first place in jointly authored papers to a junior collaborator, and that some refuse to coauthor at all, so as not to diminish the recognition due to their less well-known associates. They knew what their name did to a byline, and they spent their authority trying to counteract it.

The paper also supplies its most durable image, the forty-first chair. The French Academy, Merton reminds the reader, decided early that only forty could be members and so be called immortals. The cap made it inevitable that talented people would be excluded across the centuries, and those who held the forty chairs were not always better than those who stood just outside, but the Academy's seal made them look as if they were. In a prize that goes to a few and is denied to equally good others, the distance between the forty and the forty-first is a distance of status and nothing else. A reader who has just come from the 1957 paper will hear the earlier machinery working here, because the Guttman scale of honours means that the person who is given the first honour also finds the second, third and fourth on the way.

Merton also had an account of why the effect exists, and it is functional, the sociologist's way of saying it does some good while doing some harm. Attention is the scarce resource. Reading everything is impossible, so scientists use the name on a paper as a cue to what to read first. A famous name earns an earlier look. The earlier look, in turn, means the famous name is the one remembered when the contribution is later discussed, and the memory attaches the credit. On this reading, the effect is a side effect of a sensible habit, and a bad side effect, because it damages the reward system that the 1957 paper had shown to be the engine of science's moral order. The laureates were right to be unhappy, and science would ideally keep the selective attention and discard the selective credit.

I'd like to be careful about what that account leaves open, because the next chapter depends on it. It is an account of why a famous name captures credit when the work is the same. It says nothing about whether the credit ought to be the same, and the assumption that it ought is where the whole episode is heading.

## Chapter Four: Why the Name Might Count

The philosopher Michael Strevens published a paper in 2006, in a journal on the history and philosophy of science, with the title The Role of the Matthew Effect in Science. Its argument is that Merton's laureates are wrong, and that the credit they were uneasy about was fairly earned. This is the strongest version of the case for the effect I know of, and fair enough to give an admirer of Merton a hard moment, so it gets a full chapter.

Strevens begins with a puzzle that Merton walks past. The laureates who regard the effect as a pathology are the people who benefit from it, and science is a community in which credit is distributed by scientists. The Matthew effect exists only because the scientists who denounce it keep awarding credit in the way the Gospel describes. If they thought it was unjust, and were all in a position to stop doing it, why had they not? Merton's answer is that they cannot resist the pull of a familiar name. Strevens thinks that is too weak. Scientists spend careers policing the allocation of credit, as the first half of this episode showed. It is hard to believe that all of this machinery comes undone because they cannot tell the difference between a name that drew their attention and a result that earns it.

His alternative has three steps, and each is easy to say. First, credit in science is paid in proportion to the contribution a piece of work makes to the world's stock of reliable knowledge. Second, the value of a contribution depends not only on whether it is true but on how far the rest of the community can trust it while the checking is still going on. A drug trial's result that is probably right but unconfirmed is worth less than the same result confirmed, because fewer people will act on it, and for those who do, the risk is higher. Third, the trust that can reasonably be placed in a result depends on who is reporting it. So much of what happens in an experiment is not in the paper that a reader needs a reason to believe the unreported parts were done properly, and a record of previous work that held up is one such reason.

Put those together and the effect follows. An eminent name on a paper makes the work more usable in the years before anyone has checked it, which makes it a larger contribution, which earns larger credit. The credit goes to the eminent person because the eminence did something. On this reading the laureate who strikes his name from a paper to protect a junior colleague has taken something away from the research. It has become less trustworthy, and less quickly used, than it would have been with the name attached, and the loss falls on everyone who might have built on it.

The account also makes a testable prediction that Merton's did not. If the credit follows the guarantee, then once the result is independently confirmed, the guarantee has no more work to do, and the later fame of the junior scientist should not retroactively raise the credit for work already confirmed. Merton had been unsure on this point, and the laureates' comments in his paper pointed both ways. Strevens says the evidence he could find was mixed, and takes the prediction as a mark against his own view if it fails.

I find this argument stronger than it first sounds, and the reason is a thing medical researchers know without being told. A paper from a group with a track record of careful trials is read differently from a paper by an unknown group, because the unknown group's methods section might be hiding anything. That is Bayesian reasoning, and a reasonable one. The honest summary of Strevens's claim is that the Matthew effect is the credit-side face of a rational prior.

Now apply the argument to the thing that the first episodes in this series spent their time on, which is a panel with finite money. A panel member reading an application from a distinguished name is relying on the same prior, with the same excuse. The application's methods section will be taken on trust more readily than an unknown's, and Strevens would say the panel is right to do so. Where the track record is actually informative about unreported details, it should count.

The defence has an edge, though, and the edge is the word informative. A name vouches better when the unreported details are many and the prior record is a good guide to them, and worse when the result is checkable on its face. What it guarantees is reliability. Importance is a separate matter, and so is whether a young person with a worse name has the better idea. The guarantee also covers work already done, which is a different thing from funds to be awarded on the strength of work about to be done. Strevens's argument justifies the larger increment of credit to a famous name for a particular result. Whether it extends to an award for a future project is a separate claim the paper never makes. Whoever uses the Matthew effect to defend a funding rule has to walk an extra step on their own.

Three bodies of evidence bear on that step, and they arrive in the order in which the field built its tools. The first is the early sociology.

## Chapter Five: The Sociologists Test It

In 1970 Stephen Cole published a paper in the American Journal of Sociology with a title that states its test, Professional Standing and the Reception of Scientific Discoveries. I know its findings through a summary and not the paper itself. As the summary has it, Cole found that the assessed quality of a paper was a more important determinant of its initial reception than any of the stratification variables, the author's rank, the prestige of the department, the awards. What the author's reputation did change was speed: papers of equal quality diffused faster when their authors were better known. Cole and his brother Jonathan Cole followed it with a book in 1973, Social Stratification in Science, and a contemporary review in Nature summarised their verdict on whether universalism was upheld in science as a finding that the departures from it were rarely important. The book I have not read, and that summary is secondhand.

I want to treat these results with the respect due to the first people who tried the experiment, and then say what a careful reader should hear in them. The Coles measured quality by the thing they could measure, which was citations to the work. A citation is a vote cast by someone who has read the paper, and the readers may have been guided to the paper, or to a belief about its importance, by the very status the study was trying to hold constant. If the Matthew effect works through who gets read, a quality measure built from what was read has the effect built into it. This is the problem that the economists later named, and it explains why a thirty-year gap followed. The sociologists could not hold quality constant, because quality is what a community of status-conscious readers decides it is.

The Merton paper had seen part of this coming. Strevens notes that Merton, in 1968, said systematic information on the cases that would settle the matter was too sparse to study in detail. What was needed was an experiment in which status moves and the work stays put.

## Chapter Six: A Prize That Cannot Change Past Papers

In 2012 the economists Pierre Azoulay, Toby Stuart and Yanbo Wang circulated a working paper with the title Matthew: Effect or Fable?, which appeared in Management Science in 2014. Their design is a good example of how to buy a clean answer by narrowing the question.

They looked at the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, whose investigator appointment is a major prize in the life sciences: a five-year award, usually renewed, with a large research budget, given to scientists already well along in their careers. The prize is a status shock, and the question is what happens to the way other scientists treat the work the winner had already published. A prize that arrives in 1995 cannot improve a paper written in 1992. Any change in how that paper is cited after the award is therefore a change in perception, in how readers see the paper, because the paper is the same. That was the whole trick, and it separates the thing Merton described from the thing a critic like Strevens would call real value.

The second half of the design is the control. A random sample of scientists would not do, since the winners are by definition exceptional. So the authors assembled a comparison group of people who had won early-career fellowships in the same subfields, the Pew, Searle, Beckman, Packard and Rita Allen scholarships, and then matched each winner's article to a control article with very similar publication year, journal, and citation history up to the time of the award. They started from the four hundred and forty-three investigators appointed between 1984 and 2003, and the control pool held some twenty-four hundred scientists.

What they found, to take the working paper's figures, is a boost in citations that is real and modest. In their baseline comparison an article by a winner collected about twenty-four more cumulative citations than its matched control, against a control average of sixty-eight. That moves an article from around the seventy-second percentile of its cohort to around the eighty-first. The authors call a nine-percentile shift relatively small though still meaningful. The published abstract adds that the effect is limited to a short window of time. It is also concentrated. The boost is larger for papers written close to the time of the prize, for papers in newer areas of science, for papers that combine ideas from several fields, for papers in lower-impact journals, and for winners who were less well established when they received the award.

Read that pattern against what Strevens predicted and what Merton described, and they agree. The boost shrinks when quality is easy to read and grows when it is hard, and exactly what a signal would do if it were serving as a prior on reliability, and exactly what Merton's attention story would predict as well. The pattern does not choose between the two. What it rules out is the strongest popular version of the Matthew effect, in which fame automatically multiplies credit for everything. The multiplier on offer is small, and it fades as the work gets checked, and it is largest where the readers have least else to go on.

There is a second finding in the paper that is easy to skip and should not be. When the authors measured status at the level of the scientist and did not adjust for the quality of the individual article, the effect looked much bigger. The older literature mostly worked that way. They conclude that the standard approach can substantially overstate the effect of status, because a famous scientist's papers are on average better, and a regression that cannot see article quality credits the fame with what the quality did. This is the same trap the Coles faced from the other direction. Both traps point the same way: the effect on credit for a given piece of work is smaller than the crude correlation between fame and citations suggests.

I should give the limits of the design its due. The prize also brings money and visibility, and the authors cannot fully separate the signal from the attention a prize draws to a laboratory. The controls were themselves prize-winners, which sharpens the contrast and also means the study measures the added effect of one more prize on top of an earlier one. The Matthew effect in its original form, from nothing to something, is not what is being measured here. The study is a test of the marginal prize on already-successful people.

That leaves what Strevens's defence could not reach: the step from credit to money.

## Chapter Seven: Two Applicants and a Line

In April 2018 three sociologists, Thijs Bol, Mathijs de Vaan and Arnout van de Rijt, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences a paper with the title The Matthew Effect in Science Funding. They assembled all the review scores and funding decisions in a Dutch early-career programme that had distributed about two billion euros, for scientists who had recently finished the doctorate, and looked at the people clustered on either side of the funding line.

Here is an invented pair, built to show the logic, and labelled as constructed. Two applicants submit proposals in the same round. A panel scores the first at the line and the second a hair beneath it, a difference smaller than the disagreement between any two of the reviewers. The first is funded and the second is not. The reviewers who read their proposals could not tell them apart, and any statistician looking at the scores would say the two had the same expected merit. The study asks what happens to each of them over the next eight years.

The answer, from the paper's abstract, is that the winners just above the line accumulate more than twice as much research funding over the following eight years as the nonwinners just below it, a gap of about one hundred and eighty thousand euros. The authors find no evidence that this is because the winners did better work with their first grant. The achievements enabled by the preceding grant do not explain the improved odds. So the early funding acted as a credential. It was an asset for acquiring later funding, and not only a payment for later work.

The sentence in the abstract that gets the longest look here is the last. The authors write that, surprisingly, the funding gap is partly created by the applicants themselves, who, after failing to win one grant, apply for another grant less often. Run the invented pair to the end of the story with that finding in hand. The funded one writes the next application with a line on her CV that reads as proof that the first panel thought well of her, and she applies again. The second reads the rejection as a verdict, because that is how rejection sounds, and writes fewer applications. Some years later one has money and the other has less, and the difference between them began with a number that the panel itself would have said was noise.

This is the forty-first chair made literal. Merton's description of the French Academy was a thought about thresholds and what they do to the people near it. A fixed supply of places creates a line, and a line manufactures a status difference larger than the quality difference that put people on opposite sides of it. The Dutch study measured that manufactured difference with an unusually clean design, because the scores near the line are as close to random assignment as real data get.

It is also a different phenomenon from the one Strevens defended, and the difference matters. Strevens explained why a famous name should receive larger credit for a given result, and his explanation worked through trust in unreported detail. The Dutch result has nothing to do with faith in the unwritten parts of a methods section, because the reviewers had already scored the proposals the same. What turned the tie into a gap was the label attached afterward, and the response of the people who received it. If you ask which version of the Matthew effect the grant system should worry about, the answer sits here, since this is the one the guarantee defence does not excuse. The credit effect on papers is modest and decays, and the funding effect at the threshold accumulates.

Two cautions. The population is recent doctorates in one country's early-career programme, and the claim about sustained advantage rests on that setting. And the study shows that winning raised later funding odds with no evidence of better work, but it cannot say how many dollars were wasted by funding the wrong people, because it does not know who the right people were. That is a limit of every study of this kind, and it is a version of the problem that has run through every episode in this series: the check cannot see the thing that matters. The authors say the gap shows that early funding is an asset for later funding. They do not say it shows that the money was misspent.

## Chapter Eight: What the Three Papers Settle

Take the claims one at a time, because the evidence supports them unequally.

The 1957 claim is that priority fights come from the structure of the reward system, since recognition is the only property a scientist holds, and not from the vanity of scientists. I find this well supported by the cases Merton assembled, and in particular by the observation about bystanders, which a theory of individual egotism cannot absorb. It has an obvious consequence for anyone who runs a credit system: the fights about who was first will not go away, however the system is designed, and the better design makes them cheaper to settle and not rarer. Lyell and Hooker's joint reading in 1858 was an institution doing exactly that.

The 1968 claim is that for the same work, famous names get larger increments of credit. The evidence from the interviews is testimony, from the very people it flatters, and the natural-experiment evidence says the effect on papers is real, small, short, and largest where the quality is hardest to read. Strevens's defence suits that profile. I would hold it with a qualification, which is that the defence explains why a prior on reliability is sensible and cannot, from reliability alone, defend a prior on importance. A famous name tells you the methods section is probably honest. It does not tell you the question is right.

The third claim is the one the series has been circling since the Bush episode, and Merton never made it in so many words: that a funding system built on distinction will compound itself. Here the Dutch result is what a funder needs to know. Small, noisy differences at a threshold are turned by the system into large, lasting differences, and part of the mechanism runs through the applicants' own decisions about whether to try again. I would place it above the other two in practical weight and below them in generality, because it was measured in one programme.

There is a suggestion in all this that goes back to Polanyi's jigsaw puzzle, from the last episode. Polanyi imagined helpers at one table, each watching the pieces the others had just placed. Merton's papers describe the wages at that table. The wage is recognition, it is paid in the single currency of other people's attention, and the helpers who have already been paid are watched more closely than the others. Polanyi's picture needs the helpers to see each other's work, and Merton shows that they see some helpers' work more clearly than others. A funder that adds money on top of that is adding a second round of watching, and on the Dutch evidence the second round can be set in motion by a number nobody believed in.

A reader who sits on panels can take two practical rules from this, and the first is the narrator's own. When two proposals sit within the noise of the scoring, say so aloud, and fund neither or both by a draw, or by a criterion the panel has agreed in advance that has nothing to do with the score. Treating the tie as a tie removes the mechanism the Dutch study measured, which is a threshold manufacturing a difference. The second is for the applicant on the wrong side of a line. The finding that rejected applicants reapply less often is the one part of the gap that an individual can close alone. A score just under the line is, on the evidence of that paper, a weaker verdict about the work than it feels like at the time it arrives.

On whether to read the papers, the 1957 address is the more rewarding of the two, long as it is. It is witty, it is full of letters, and its Darwin section is worth the price of the volume. The 1968 paper is shorter and its most famous idea is clearest in Strevens's discussion of it. The Azoulay paper is readable by a clinician in an evening, and its design section is a model of how to ask a clean question. The Dutch paper takes less time still.

I should say how this was grounded. I read the 1957 paper from a scan of the journal text, covering the opening survey of disputes, the discussion of the norms and the reward system, the eponymy catalogue, and the Darwin and Wallace section, while the section on multiple discoveries and the closing pages went unread. The 1968 paper itself never came into my hands. The definition and the forty-first chair passage come from quotations in the Azoulay paper, in its NBER working-paper version, and the laureates' remarks and the account of Merton's explanation come from Strevens's article, read in full, which quotes Merton with page references. The acknowledgement that the paper should have carried Zuckerman's name comes from a biographical summary. Cole's paper and the Coles' book reached me through search summaries and a review. The Dutch paper reached me as its published abstract and not its tables, so the figures stated here are the abstract's and any more detailed claim about the Veni and Vidi stages is outside what I checked. A grant committee has ranked forty proposals, and the money covers twenty-two. The panel chair has been told that the twenty-second and the twenty-third are separated by a rounding error, and that one of them will be remembered for the next eight years as a person the system chose. The question to take into that room is what the committee would do if it were allowed to say out loud that it does not know.
