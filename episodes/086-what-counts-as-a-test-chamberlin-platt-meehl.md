## Chapter One: A Theory of the Weather

Suppose a man tells you he has a theory of the weather, and that it predicts rain next April. April comes, and it rains. You are not impressed. Suppose instead his theory predicts more rain in April than in May, and he shows you a table with three asterisks, the mark of a result that chance alone would produce less than once in a thousand tries. You are still not impressed, and you are right not to be, because you knew that before he had a theory. Now suppose he names seven particular days of the month as wet ones, and the sky obliges on those seven. Now you lean forward. And if he names the amount for each of those days, and gets all seven within a reasonable margin, you start to wonder what he knows.

That ladder is Paul Meehl's, from a lecture he gave to the American Psychological Association in 1976 and published two years later. Meehl was a clinical psychologist at Minnesota, a past president of that association, and the author of a famous short book showing that simple formulas predicted patients' outcomes as well as clinicians did or better. He built the weather ladder to make one claim feel obvious before he made it uncomfortable. Every rung is a passed test. What changes as you climb is how likely the theory was to fail if it were worthless.

The question under this episode is older than any statistics. When a scientist says an idea has been tested and survived, what has happened? There is a version of the answer every student learns: you state a hypothesis, you predict, you check, and a matching result supports you. Three papers, each short enough to read on a train, attack that version from different sides. A geologist in 1897 said the trouble was the scientist's heart. A biophysicist in 1964 said it was the design of the experiment. And Meehl, in 1967 and again in 1978, said it was the shape of the prediction, and that in his own field the standard method made tests easier to pass as the data got better.

This episode opens a series on the statistical analysis plan, the document a trialist signs before the data are unblinded, promising which comparisons count and how. Each promise in that document was won in an argument between named people. The first promise is the one this episode is about: that the trial will test something.

## Chapter Two: The Parent of a Family

Thomas Chamberlin was a geologist at Chicago, remembered now mostly for a theory of how the solar system formed. The paper that outlived the theory began as a talk and was revised in 1897 for a column called Studies for Students in the Journal of Geology; that revision is the one I read. It is twelve pages, written in the long Victorian sentence, and it is a paper about love.

He describes three phases of mental procedure. The first he calls the ruling theory. A man offers an explanation for something new. What follows in his account is motivated reasoning described sixty years before anyone named it. The moment the explanation seems satisfying, affection for it springs into existence, he writes, and as it grows into a definite theory, the author's parental feelings cluster around it. He still tells himself he holds it tentatively, but he holds it lovingly tentatively, which is not the same as impartially. Then the selection begins without his noticing: the mind lingers with pleasure on facts that fit and feels a natural coldness toward the ones that do not. The theory is stretched to fit the evidence, and the evidence squeezed to fit the theory. A premature explanation becomes a tentative theory, then an adopted one, and finally a ruling one, and once that last stage is reached, unless the theory happens to be true, the hope of good results is gone.

The second phase is the working hypothesis, an idea held as a tool for finding facts rather than as a thing to be proved. Chamberlin calls it an incalculable advance, and then points out the obvious defect. Affection clings to a beloved idea just as easily when you call it a hypothesis. The name changes; the parent does not.

His remedy is the third phase. You bring into view every rational explanation of the thing in front of you and give each a working form and a place in the investigation. The investigator becomes the parent of a family of hypotheses, and by being parent to all of them is forbidden to dote on one. The method, in his phrase, distributes the effort and divides the affections. Where some of the hypotheses are other people's, the right use of the method requires adopting them into the family on the same terms as your own.

Then comes the example, and it is not the one later readers remember. Chamberlin takes the origin of the Great Lake basins. Several geologists had pressed several explanations with great force. The basins were river valleys before the ice came; that was practically demonstrable. Their outlets were blocked by glacial drift; also demonstrable. Great lobes of ice moved through them and carried their bottoms away downstream; demonstrable again. And the weight and cold of the ice may have bent the crust beneath them downward. Chamberlin's conclusion is that the full solution needs every one of these, plus an estimate of how much each contributed and in what way. The method of a single hypothesis, he says, is incompetent here, because it stops when it finds one effective cause and is satisfied.

The next man to pick up this paper read it differently. Chamberlin's family of hypotheses is not a tournament in which one child survives. He expects several to survive, and the work is apportioning the credit among them. He even notes that the method is unpopular with young students, who would rather be told the glaciers scooped the basins out than estimate the fraction each of three agencies accomplished.

So Chamberlin's diagnosis is psychological and his cure is a habit. Keep the rivals alive, because the only alternative is to let one of them rule. What he does not say is how an investigator with a full family of hypotheses decides which ones to bury. That question waited sixty-seven years for a man in a hurry.

## Chapter Three: The Man With the Notebook

John Platt was a biophysicist, and his paper in Science in October 1964 opens with a joke at the expense of grant committees. Scientists, he wrote, keep up a polite fiction that all science is equal, which keeps everyone cordial when it comes to recommending each other for government money. Some fields, he went on, are moving an order of magnitude faster than others, and he named two: molecular biology and high-energy physics. His explanation was not talent, funding, or the tractability of the subject. It was a method.

He called it strong inference, and it has four steps. Devise alternative hypotheses. Devise an experiment, or several, whose possible outcomes will each exclude one or more of them. Carry the experiment out cleanly. Then recycle: make subhypotheses about what remains, and go again. It is, he says, like climbing a tree, where at each fork nature chooses the branch. Or like the table in a first-year chemistry book for identifying an unknown sample: add reagent A, and if a red precipitate forms, filter and add reagent B.

His evidence is a set of scenes. At the Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, he reports, the blackboards of Francis Crick or Sidney Brenner on any given morning would be covered with logical trees, the new result on top, two or three explanations beneath it, and under those the experiments that could cut the list down. At a biophysics conference in Boulder in 1958, Leo Szilard told the room that the problems of protein synthesis and antibody formation were nearer solution than people thought. If you do stupid experiments and finish one a year, Szilard said, it can take fifty years. If you stop and think how proteins can possibly be made, there are only about five ways, not fifty, and a few experiments will distinguish them. An electron microscopist objected that this was philosophy of science. Someone asked whether the official photograph would be before lunch or after. And on the way out, Platt reports, one man was heard muttering to himself, asking what Szilard expected him to do, shoot himself?

Then Platt goes back to Chamberlin. He quotes the passage on parental affection and the line about dividing the affections, and he gives it a job. Disproof, Platt says, is a hard doctrine. If you have a hypothesis and I have another, one of them must be eliminated, and scientists seem to have no choice but to be soft-headed or quarrelsome. Chamberlin's method removes the difficulty: when each person holds several hypotheses, the conflict is between ideas rather than people, and everyone can aim at disproof every day without combativeness. The lab, Platt writes, becomes a detective story the students can hardly wait to get back to.

That is a generous reading, and it is also a quiet conversion. Chamberlin's family was a set of co-causes to be weighed. Platt's is a list of suspects to be eliminated until one is left. In the Great Lakes, a Platt-style experiment that "excluded" glacial scouring would have been wrong, because scouring was part of the answer. Platt's tree works when the branches are exclusive, when Szilard's five ways are five different mechanisms of which nature uses one.

Platt's cleanest fork comes from organic chemistry, and it runs to a definite end. Do the bonds in benzene alternate, single and double around the ring, or are all six equivalent? If they alternate, there should be five distinct ways to attach two identical substituents to the ring. If they are equivalent, three. Chemists made the compounds and counted. Three. Nobody needed a significance test, because nothing in the world except the right theory would have produced the number three.

He also has a tool that anyone can carry, which he calls the Question. On hearing any theory, ask its author what result would kill it. On hearing an experiment described, ask what hypothesis it disproves. His wording is courtly, "But sir, what experiment could disprove your hypothesis?" and he predicts that many supposedly great scientists would sputter and turn livid. He suggests that funding agencies could use it, and tells them who to back: not the man who wants to make a survey or a more detailed study, but the man with the notebook, with alternatives and a deciding experiment for each, already at work.

Platt has a passage that sounds, at first, like a rejection of numbers. Much of the mathematicising in physics and chemistry, he writes, is irrelevant if not misleading. You can catch a phenomenon in a logical box or a mathematical box; the logical box is coarse but strong, the mathematical one fine-grained but flimsy. When an experiment agrees with a calculation to five decimal places, a great many alternatives are excluded. When the fit is to one or two places, it may be no better than a rule of thumb, and a qualitative exclusion would test more.

Hold the benzene count next to that passage. The count of three is a number, and a very small one. What makes it strong is not that it is qualitative. It is that the chance of getting three if the theory were wrong was close to nothing. Platt's tree assumes every fork is like that, that the alternatives make different predictions and nature can only satisfy one. The next question is what happens in a field where they do not.

## Chapter Four: The Paradox

Meehl's 1967 paper is thirteen pages in Philosophy of Science, addressed to philosophers, and its tone is a man calmly describing a fire in the next room. The paradox goes like this. In physics, when you improve your instruments or gather more data, the test a theory has to pass gets harder. In psychology, the same improvement makes it easier. So the corroboration a theory earns from a success gets stronger in physics as methods improve, and weaker in psychology.

The first is about the null hypothesis, the claim that two groups do not differ at all. Meehl argues that in biology and social science this claim is, in his bracketed word, quasi-always false. Any outcome you care about depends on many causes, and for two naturally defined groups to have identical averages, every cause would have to balance to the last decimal. He gives an example that has become famous among methodologists: the speed at which schoolchildren name colours, compared across their fathers' religious preference. There is no theory linking the two. But religion correlates with social class, social class with how much a child wants to please a teacher, father's occupation with the child's intelligence and vocabulary, and a child from a more liturgical church might simply have more colour in his life. The difference will not be zero. Meehl and David Lykken had data on more than fifty-five thousand Minnesota high school seniors and forty-five assorted variables, from birth order and religion to interest in woodworking, and ninety-one percent of the pairwise associations among them were statistically significant.

The second move is the switch most researchers had made by then, from testing whether two groups differ to testing whether they differ in the direction the theory predicts. That sounds more demanding. Meehl shows what it buys. Imagine an urn of theories and an urn of experiments, paired at random, so that a theory has no connection at all to the experiment it is said to predict. Since some true difference almost always exists, the true difference favours the theory's direction half the time, by pure symmetry. A perfectly powerful experiment would then confirm a perfectly worthless theory in about half of all tests.

The third move is the paradox itself. As you add subjects, or measure more reliably, or control more noise, the probability that a worthless theory passes rises toward one half. The researcher who does everything the textbook asks, who calculates power and recruits enough people, is lowering the bar his theory has to clear. In physics the theory supplies the number that plays the role of the null, a predicted value, and more precision narrows the band around that value until any real miss shows up. In psychology the null is a straw man that more data will always knock down, and the only thing the theory contributes is a guess about which way it falls.

Meehl adds a meteorological image of his own that the 1978 lecture would expand into the ladder you began with. Nobody, he says, would be impressed by a theory that successfully predicted rain on the seventeenth of April, given that it rains on about half the days in April anyway.

If the episode on Fisher and Neyman earlier in this series comes to mind, it should. That episode described Niels Waller's test of this exact prediction, as reported by Gerd Gigerenzer: a computer chose personality items and random directions, and forty-six percent of the coin-flip hypotheses came out confirmed. Meehl's arithmetic said a half; the machine found a little under.

The paper ends with what Meehl calls five intellectual vices that make the situation worse than it has to be, and one of them is a portrait. A zealous and clever investigator runs experiment one; it fails; he blames an auxiliary assumption, one of the side beliefs about instruments and conditions that link a theory to an observation; experiment two tests that assumption, using new auxiliaries of its own; and so on through a long series that looks to an uncritical reader like an integrated research programme without ever corroborating or refuting a single strand. Meanwhile he has a long publication list and is promoted to full professor. Meehl's phrase for him, a potent but sterile intellectual rake, is the paper's one indulgence, and its footnote is another: the author is not mad at anybody, but he is a bit distressed at the state of psychology. Imre Lakatos, whom Meehl cites, had a name for the rake's career earlier in this series: a degenerating programme, one that only patches itself after each failure and never predicts anything new.

## Chapter Five: Running the Numbers

The paradox is easier to believe with a sum in front of you, so here is one. The numbers are made up to show the arithmetic.

Imagine a field where a typical true difference between conditions is small, a tenth of a standard deviation, and where it points in a direction unrelated to anyone's theory. A researcher with a worthless theory predicts a direction and runs a one-sided test at the conventional five percent level. With fifty people per group, the test is weak. When the true difference happens to lie in his direction, he reaches significance about one time in eight; when it lies the other way, almost never. Averaged over the two, a worthless theory passes about seven times in a hundred.

Now give him five thousand per group. A tenth of a standard deviation is now detected nearly every time it exists. It lies in his direction on one occasion in two, so the worthless theory passes half the time.

Set that beside a theory with something to it, one whose predicted effect is real and half a standard deviation in size. With fifty per group it passes about four times in five; with five thousand, essentially always.

So how much should a pass move you? One honest measure is the ratio of two chances: how likely the pass was if the theory is right, against how likely if it is worthless. In the small study, four in five against seven in a hundred, about eleven to one. In the big one, about one against one half, two to one. The larger, better, more expensive study produced a success that tells you less about the theory.

There is an objection here and it is a good one: the large study tells you far more about the size of the difference. That is true, and Meehl would agree. His complaint is about what the significance test is being used to support. Report the effect and its interval, and the big study is plainly better. Report instead that the theory predicted a difference and the difference was significant, you have reported something a coin could have arranged half the time.

Now go back to Platt's tree and try to climb it in this field. Suppose, in Chamberlin's spirit, the researcher lists three rival explanations for why some intervention should improve recall: it raises arousal, it sharpens attention, or it reduces anxiety. Each predicts that the treated group recalls more. A significant difference in that direction excludes none of them. Platt's fork requires branches that predict different outcomes, and directional predictions in a world where everything affects everything a little all point the same way. Strong inference does not fail here because scientists are lazy. It has nothing to bite on, because the rivals have been stated too weakly to disagree.

That is the first place the three papers meet. Chamberlin says keep the rivals. Platt says pit them against one another. Meehl says that in his field the rivals, as usually stated, cannot be made to compete, because none of them forbids anything the others allow.

## Chapter Six: Sir Karl and Sir Ronald

Meehl's 1978 paper runs to nearly thirty pages, and the title names the two knights it sets against each other: Sir Karl Popper, who held that a theory is tested only by putting it in danger of refutation, and Sir Ronald Fisher, whose tests of significance had become psychology's standard of proof. Meehl calls the two, as practised, not well integrated, perhaps incompatible.

He opens with what happens to theories in the soft areas of psychology. They never die, he says, borrowing General MacArthur on old soldiers; they fade away. His student days were full of level of aspiration, a construct that was supposed to explain everything from psychopathology to politics; forty years later the phrase was missing from the textbook indexes. It was never refuted and never confirmed. It dried up and blew away. A newer example was the risky shift, the finding that groups make riskier choices than individuals. Meehl quotes Dorwin Cartwright, who reviewed one hundred and ninety-six papers from the 1960s and concluded that after ten years the original question remained unsolved, and that the research had cast doubt on whether the phenomenon was even what it had been called.

This is the paradox's consequence at the scale of a field. If tests are easy to pass, every theory collects successes. When all of them have successes, none is ever forced out, and the only thing that ends one is boredom.

Then Meehl names the culprit. His sentence, "Sir Ronald has befuddled us, mesmerized us, and led us down the primrose path," is followed by the charge that relying on refuting the null to corroborate a substantive theory was one of the worst things that ever happened in the history of psychology.

The second exhibit is the review article. A reviewer tabulates studies of a theory, marking each with one, two or three asterisks for significance in the predicted direction. Meehl's invented example is Fisbee's theory of the mind, with seven favourable results and three unfavourable. The reviewer counts noses and concludes the theory is fairly well supported, although further research is needed to explain the discrepancies. Meehl calls this scientifically preposterous. A confirmation is logically weak, because a true prediction does not prove the theory that made it. A refutation, if the auxiliary conditions held, is logically strong. Seven passes that each had an even chance under a worthless theory do not outweigh three failures; seen properly, the failures do Fisbee far more damage than the passes do him good.

Meehl's answer to the obvious rebuttal is about fertiliser. Someone will say Fisher was a genius and his methods worked in agronomy; why not in psychology? Meehl's reply turns on the distance between two claims, the statistical hypothesis that the plots differ and the substantive theory about the world. If you want to know whether potash helps your corn, and plots with potash yield more, then given what everyone knows about how corn grows, and given that the harvest did not cause what you did in the spring, the only substantive conclusion is that the potash made the difference. The statistical hypothesis and the theory are, as he puts it, quasi-identical. In soft psychology, a difference between groups is consistent with Fisbee's theory, with his rivals, and with Meehl's grandmother's common sense. The test refutes the null and leaves every theory standing.

A well-run trial of whether a drug lowers blood pressure sits close to the potash. Randomisation means the groups differed only in what was given, the outcome is the thing you care about, and a significant difference in the right direction says, with little remaining distance, that the drug did it. The same trial's claim about why, that the fall came through one particular pathway rather than another, sits back in Fisbee's world. The test of the intervention is strong; the test of the mechanism, done the same way, may be no better than rain in April.

Meehl then concedes something that Platt's paper never quite faces. A theory never reaches an observation alone. It needs auxiliary theories about the instruments and conditions, and a failed prediction refutes only the bundle: theory, auxiliaries and conditions together. His example is a study of social fear and perception that recruits introverted adolescent boys by a personality scale and tries to frighten them with an attractive, hostile research assistant wearing Chanel Number Five. If nothing happens, was the theory wrong, or the scale, or the assistant's performance on the day? In physics, Meehl argues, the auxiliaries are usually well tested and often built from the same theory; in soft psychology they are as shaky as the theory itself. Platt's clean exclusion assumes the auxiliaries hold. Meehl is saying that in many fields they are the first thing to go.

## Chapter Seven: Calipers and the Logical Box

So what does Meehl want instead? Not more significance tests, and not none. He wants predictions that risk more. When a theory cannot yet give a number, it may still give an order: this group above that one, and the first two closer together than the second two. It may give a shape: the curve should rise, peak and fall, with three humps of increasing height. He points to the early history of quantum theory, when physicists could not derive the curve for radiation from a hot cavity but could predict that measurements at different temperatures, plotted the right way, would fall on one curve, and they did. He points to his own work on classifying patients into hidden types, where the mathematics yields several independent estimates of the same quantity, and the estimates must agree within tolerance if the model is right. He calls these consistency tests, and the paper reports ninety-four percent success for them in its tests of his classification model, with no false negatives.

His moral is a sentence of plain instruction: approximate agreement with a predicted point value, rank order or function form is always worth more than a precise probability that one thing merely differs from another. And he describes the physicist's power as a combination of rich mathematics and accurate instruments, which he pictures as an invisible hand holding a pair of fine calipers.

Here the constellation looks like a quarrel. Platt said the mathematical box was flimsy and the logical box strong. Meehl says the strong tests are the numerical ones. I think they are describing the same thing from two sides, and the benzene count shows how. Platt distrusted equations that fit loosely, the two-decimal-place agreement that many theories could produce. Meehl distrusted directional claims that any theory could produce. Both are saying that a test is worth what it risked. The count of three in benzene risked everything; nothing but the right structure gives three. Meehl's seven named days of April risk nearly as much. A significance star in the predicted direction risks a coin toss.

A passed test is evidence for an idea in proportion to how surprising the result would have been without it. Chamberlin's contribution is to keep the scientist from making the comparison dishonestly, by loving one idea so much that the rivals are never stated. Platt's is to insist the comparison be built into the experiment, so that the outcome splits the rivals. Meehl's is to point out that the comparison is empty unless the prediction forbids something a worthless theory would allow, and that the standard statistical ritual, in fields where everything is related to everything, forbids almost nothing.

Each of them also sees what the others miss. Chamberlin alone saw that in the messy sciences several hypotheses are often all partly true, and that the job is to apportion causes rather than to crown one. That is close to what Meehl's soft psychology needed: not a tree of exclusions but estimates of how much each cause contributes: effect sizes with their uncertainty. Platt alone saw the social machinery: that a method has to be practised daily, written in a notebook, and taught, and that grant committees could reward it. Meehl alone did the arithmetic.

## Chapter Eight: The Case Against

The admirers' case is easy to find. Platt's paper is handed to new graduate students in biology to this day. Douglas Fudge, writing on its fiftieth anniversary in the Journal of Experimental Biology, gives it to every new member of his lab, and counts more than thirteen hundred citations, many from psychology, ecology and business. Fudge is a good admirer, because he adds what Platt left out. When students ask where good hypotheses come from, he tells them, in his words, "the same place good poetry comes from," and admits that is a maddening way of saying he does not know.

The hardest case against Platt that I could reach is by William O'Donohue and Jeffrey Buchanan, in Behavior and Philosophy in 2001, which I have only from its abstract. It lists eight objections, and three of them cut. Platt never showed that the fast-moving fields used his method more often than the slow ones; he showed that some of their famous experiments could be described his way. His history is thin, and fuller accounts of the cases he cites, they argue, do not show the method in use. And no one can enumerate a complete set of alternatives, which is fatal to Platt's assumption that elimination leaves the truth; the deciding experiment, besides, refutes a bundle of theory and auxiliaries, the point Meehl himself conceded.

The objection about winners lands hardest, I think. Platt's evidence is a set of winners described after the fact. Molecular biology in the 1950s had the kind of problem his method suits: a small number of physically distinct mechanisms, an organism you could breed overnight, and outcomes, like the density bands in a centrifuge, that differed in kind. A field with those advantages would move fast under almost any method, and Platt's paper cannot separate the method from the problems it was used on. That is a Meehlian complaint against Platt: his evidence for strong inference is itself a weak test.

Against Meehl there are two serious objections. The first is that his argument applies with full force to studies of groups as they come, and less to randomised experiments, which he himself grants in 1967; in a true experiment, a manipulation with literally no effect is possible, and the null is not quite so reliably false. Meehl's reply was that brains are connected enough for almost any stimulus to shift arousal a little, and I find that persuasive for psychology. For a trial of a drug on mortality, it is less so. Plenty of trials find nothing, and some of those nothings are true.

The second objection came from Ronald Serlin and Daniel Lapsley in 1985, and I know it only through secondary accounts and the paper's abstract, which I could not open in full. Their answer to the paradox is to stop testing against exactly zero. A researcher specifies in advance a band of differences too small to matter, a good-enough belt in their phrase, and tests whether the result lies beyond it. Then more data does make the test harder, as in physics, because a trivially small true difference will be shown to be trivially small. Run it in the made-up numbers from earlier: with five thousand per group and a belt set at a fifth of a standard deviation, the worthless theory's true difference of one tenth fails the test essentially every time, and the real half-standard-deviation effect passes every time. The paradox is gone.

This is the objection I side with, and it is also a concession to Meehl rather than a defeat. The good-enough belt works by making the theory say something about size. That is the move Meehl asked for: a prediction that risks more than a direction. What Serlin and Lapsley showed is that you can get most of the way there without the full theory of calipers, by deciding beforehand what difference would be too small to count.

Meehl himself supplies the last objection to Meehl. Near the end of the 1967 paper he says the situation is, in a sense, nobody's fault, because forcing soft theories into point predictions before they are ready would be premature quantification. That is fair, and it limits his cure. The fields he described could not simply decide to predict seven days of rain. What they could do, and mostly did not, was stop counting asterisks as if they were evidence.

## Chapter Nine: The First Promise

What does any of this change in a statistical analysis plan? The plan has a section, usually near the top, headed something like objectives and hypotheses, and in most trials it contains a sentence of the form: we hypothesise that the intervention will improve the primary outcome compared with control. That sentence is a directional prediction, and by now its weakness should be clear.

For the intervention itself, the sentence is closer to potash than to Fisbee, and it is defensible. But even there, the sample size section is already doing what Serlin and Lapsley asked. A trialist chooses a minimal clinically important difference to power the study, and that number is a good-enough belt in everything but name. The weakness is that it lives in the sample size calculation and not in the hypothesis. The hypothesis section says better; the power calculation says better by at least this much. A plan that writes the second into the first turns a coin-flippable claim into a risky one, and it makes the interpretation of a small, significant, unimportant result a matter already decided before unblinding.

The secondary hypotheses are where Meehl bites hardest. They are often where the mechanism lives: the intervention improves the outcome through some pathway, and the pathway will show up as a change in an intermediate measure. Written as a list of directional predictions, each one a separate test with its own asterisks, they reproduce the Fisbee table, seven for and three against, with a discussion section that counts noses. Meehl's remedies translate directly. Predict an order: the intervention should move the intermediate measure more than the final outcome, or more in the patients who started worst. Predict a shape: if dose varies, the response should rise with it and then flatten. Predict a consistency: two independent routes to the same quantity should agree.

And Chamberlin's family belongs in the plan too, though almost no plan has it. For each claim about mechanism, the plan can name the rival explanations that would produce the same primary result, and for each rival, the pattern in the secondary outcomes it would predict. If every rival predicts the same pattern, the trial cannot distinguish them, and the plan should say so before anyone has a favourite.

Grant applications have the same disease in a stronger form. Specific aims are commonly written as hypotheses to be tested: that the intervention will improve some outcome compared with usual care. That is Platt's language, the vocabulary of hypotheses and tests, attached to Meehl's weakest kind of prediction. A reviewer who asked Platt's Question of such an aim, what result would disprove it, would usually hear: no significant difference. And as Meehl showed, a large enough study will find a difference whether the idea is good or not.

## Chapter Ten: Verdict

Read together, the three papers make a short course on evidence that most training programmes never give; each read alone misleads. Chamberlin alone is a lovely essay on temperament that never tells you how to decide. Platt alone is a recipe that works on problems shaped like benzene and fails silently on ones like personality, and his evidence that it works is a list of winners. Meehl alone is a demolition, careful and funny and correct, with a cure that most fields cannot yet take.

Where I side with the critics: Platt oversold, and O'Donohue and Buchanan are right that his history is a set of anecdotes. Meehl's argument is weaker for randomised trials than he allowed. And neither man said much about where the rival hypotheses come from, and that step decides everything after it.

Start with Meehl's 1978 paper, long but conversational; with only half an hour, take the pages from the weather ladder through the potash, where the argument lives. Platt's six pages take twenty minutes, and none of them can be spared. Read Chamberlin's twelve pages for the description of parental affection, and then the Great Lakes passage, the part of his paper that Platt left behind and that soft science needed. Of Meehl's 1967 paper, the colour-naming example and the rake are what the later lecture does not repeat.

Here is the sentence a plan usually carries: we hypothesise that the intervention will improve the primary outcome compared with control. Here is the one Meehl would sign: we predict a difference of at least the minimal important amount, in the stated direction, and we will treat a significant difference smaller than that as a failure of the hypothesis, not a partial success. And beneath it, a second: the rival explanation we take most seriously predicts the same primary result but the opposite ordering in two named secondary outcomes, and the trial will be read as favouring whichever ordering appears. A few more lines in the hypothesis section, and the trial has something it can lose.

Next time, Gosset against Fisher, and the Lanarkshire milk experiment, in which twenty thousand schoolchildren were fed milk and their teachers were allowed to rebalance the groups by eye. Thanks for listening.
