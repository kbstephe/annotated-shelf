## Chapter One: A Sentence in a Grant Review

Not long before 2010 a reviewer's critique of an application contained this sentence, and Peter Bacchetti, a biostatistician at the University of California, San Francisco, copied it into a paper because it was so ordinary: it is unclear if the study will be sufficiently powered to allow the proposed analyses. There is no number in it. It does not say what power the reviewer had in mind, which effect, or what would happen to the analyses if the power were somewhat lower. It simply presumes that below some line the study is ruined, and that the applicant, not the reviewer, must prove which side of it the proposal falls on.

Bacchetti opened that paper, published in BMC Medicine in 2010, with a memory from early in his career. An epidemiologist told him that dealing with sample size was the price one pays for being a biostatistician. He spent, he writes, untold time paying it, and came to believe that the effort produced no real scientific benefit.

The price has a familiar shape. Choose a difference you hope to detect, guess the outcome's standard deviation or event rate, fix the false-positive rate at five percent, and solve for the number of patients that gives an eighty percent chance of a significant result if the hoped-for difference is real. A study with fewer patients is called underpowered, and a study that is underpowered is, in a long tradition, called unethical. Douglas Altman, in a much-cited series in the BMJ in 1980, wrote that a study too small to detect clinically important effects may be scientifically useless, and hence unethical in its use of subjects and resources.

That sentence makes two charges at once. One is about knowledge: a small study cannot tell you what you need to know. The other is about people: because it cannot, the patients who joined it were put at risk for nothing. Three answers to those charges are this episode's subject. Scott Halpern, Jason Karlawish and Jesse Berlin, at the University of Pennsylvania, restated the ethical charge in JAMA in 2002, in a paper titled The continuing unethical conduct of underpowered clinical trials. Bacchetti and three colleagues answered it in the American Journal of Epidemiology in 2005, arguing that the charge fails even on its own terms. And in 2014 Andrew Gelman, at Columbia, and John Carlin, in Melbourne, made a different charge against small studies, which neither side of the ethics argument had quite made: a small study that does reach significance is likely to be wrong about how large the effect is, and sometimes about which way it points.

The three papers are usually cited on the same side or on no side at all. Put in one room, they argue with each other, and between them they decide what the sample size section of an analysis plan is for. That section is the most ritual paragraph in a protocol, and the most argued over in review. The question running through the episode is whether it can be made honest.

## Chapter Two: The Case for the Line

Start with the strongest version of the case against small trials, which is Halpern's, since it is the one most trialists already half-believe.

The setting of the 2002 paper is a counterattack. In 1997 Sarah Edwards, Richard Lilford and colleagues had argued in the Lancet that underpowered trials are not necessarily unethical. Their reasons were two. Small trials can be combined in a meta-analysis, so a trial too small to settle anything by itself can still contribute to a pooled answer. And even a trial that cannot test a hypothesis well gives an estimate with a confidence interval, and imprecise results are better than none. The old charge, they said, had been made untenable by the rising acceptance of evidence amalgamated from many studies.

Halpern, Karlawish and Berlin took both arguments seriously and found each wanting in practice. A meta-analysis can rescue a small trial only if the trials are comparable, using similar methods, populations and outcomes, and then only when the small ones reach print. Small trials with null results are the ones most likely to stay in a drawer, so the pooled answer is drawn from a biased sample, and the bias is worst exactly where the rescue was supposed to apply. As for the estimate and its interval, they wrote that narrowing a confidence interval to a width still compatible with both benefit and harm generally does not justify exposing people to the common risks and burdens of research. Their summary of the effect of a small sample on an ethics board's arithmetic was that it shifts the risk-benefit calculus in an unfavourable direction.

The paper does not condemn every small trial. It allows two exceptions. A trial in a rare disease may be underpowered if its investigators document explicit plans to include its results in a prospective meta-analysis with similar trials. And early-phase trials of drugs or devices may lack power for a comparison between arms, provided they are adequately powered for whatever else they set out to do. In both cases participants must be told that their participation may help future patients only indirectly.

That last condition holds the ethical core of the argument. People volunteer for trials, very often, to help someone else. A volunteer told that the trial will answer the question, when it almost certainly will not, has been misled about the one benefit on offer. Two of its three authors were physician-ethicists, and the paper reads as a defence of the volunteer's informed consent as much as a rule about arithmetic.

So the steelman runs like this. A trial's justification is that what it learns outweighs what it costs its participants. Below some size it learns too little to pay that cost, either because its result will be ambiguous or because the systems that might combine it with others are unreliable. Above that size, the ledger balances. The eighty percent convention marks, roughly, where the ledger starts to balance. Everyone knows the marker is rough, but a rough line is better than none, and an ethics committee needs a line.

The steelman has a hidden premise, and it sits in the phrase "below some size." The argument needs the value of a trial to rise steeply around a threshold, so that a trial a little short of it is worth much less than one a little past it. If value climbed smoothly and flattened as trials grew, there would be no size below which the ledger turns red that is not also a size where every extra patient is worth less than the last. The next question is what shape the curve actually has.

## Chapter Three: Value Per Volunteer

Bacchetti's answer, worked out first with Leslie Wolf, Mark Segal and Charles McCulloch in 2005 and generalised in Biometrics in 2008, is that the curve has the shape of nearly every curve in statistics where precision is concerned. It rises steeply at first and then flattens. Each added patient buys less than the one before. Power itself behaves this way once you are past the first few patients; so do the expected width of a confidence interval, the expected information about a parameter, and the expected value of a decision made with the result. The 2008 paper checked a long list of such measures, Bayesian and frequentist, and found the same concave shape in every one.

From that shape the ethical argument follows in two lines. Suppose the burden on each participant is roughly the same whatever the trial's size, so that total burden grows in proportion to the number enrolled. Suppose the trial's value grows more slowly than that, as the curve says it does. Then the ratio of value to burden is highest for small trials and falls as the trial grows. If a large trial is ethically acceptable because its value exceeds its burden, an otherwise identical smaller one is acceptable too, since its ratio is better. The 2005 paper put it as a concession wrapped around a conclusion. Even granting the most hostile premise, that a study's only value is its power, with nothing from estimates, intervals or meta-analyses, the balance between value and burden does not improve as the sample grows. The argument for condemning small studies, they wrote, fails on its own terms.

The 2010 paper turned this into a picture. If you halve a planned sample, you keep more than half of the study's projected value, every time, because the curve is concave. The image the convention runs on, a cliff at eighty percent power, is what Bacchetti calls the threshold myth. Cutting below the line is a loss, sometimes a large one, but never the drop from adequate to worthless that the language of reviewers assumes.

Then he lists what the myth costs. Two of the harms are about measurement. Sample size formulas depend steeply on inputs nobody knows well. Double the assumed standard deviation and the required sample quadruples; halve the difference you hope to detect and the same thing happens. Bacchetti reports one study of trials in four leading general medical journals, where the standard deviation assumed at the planning stage could be compared with the one observed: about a quarter of the trials had sample sizes off by more than fivefold, and over half were wrong at least twofold. A line that moves by a factor of five when one guess changes is not much of a line.

The remaining harms are about people, and they are what make the paper read like a complaint from inside the room. Because cost and feasibility always shape how many patients a trial enrols, and the convention allows only power as a reason, investigators reverse-engineer the inputs until the formula returns the number they could afford. The trade has names in the literature, the sample size game and, less politely, the samba, and one published dramatisation ends with the statistician asking the client where they would like to start fudging. Because any power calculation can be questioned by nudging its inputs, any reviewer who dislikes a proposal for reasons he cannot articulate can always fault the numbers. The study section's noise that Elizabeth Pier and colleagues measured in their mock review panels, reviewers who agree on almost nothing near the payline, has here a ready-made vocabulary. And because the inputs are least knowable for new ideas, the convention falls hardest on the most novel proposals.

The harm Bacchetti spends longest on is the one he found in most of the draft manuscripts he reviewed. A trial powered at eighty percent seems to promise a definitive answer. When the result comes back with a p-value above 0.05, the promise is cashed the wrong way: the trial was adequate, it was not positive, so it must be negative. His example is a large trial of vitamins C and E in pregnancy, with about nine hundred and thirty-five women per arm. Its estimate implied one serious adverse outcome prevented for every thirty-nine women treated. The p-value was 0.07. The authors concluded that supplementation does not reduce the rate. Bacchetti places that result in a table he proposes for every grant application: three rows for the precision you might achieve, which for a yes-or-no outcome depends on how common the outcome proves among controls, three columns for an effect as large as hoped, half as large, or none, and in each cell the estimate and confidence interval that would appear in the paper. By his reading, the vitamin trial landed close to the corner where outcomes among controls were commoner than expected, so precision was lower, and the effect was as large as hoped. In that cell his table shows a reduction of 2.8 percentage points, with an interval from 5.6 points of benefit to a hair on the other side of zero. For a cheap, safe supplement and a grave outcome, he argues, that is a very encouraging result. Read through the threshold, it was a null.

The ethics argument then took a turn. Ross Prentice, the Seattle statistician, wrote an invited commentary in the same issue of the journal. Bacchetti's group had included a hypothetical example, with burden assumed deliberately high, in which a trial sized by the convention would be unacceptable and only trials below about a hundred and thirty participants, with power no higher than about half, would pass. Prentice found the implication absurd. On their arguments, he wrote, only trials with power below fifty-two percent would be ethically defensible, and he ended the sentence with an exclamation mark. He proposed a different shape for value per participant: low for a trial too small to change practice, roughly flat over the range of sizes that could, and falling only beyond the point where the question is settled. His reason was altruism. A participant's satisfaction in contributing to a definitive study of an important question, he argued, does not shrink because others contribute too.

Halpern, Karlawish and Berlin made a version of the same point in a letter later that year, adding that Bacchetti had got the arithmetic of social benefit wrong. Each participant's altruistic reward, they said, is paid in full if a larger trial proves the treatment and paid not at all if a small trial cannot tell.

Bacchetti's rejoinder settles the ethical half, I think, in his favour. Altruistic satisfaction cannot be counted as part of the value that justifies the trial, because participants are entitled to assume that someone has already judged the trial worth its burden before inviting them in. If the trial fails that test, no amount of satisfaction can rescue it; the satisfaction would be produced, as his group put it, under false pretences. And the arithmetic was never a quotient over the population that benefits. It was total value against total burden, divided through by the number of participants on both sides, which leaves the inequality unchanged. At least four eminent statisticians, a later account of the dispute noted with some weariness, seemed to doubt that dividing both sides of an inequality by the same positive number leaves it true.

At the end of the ethical exchange, then, the threshold has lost. There is no size below which a trial stops being worth its burden that is not set by assumptions about cost and value which an ethics committee could examine directly. But the epistemic charge, that small studies tell us wrong things, is untouched by any of this. Bacchetti's curve measures what a study is expected to be worth if its result is read sensibly. Whether results are read sensibly is a different question, and it is the one Gelman and Carlin asked.

## Chapter Four: Wrong Sign, Wrong Size

Their paper, in Perspectives on Psychological Science, starts at the moment of triumph. You have just finished running an experiment and you find a significant effect. How much should you trust it?

Power, they point out, describes only the probability of passing the test. It says nothing about what a passed test tells you. So they define two quantities for the studies that pass. A Type S error, S for sign, is a significant estimate pointing the wrong way. A Type M error, M for magnitude, is a passing result far larger than the truth, and they measure it with the exaggeration ratio: among all the replications of a design that would come out significant, the average size of the estimate divided by the true effect.

The arithmetic takes three ingredients. A true effect size, which you must supply from outside the study, from the literature or from what is physically plausible. A standard error, which the design determines. And the significance threshold. From these, the sampling distribution of the estimate does the rest. Gelman and Carlin give the anchor point. A study with eighty percent power has its true effect about 2.8 standard errors from zero. Its significant results overstate the truth by about twelve percent on average, and the chance of a wrong sign is around one in a million. At that power the significance filter is nearly harmless. As power falls below about a half, the exaggeration ratio begins to climb steeply, and below about one in ten the wrong-sign rate becomes serious.

Their first example is one Gelman had been arguing about for years. In 2007 a psychologist, Satoshi Kanazawa, reported from a national survey of about three thousand adolescents followed into adulthood that parents rated most attractive had more daughters: fifty-six percent of their first-born children were girls, against forty-eight percent in everyone else. The eight-point difference had a p-value of 0.015, significant by the usual rule, with a standard error that works out to 3.3 points.

Now consider what is plausible. The sex ratio of human births has a large literature. Factors such as parental age, race, birth order and season shift the proportion of girls by between less than a third of a point and about two points; famine and deep poverty, the largest effects known, by up to three. Gelman and Carlin judged that any real effect of parental attractiveness, measured on a noisy one-to-five rating, would be well under one percentage point. They ran the design analysis at a tenth of a point, three tenths and a full point.

At a full percentage point, generous by their account, the study had a power of about six percent. A significant result from it would point backwards about one time in five, and on average would overstate the real difference eightfold. At three tenths of a point, the wrong-sign rate was roughly forty percent and the expected exaggeration twenty-five-fold. At one tenth, a significant result was barely more likely to land on the right side of zero than the wrong one, and would overstate the effect seventy-seven times. To study a real difference of a tenth of a point with a standard error small enough to detect it, the authors estimate, would take more than a million births.

The example has a sting in its tail. If the author had been asked for a power calculation after the fact, using his own estimate of eight points as the effect, the study would have looked respectably powered. The weakness of the design produces a large estimate, the large estimate makes the design look strong, and conventional retrospective power closes the circle. The only exit is an effect size from outside the data. Gelman and Carlin's alternative question for any design is short: what might be expected to happen in studies of this size?

None of this contradicts Bacchetti. His curve is about the expected value of a study's whole output, estimate and interval read together. Gelman and Carlin are describing what happens to that output when only its significant part is kept and believed. Their own example of the problem in medicine is the decline effect, the tendency for reported effects to shrink as later studies come in, which they suspect is largely the significance filter at work. The filter is the mechanism. A literature, a journal, a reader or an investigator who keeps results that pass and discards the rest is selecting the overestimates. Small studies simply give the filter more to select from.

That suggests where the two charges against small trials meet. It is in a place every applicant for a large grant has been: the pilot study.

## Chapter Five: The Pilot and the Big Grant

The following pipeline is invented, and the numbers are computed only to show the arithmetic; they describe no real programme. A team believes a new intervention improves a continuous outcome. In truth it does, by 0.3 standard deviations, a modest effect of the size many behavioural and nutritional interventions achieve when they work at all. The team cannot know that number. They run a pilot with thirty participants per arm, which is about what a career-development award or a small foundation grant pays for, and plan to use its estimate to size a full trial for a large grant.

Begin with the pilot's chance of detecting it. With thirty per arm, the standard error of the difference is about a quarter of a standard deviation, so the real effect sits a little over one standard error from zero. The pilot will reach significance about one time in five.

Now suppose, as often happens in practice even when nobody writes it down, that the team proceeds to the big application only if the pilot looks good. Take the strictest version first: they proceed only on a significant pilot. Among those pilots the exaggeration ratio is a little over two. The typical significant pilot reports an effect of about 0.6 standard deviations, double the truth. The team plugs that into the usual formula, with the customary power and significance level, and asks for about forty participants per arm. The trial they get funded has a true power not of eighty percent but of about twenty-seven, and none of the trials sized this way reaches even fifty percent. The trial that should have been run, sized on the real 0.3, needs about a hundred and seventy-five per arm.

A gentler rule changes the proportions without changing the direction. Suppose the team proceeds whenever the pilot's estimate is at least as large as what they hoped, 0.3 or better, significant or not. That happens about half the time. The median estimate among those pilots is a little under a half standard deviation, the median planned trial about seventy per arm, and its actual power, at the median, around forty-three percent. Even a team that goes ahead on any positive pilot, which happens in nearly nine of ten tries, ends up with trials whose typical real power is near seventy percent, because the pilots it discards are the ones that would have pulled the average estimate down.

Every step of that pipeline is locally reasonable. The pilot is small because pilots are cheap. The decision to proceed depends on promise because funders want promise. The power formula uses the best available estimate of the effect, the team's own. And the whole procedure ends with a trial that is eighty percent powered on its own paperwork and has, in fact, a coin-flip chance or worse of detecting the effect it was built for. When it fails, the threshold reading Bacchetti complained of finishes the job: an adequately powered trial found nothing, so the intervention does not work.

Run the same story through each of the three papers and each identifies a different villain. For Halpern, the villain is the underpowered definitive trial: forty per arm was never enough, and its participants were told they were answering a question they could not answer. For Bacchetti, the villain is the eighty percent ritual: the team spent effort computing a number with a fivefold uncertainty and then believed it. For Gelman and Carlin, the villain is the effect size borrowed from the pilot and filtered by the decision to proceed, which guaranteed it was too large.

All three are right about their own villain. But only one of the villains acted at the moment the error entered. The error came in when a filtered estimate was treated as an unfiltered one.

## Chapter Six: The Filter, Not the Size

Lay the two charges against small trials side by side and ask what each actually needs.

The ethical charge, in Halpern's form, needs the threshold: a size below which value collapses. Bacchetti showed that the collapse does not happen for a study whose estimate and interval are reported and read, whatever they turn out to be. What remains of Halpern's argument, once the threshold is gone, is everything he said about the downstream world. Small negative trials go unpublished. Meta-analyses drawn from published trials are biased. Investigators read p above 0.05 as proof of nothing. Each of those is a filter applied after the trial is done.

The epistemic charge, in Gelman and Carlin's form, needs the filter explicitly. Their exaggeration ratio is defined conditional on significance. An unfiltered estimate from a small trial is unbiased; it is noisy, and its interval says so. The overstatement appears only when someone keeps the significant estimates and believes them at face value. Small samples raise the stakes of that habit. They do not create it.

So the two halves of the case against small trials, the charge made for the volunteers and the one made for the truth, reduce to one claim. A small study does harm when its result passes through a filter that keeps the flattering outcomes and discards the rest, and is then believed as though it had not been filtered. Without the filter, it is a noisy contribution whose noise is visible. With it, the same study becomes a machine for producing confident overestimates, and a larger trial is a less efficient version of the same machine.

This is not a novel point among statisticians, and none of the three papers would disown it. But it changes what each of their prescriptions is for. Halpern's two exceptions, the rare disease with a pooling plan and the early-phase trial, turn out on this reading to be filter controls. A prospective meta-analysis fixes in advance which trials will be pooled, so the pool cannot be chosen by results; a public wiki page on the dispute, last revised by Bacchetti, makes the related point that a review restricted to prospectively registered trials has no selection bias whatever else is missing. Bacchetti's table of nine possible results, filled in before the trial, is a filter control too: it commits the investigators to reading the corner they land in as a result, not as a failure. And Gelman and Carlin's insistence on an effect size from outside the study is a refusal to let the filtered pilot estimate into the design.

The convention Bacchetti distrusted, and Gelman and Carlin found insufficient, can be seen in the same light. At a real eighty percent, the significance filter costs about twelve percent of exaggeration and essentially no wrong signs, a defence of the convention that neither its defenders nor its critics made. A trial that truly has eighty percent power can be filtered without much damage, which is one reason a field that filters heavily, as most fields do, might reasonably want its trials large. The trouble is the word "truly." The power is computed from a guessed effect, and if the guess came from a filtered source, the eighty percent is a number on paper. The pilot pipeline produced a trial that was eighty percent powered on paper and twenty-seven percent powered in fact.

That reconciles more than it decides. A field that controlled the filter completely would have no epistemic case against small trials and, by Bacchetti's arithmetic, no ethical one either. No field does. The real disagreement between Halpern's side and Bacchetti's is over whether to design trials for the field as it should behave or for the field as it does. Halpern designs for the field that exists, and wants trials big enough to survive its habits. Bacchetti designs for the field that could exist, and wants the habits fixed rather than the volunteers multiplied. The red die from two episodes ago is a fair comparison here. Senn said a randomised trial must be analysed the way it was randomised, conditioning on what you can see. Gelman and Carlin are saying that a significant result must be interpreted the way it was selected.

## Chapter Seven: The Case Against Everyone

If the filter is the villain, why should an ethics committee not simply approve small trials and police the filter instead? Each position has a weakness that bears on that answer.

Bacchetti's arithmetic assumes the burden per participant is the same whatever the trial's size, and that the trial's value is the only thing being weighed. For many trials that is fair. For a trial whose purpose is a regulatory decision or a change in guidelines, the value curve is not smooth; Richard Simon, in the discussion of the 2008 paper, observed that late-phase trials usually carry a strong interest in a definitive result. A guideline panel or a drug agency does treat a result near a threshold differently from one comfortably past it, and the panel's habits are part of the world the trial has to work in. Bacchetti answers that no sample size can guarantee a definitive answer, since even a huge trial can land in the grey zone between an effect too small to matter and one large enough to act on. True, but beside the reply: a larger trial lands there less often.

A second weakness is the freedom Bacchetti offers. He proposes that a sample size can be justified on cost alone: the size that minimises the total cost per participant, or a smaller one for innovative work, either of which his group proved more cost-efficient than any larger size. That is an honest reason, and he is right that hiding it behind invented power assumptions erodes integrity at the start of a project. But a convention also protects. A required power calculation forces an investigator to name a primary outcome, a hoped-for effect and a plausible variance before the money arrives, which is a modest guard against the forking paths of the analysis that comes later. Bacchetti's answer, that these can be stated directly without a power calculation, is correct in principle. In practice a section that must produce a number gets written; a section that may produce reasoning sometimes does not.

On the ethical side, his strongest critics brought what that wiki page calls associated characteristics. Katherine Button and colleagues, writing in neuroscience in 2013, argued that biases have been documented to be far more common in very small studies than in larger ones, so low power has an ethical dimension because unreliable research is wasteful. The wiki's reply compared condemning a small study for the sins of other small studies to collective punishment, and drew an analogy to banning men from city streets because men commit more violent crime. That is a fair point about fairness to individual investigators. It is weaker as policy. An ethics committee cannot audit every investigator's character, and size is a crude but observable proxy for the habits it cannot see.

Gelman and Carlin's method has the opposite weakness. Everything in it depends on the true effect size you supply, and they say plainly that any such assumption is just that, an assumption. Choose a small enough hypothetical effect and any study looks hopeless; the design analysis becomes a way for a sceptic to condemn whatever result he dislikes. The sample size samba Bacchetti described, nudging inputs until the number comes out right, has a mirror image here, nudging the assumed effect down until a significant finding dissolves. Error statisticians in the line of Deborah Mayo have raised a version of this, that the plausible effect amounts to a prior admitted through the back door of a frequentist method; I could not retrieve her writing on this for the episode, so I give the objection in my own words. Gelman and Carlin's reply is that a rough external guess is still better than the filtered internal one, and that the exercise forces a quantitative claim the literature can test. On the sex ratio example, where a century of demography constrains the guess, that reply is overwhelming. On a novel intervention with no precedent, it is thinner.

And Halpern's position has the weakness Bacchetti found: it depends on a threshold that does not exist. But it has a strength the duel tends to hide. His paper was, before anything else, about what volunteers are told. A trial that asks for consent by promising to answer a question, when on any honest calculation it has a modest chance of doing so, has misdescribed itself. That objection survives the loss of the threshold intact, because it concerns the honesty of the description, not the size of the sample. Bacchetti and Halpern agree on more than their exchange suggests. Both want the reason for a sample size stated truthfully. They disagree about which truth an ethics committee should require.

## Chapter Eight: What the Section Should Say

So what does all this change in an analysis plan?

The sample size section of a statistical analysis plan, and the matching paragraph of the grant, usually does one thing: it reports the power for a single hypothesised effect. On the reading this episode has built, it should do three.

It should say where the hypothesised effect came from, and the answer should not be the investigators' own pilot unless the pilot was going to be reported whatever it showed and its estimate has been shrunk toward the external evidence. Gelman and Carlin's language is useful in that paragraph: the effect is a plausible value from outside the data, and a range of such values, not a single one chosen because it gives the number required.

It should show what the paper would report across that range, in the form Bacchetti proposed: the estimate and confidence interval the trial would produce at the hoped-for effect, at half of it and at none, under optimistic and pessimistic assumptions about the variance. That table, unlike a power figure, is what the eventual paper will contain, and writing it in advance commits the authors to reading whichever cell they land in as a result rather than as a failure.

And it should say the real reason for the number. If the trial has a hundred and twenty per arm because that is what three years of recruitment at two sites can deliver, the plan can say so and then show what that number can learn. The power calculation can stay, since reviewers expect it, but as one row of the honest account rather than its disguise.

The verdict on the duel follows from Chapter Six. Bacchetti wins the argument about ethics: no sample size line separates acceptable from unacceptable trials, and the case for one rests on a myth about the shape of value. Gelman and Carlin win on evidence: small studies read through a significance filter produce confident overestimates, and the pilot pipeline is that problem in its most expensive form. Halpern keeps consent. The common enemy of all three is the filter, and the filter is downstream of the trial, in decisions about what to publish, what to proceed with and what to believe, which are decisions a trialist mostly controls.

For reading: Bacchetti's 2010 paper is about five thousand words and states one side whole; the section on harms is the part a grant writer will recognise. Gelman and Carlin's paper is eleven pages, and its two worked examples, the sex ratio and a study of menstrual cycle and voting, can be read in twenty minutes. Halpern, Karlawish and Berlin is behind a paywall, and much of it reaches a reader without access only through its opponents' quotations; read its abstract and its two exceptions, then Prentice's two-page commentary if you can reach it.

A pilot protocol can close the invented pipeline with a single paragraph, written before the first participant is enrolled. It names the effect size the full trial will be sized on, taken from the external literature, and states that the pilot's own estimate will not raise it. Let the pilot do what pilots do well: measure recruitment, retention, the variance of the outcome, whether the forms get filled in. Then report its effect estimate in full, whatever it is, with its interval. If the pilot's estimate later comes in at double the external figure, the team has not lost anything; it has an early warning that it is looking at a filtered number. And when the full trial is planned, the forty-per-arm design that the pilot would have tempted them into is simply not available.
