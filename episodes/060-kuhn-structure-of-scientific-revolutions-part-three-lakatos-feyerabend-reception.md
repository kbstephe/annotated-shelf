## Chapter One: A Planet Nobody Has Seen

Imre Lakatos tells a story in his 1970 essay, and it opens with a small planet that misbehaves. A physicist of the period before Einstein takes Newton's laws, feeds in the accepted starting conditions, and calculates the path of a newly found planet. The planet wanders off the calculated path. The physicist does not conclude that Newton is wrong. He supposes a second, unseen planet pulling on the first, works out its mass and orbit, and asks an experimental astronomer to look. The planet is too small for any existing telescope, so the astronomer applies for a research grant to build a bigger one. Three years later the telescope is ready and the planet is not there.

The physicist then proposes a cloud of cosmic dust hiding it, calculates the cloud, and applies for a grant to launch a satellite. The satellite finds no cloud. A magnetic field, then, in that region of space, which disturbed the satellite's instruments. A second satellite goes up, and finds none. Lakatos ends the story with an ingenious further hypothesis, or else with the whole affair buried in the back volumes of the journals and never mentioned again. He calls the case imaginary, and so should I. Everything in it, grants included, is his invention.

Hold on to the tale, because it is a question dressed as an anecdote. At which round did the physicist stop being a scientist and start being a man defending a belief? After the telescope? After the first satellite? Every request is reasonable taken alone, and each one is a prediction that could have failed and did. Whoever reads the second grant application sees a competent person with a testable plan. Whoever reads all three in a row sees something else. And a reviewer who must decide on the third has to answer a question that the stack of three does not contain: whether a fourth round is what it looks like.

History furnishes a real version, with the same man on both sides of it. In eighteen forty-six Urbain Le Verrier, working at his desk in Paris, used the pull of an unseen body on Uranus to compute where to point a telescope, and Johann Galle at Berlin found Neptune in the neighborhood of the place he named, within the first night of looking. Newtonian mechanics had looked in the dark and produced a planet. Le Verrier then turned to Mercury. By eighteen fifty-nine, after a fuller analysis of meridian observations and fourteen transits, he had a residue in the motion of Mercury's perihelion, the point of closest approach to the Sun, which crept forward about forty-three seconds of arc per century more than Newton's law allowed. He applied the same method. In December of that year he got a letter from a country doctor and amateur astronomer named Lescarbault, who reported seeing a small round spot cross the Sun that March. Le Verrier traveled to see him, was satisfied, and announced a planet inside Mercury's orbit on the second of January, eighteen sixty. He called it Vulcan. Its computed year was a little under twenty days.

Other observers contested it from the start, and claimed sightings followed anyway. In the eclipse of July eighteen seventy-eight two serious astronomers, James Craig Watson and Lewis Swift, each reported intra-Mercurial objects, and their reports did not agree with each other or with the known stars. Searches at later eclipses went on into the nineteen hundreds. In nineteen eight, Campbell and Perrine at the Lick Observatory, after photographic work in three eclipse years, judged the question closed. Seven years after that, Einstein's general relativity produced the forty-three seconds of arc per century from the curvature of space around the Sun, with no planet and no adjustable constant.

So here are two cases with one method, one author, and one set of mathematics. In the first, the unseen body was there. In the second it was not, and the anomaly belonged to a theory of gravitation that did not yet exist. Nothing available in eighteen forty-six or eighteen sixty told them apart. The shape of Lakatos's story is the shape of both. This episode is about what, if anything, a person deciding in the present tense could know that would sort the Neptune from the Vulcan, and about what the people who argued over Kuhn's book in the nineteen sixties thought the answer was.

## Chapter Two: A Colloquium That Did Not Go to Plan

Episode fifty-eight followed the first half of Kuhn's book, and episode fifty-nine the second. The part left over is the argument that followed. It happened at Bedford College in Regent's Park, in London, in July of nineteen sixty-five. The colloquium ran from the eleventh to the seventeenth, and on the thirteenth there was a symposium on criticism and the growth of knowledge, with Kuhn, Paul Feyerabend and Lakatos as the main speakers. Karl Popper took the chair.

The volume that came out five years later is candid about how little it resembles the day. The editors say in the preface that it is a rational reconstruction and not a report of the discussion. Feyerabend's and Lakatos's papers arrived after the colloquium, and John Watkins stepped in to speak against normal science in their place. The book that carries the debate is a set of papers written by people who had already heard each other, cast as a conversation that never took place in that order. Kuhn's first paper opens it, the replies follow, and Kuhn's answer to all of them closes it.

The line of attack has two prongs. Popper's essay, covered in episode fifty-eight, was one. The other came from the pair who stood nearest to Popper's tradition and the farthest from Kuhn's description of it: Lakatos, a Hungarian émigré in London who had spent his career trying to rescue Popper's account of science from its own problems, and Feyerabend, who had been at Berkeley when Kuhn was there in nineteen sixty and sixty-one. They disagreed with Kuhn from opposite directions. Lakatos held that Kuhn had removed any grounds for calling a scientific choice rational. Feyerabend took the book to describe a way of working that would suit the worst of scientists, and to mistake that description for an ideal.

Kuhn's reply follows the order of their charges, so it helps to take them in that order. Lakatos goes first.

## Chapter Three: Mob Psychology and the Machine That Answers It

Lakatos's essay is long, over a hundred pages in the volume, and the charge against Kuhn takes up one paragraph of it. The paragraph runs through Kuhn's picture as Lakatos reads it. Anomalies abound in science at all times, he says, and in the normal periods a dominant paradigm holds. Then a crisis arrives, and for Lakatos a crisis is a psychological concept, a contagious panic. A new paradigm emerges that cannot be compared to the old one by any standard they share, since each paradigm brings its own. The change is a bandwagon effect. His summary is that in Kuhn's view scientific revolution is a matter for mob psychology.

The charge is of a particular sort. Lakatos does not deny Kuhn's history. He accepts that anomalies are everywhere, that scientists cling to theories, and that falsification of the simple kind, where one clash with an observation sinks a theory, does not describe how any serious theory has died. His own imaginary planet story is an argument for that. The disagreement is about whether a philosopher is entitled to say more. If a theory is never killed by a single observation, Kuhn takes it that the process of choice is a matter for the sociologist, and Lakatos wants to keep a normative account in which one can say that a choice was a good one.

His machine for doing so is the methodology of scientific research programmes, and its parts are few. A programme has a hard core, a set of claims that its defenders have decided not to give up whatever happens: for Newton, the three laws of motion and the law of gravitation. Around the core is a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, the ones that take the knocks. When the planet misbehaves, the belt gets a new member, the unseen body. A rule tells the working scientist what not to do, which is to aim the refutation at the core. The same programme carries a positive heuristic, a plan of attack for which problems to solve in which order. The Newtonians began with a point-sized sun and one point-sized planet, then added more planets, then planets that were not points, then forces between planets, and the order was set by the programme and not by whatever anomaly was newest.

The test comes next. A programme is progressive if each change to the belt predicts something new, and at least now and then some of those predictions come true. It is degenerating if the belt only absorbs facts already known. Lakatos's sense of the word new is sharp: a novel fact is one that was improbable or even forbidden in the light of what was known before. An adjustment that merely rescues the theory from an observation that has just come in is the mark of decay, whatever it looks like on the page.

He runs the test on two cases, and the first gets its whole length here, because it is the one that makes the test bite. In eighteen fifteen an Edinburgh physician, William Prout, published an anonymous paper claiming that the atomic weights of the elements were whole numbers, which suggested that every atom was built from hydrogen. The measurements disagreed. Chlorine came out at about thirty-five and a half. Prout's answer, in Lakatos's reconstruction, was that chemical substances as they come are impure, so the instruments that produced the figures were unreliable, and his followers spent decades improving the purification of elements. Lakatos adds in a footnote that this is a rational reconstruction and not the history: Prout himself denied that there were any anomalies and gave chlorine as exactly thirty-six.

By eighteen sixty the Belgian chemist Stas, after meticulous work, declared the hypothesis without foundation. Marignac replied that Stas's experiments might be exact and the differences might still come from imperfect methods. In eighteen eighty-six William Crookes proposed that the elements as found were mixtures, and invented a theory of fractionation to sort them. His theories were bold and, as Lakatos puts it, false, and they predicted nothing. Maxwell argued from physics that two kinds of hydrogen could not exist, since they could be separated and nobody could separate them. The programme looked finished. After nineteen ten, Rutherford's group and Soddy found that two samples of the same chemical element can differ in weight and cannot be separated by chemistry. Prout was right in a form nobody had imagined. Lakatos's verdict is that at no point was there a rational reason to eliminate the programme.

Read that against the planet Vulcan. The Proutians, by the standard of the test, were degenerating for decades at a time. Crookes's theories made no predictions that came true. And the programme turned out to be right. The Vulcan hunters, by the same standard, had a belt that predicted a planet, and the planet was not there. Both look the same from the inside for most of their history. The test cannot tell them apart in the interval that matters.

Lakatos knew it, and says so in a section he calls the end of instant rationality. Decisive experiments, he writes, are seen as decisive only decades later. The anomaly in Mercury's perihelion had been an unsolved difficulty in Newton's programme for years, and it became a brilliant refutation only because Einstein's theory explained it better. Young's double slit of eighteen hundred and two did not look like a decisive one until Fresnel built the wave theory into something that could not be matched. His phrase for it is that the owl of Minerva flies at dusk: rationality works much more slowly than most people think, and it works fallibly.

The demand he places on a programme is therefore weaker than the one the imaginary physicist would need. The belt must be consistently content-increasing, each step proposing something that goes beyond what was known, and every so often the increase must be confirmed. He calls that intermittent progress, and says it leaves plenty of rational room for dogged adherence in the face of refutations. What the test forbids is staying faithful once a programme stops anticipating anything. It does not say when to leave.

A last feature will matter in the final chapter. Lakatos does not want the monopoly that Kuhn calls normal science. For him a monopoly is a pathology, and he says on this point that theoretical pluralism is better than monism, that Popper and Feyerabend are right and Kuhn wrong. His histories are histories of rival programmes. That is the second move he makes against Kuhn, after the charge of irrationality, and it is the one Feyerabend pushes furthest.

## Chapter Four: The Reply, and the Argument About Risk

Kuhn's answer comes in the last chapter of the volume, and he begins by saying that the charges about irrationality, mob rule and relativism are ones for which his own earlier rhetoric is probably partly responsible. He quotes himself: the passage about conversion experiences, lifelong resistance not being a violation of scientific standards, and the historian's inability to find a point at which resistance by someone like Priestley becomes illogical. He grants that one could read that as might making right. Then he says what he meant.

In a debate over theory choice, neither party has access to an argument that works like a proof. A proof works because the premises and the rules of inference are stipulated beforehand, so a disagreement can be traced step by step to one person's violation of a prior rule. In a theory dispute, the two sides differ instead about the meaning or application of the rules, and so the retracing never reaches a place where one of them must concede. Their recourse is persuasion, which he insists is not the absence of reasons. The reasons are the ones philosophers of science have always listed. He names accuracy, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness, and says scientists must be taught to value them and shown examples of them in use.

What he denies is that the reasons are rules. They are values, and values come into conflict. A later essay of his, from nineteen seventy-seven, sets out five: accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness. That essay also supplies the case that makes the point. By accuracy, Copernicus's system was not better than Ptolemy's until Kepler reworked it more than sixty years after Copernicus died. By consistency, the Ptolemaic system won, since it fit the physics of the time, which explained falling stones and water pumps on the assumption of a motionless earth. By simplicity, it depended on which sort you meant: the labor of computing positions was about the same in the two systems, while the machinery for explaining retrograde motion took one circle per planet for Copernicus and two for Ptolemy. A chemist choosing between oxygen and phlogiston was in a similar position. Oxygen did the weights in reactions, and phlogiston did the resemblance of metals to each other. Each was more accurate about something, and choosing meant ranking the somethings.

From there Kuhn makes the argument that I think Lakatos did not see coming. Scientists who share the same values will still choose differently in the same situation, because they weigh them differently and apply each one differently. Kuhn says that variation may be essential to progress. The choice of a theory, which is as Lakatos says also the choice of a research programme, carries major risk, especially early. Some scientists must choose it early, by virtue of a value system that happens to differ from the average in how it applies, or the programme will never be developed to the point where it can persuade anyone. The choices made by those atypical value systems are, he says, generally wrong. But if every member of the community applied the values in the same high-risk way, the group's enterprise would end. And if the community decided only with hindsight, as Lakatos proposes, the decisions would lose their function. The scientific community cannot wait for history, though some of its members do. The result is achieved by distributing the risk.

Put that beside the Neptune and the Vulcan. In eighteen sixty some astronomers hunted for a planet inside Mercury's orbit, and most of them were wrong, and one or two of them found something that was not there. Kuhn's account says that a community in which nobody went hunting would have been in worse shape for the next time the method worked. It does not say the hunters were right. It says a field has a use for people who are willing to be wrong early, and that no rule of method can sort them from the rest before the result comes in. The argument is a statement about the field and not about the individual, and an individual gets no comfort from it.

Kuhn goes on to answer the phrase itself. A mob is characterized by its rejection of values its members ordinarily hold, he writes, and a specialist group that behaved that way would have stopped doing science. He adds that nothing in his book implies that scientists may choose any theory they like so long as they agree and enforce it, since nature will not be confined in just any set of boxes. And he lands a counterblow on Lakatos's claim to the normative. Lakatos's own position, he says, rests repeatedly on decisions governed not by logical rules but by the mature sensibility of the trained scientist, and that is a social-psychological reliance of just the kind he had been accused of. The difference between the two men, in Kuhn's phrasing, was about substance and not about method.

I think Kuhn is mostly right, with one exception. The value-judgment account answers the mob charge and it matches what Lakatos concedes in his section on instant rationality, since both end with the same hole: a judgment made in the present, without a rule for it. The exception is that Kuhn's answer to Lakatos's question about when to leave is that the community will leave when its trained members' weights tip, and that is more or less where the original charge began. Lakatos's test narrows the room for opinion without closing it. Kuhn's reply says the room must stay open. They end up agreeing, with different sentiments, that somebody's judgment has to be spent in advance of the evidence.

Feyerabend thought the open room was where the trouble started.

## Chapter Five: The Berkeley Café and the Case for Funding Rivals

Feyerabend's essay is titled Consolations for the Specialist, and its first section says where the argument comes from. In nineteen sixty and sixty-one, when Kuhn was a member of the philosophy department at Berkeley, he writes, he had the good fortune to discuss science with him and has looked at it in a new way ever since. A footnote adds that some of the debates were conducted in the now defunct Café Old Europe on Telegraph Avenue, and greatly amused the other customers by their friendly vehemence. He could recognize Kuhn's problems and account for aspects of science Kuhn had pointed to, with the omnipresence of anomalies as one example. He could not accept Kuhn's own theory, and he was less prepared to accept the ideology behind it, which he said could give comfort only to the narrowest and most conceited kind of specialism and would tend to inhibit the advancement of knowledge.

His first charge is ambiguity. Is Kuhn describing or prescribing? Feyerabend reports that more than one social scientist had told him that, thanks to Kuhn, he had learned how to turn his field into a science, which meant restricting criticism, reducing the competing theories to one, and compelling the restless colleagues to do serious work. If that is what Kuhn's book teaches, Feyerabend says, it is a recipe. Kuhn's reply at the end of the volume says that his work does repeatedly make normative claims and that Feyerabend is right about that, and that the descriptive and the normative are mixed in all of them.

The main argument is about tenacity and proliferation, and it concedes much to Kuhn. Feyerabend grants that holding a theory in the face of a refuting fact is rational. Theories can be developed and improved, and a difficulty that sinks the theory in its present form may be accommodated by a later form. It would be a surprise, and a cause for suspicion, if all the available evidence supported a single theory even if the theory were true. The case he builds on is Copernicus and the telescope. A test of the Copernican view involves assumptions about the atmosphere and about the motion of bodies, and also assumptions about how the senses relate to the world, which includes whatever theory you have of vision through a telescope. There is no guarantee that a revolution in cosmology will arrive together with improvements in every auxiliary subject. Who, he asks, would expect the invention of Copernicanism and of the telescope to be followed at once by the appropriate physiological optics? The basic theory and the auxiliary sciences are out of phase, and so the refuting instances do not show that a new theory is doomed. They show that it does not yet fit with the rest of science.

If tenacity is rational, he argues, then the only way to get a change is to supply the incumbent with competitors, and so tenacity requires a principle of proliferation: scientists must develop alternatives to the incumbent before it is in trouble. Nothing else can show up its difficulties. He then presses Kuhn for inconsistency. Kuhn describes normal science as dogmatic and narrow, and insists that refutation requires an alternative, so where do the alternatives come from in a field so monolithic? He remembers Kuhn criticizing David Bohm for disturbing the uniformity of quantum theory. Is proliferation allowed only when the rival is already well entrenched, as Einstein's was? But a field in which several theories coexist is the very condition Kuhn called pre-science.

Lakatos, in a footnote to his own paper, writes that Feyerabend, who had done more than anyone to spread Popper's ideas, seemed to have joined the enemy camp, and adds that his new paper was intriguing. The two men were friends, and they planned to write a book together, to be called For and Against Method. The planned book never appeared. Lakatos died in nineteen seventy-four, and Feyerabend's half became his best-known book the following year, according to the Stanford Encyclopedia's account of it. From that book comes the slogan that Feyerabend is remembered by, anything goes, which the encyclopedia says he had endorsed already in the nineteen seventy essay. His own use of it was a claim about the limited validity of any single methodological rule, not a recommendation of whim. That book I did not read for this episode, and the point rests on the encyclopedia.

What does Feyerabend's side give a person deciding where to put money? Quite a lot, and it is the plainest policy of the three. If a field funds only the programme with the best record, it will learn nothing about the programmes that have not had a chance to get a record, and by Lakatos's own admission the record that counts takes decades to read. Proliferation says to fund rivals while the incumbent is still progressive, because that is when a rival can be developed in an environment that does not yet need it. The cost is the Vulcan hunt. On Feyerabend's account, the half-century of eclipse expeditions was the price of having a field in which anyone could try, and nobody could say in advance which attempt was the Neptune.

His essay ends in a way that makes his difference from Lakatos clear. Is a position that lets standards change with the episode irrational? Yes and no, he answers. Yes, because there is no single set of rules that will guide us through every turn in the history of thought. No, because each episode is rational in the sense that some of its features can be explained by the reasons people had at the time. And he asks the question that Kuhn raised and Lakatos wanted to close: what happens to the standards, as distinct from the theories, during a revolution? If they are changed by causes that are not arguments, he says, a rationalist who wants to keep the field in line will have to answer with causes of his own, propaganda included.

## Chapter Six: What Kuhn Gave Up

After the volume, Kuhn spent the rest of his career answering the reading of his book that Lakatos and Feyerabend had not invented but had anticipated. The evidence is in his own words, in a volume of essays called The Road Since Structure, assembled with his input before his death.

The first thing he gave up was the idea that a revolution involves a break in communication that cannot be crossed. Feyerabend and Kuhn had both used the word incommensurability in nineteen sixty-two, and in the volume of essays Kuhn writes that, unlike Feyerabend, at least as he and others read him, he does not believe the breakdown is ever total or beyond recourse. Where Feyerabend talks of incommensurability without qualification, he has regularly spoken of partial communication, which can be improved to whatever extent circumstances demand and patience permits. The refined version is local. Only a few terms, those that classify nature into kinds, fail to translate between the old theory and the new, and everything else can be compared. This fits what episode fifty-nine found in the ulcer story, where the two camps argued over a trial that both could read.

The second thing he gave up was the company he had acquired. In a lecture at Harvard on the nineteenth of November, nineteen ninety-two, he said that the transformation of the image of science that he had helped to cause had produced a by-product that frequently troubled him, because it was developed by people who often called themselves Kuhnians. He thought their viewpoint damagingly mistaken, and had been pained to be associated with it. The sociology of scientific knowledge at Edinburgh, the programme that claims the content of beliefs is explained by interests and power, he addresses by its name: the strong programme. He writes that he finds its claims absurd, an example of deconstruction gone mad. The line is often given to him in a shorter form, as a remark that he was not a Kuhnian. I searched the collected essays and the autobiographical interview in that volume and did not find that sentence. What he does say is that he was pained to be associated with the people who used the name.

He did not simply push the strong programme away, though. The lecture goes on to say that the programme and its descendants had been dismissed as uncontrolled expressions of hostility to authority, and that he had reacted that way himself for some years. Now he thought the easy answer ignored a real philosophical challenge. There was a continuous slope from the observations that underlie sociological studies of laboratories to their conclusion, which he found unacceptable, and it was not clear how to deflect the line without giving up what had been learned on the way. His diagnosis was that the sociologists took the traditional view of knowledge too much for granted. They assumed that if facts do not come first, and conclusions are not forced by them, there is no knowledge. Perhaps, he suggested, knowledge properly understood is the product of the very process their studies describe.

His constructive proposal is the most Lakatosian thing he wrote. When questions of rationality arise for a historian, he says, they are addressed not to the beliefs held before or after a change but to the change. Why, given the body of belief they began with, did the members of a scientific group elect to alter it? The question is about the small step, taken in context, and the evaluation is a judgment about whether it was a good step. That is a request for a theory of rational change, which is what Lakatos had said the book could not have. The editors of the volume add that in the last years Kuhn described his position as a post-Darwinian Kantianism, which presupposed a fixed thing in itself, and that he later disavowed that notion in conversation with them.

The third item is something he did not give up. He had written in his reply to the critics that normal science is a corollary of revolutions: if it did not exist, or could be dispensed with, then revolutions would also be in jeopardy, and revolutions through criticism need normal science no less than revolutions through crisis. Feyerabend and Lakatos had both wanted the normal periods thinned out. Kuhn kept them.

## Chapter Seven: A Review Panel in the Present Tense

Take a constructed case, and I am building it for the purpose. A study section has three renewal applications for programmes at the same university, each in its fifteenth year. The first has predicted, in each of its last two cycles, a result that could have come out the other way, and one prediction failed. The second has absorbed three disappointing results by adding a mechanism each time, and none of the mechanisms has made a prediction that was then tested. The third is a newcomer's rival to the first, with two preliminary findings, both retrospective, and no result that could embarrass it.

Lakatos's test sorts the first two without effort. The first is progressive, even with a failure in it, since a failed prediction implies the belt was making predictions at all. The second is degenerating, and the question to ask of it is what the three mechanisms forbade. The test says nothing at all about the third, which has no record, and by Lakatos's own account it is the sort of application that a record-based rule excludes. Prout would have been the third application, and Crookes the second.

Feyerabend's rule says to fund the third and to keep funding the first, since the first's record is the only reason anybody can still tell what the third's difficulty is. Kuhn's account says the panel is not a machine. Its members will weigh simplicity against scope differently, and that is the form in which a field stores its willingness to be wrong early. A panel in which every reviewer scored the same way would produce a reliably cautious field. Episode fifty-eight gave the simplified NIH review framework, effective in January of twenty twenty-five, which asks about the importance of the research, its rigor and feasibility, and the expertise and resources of the team. The middle factor is a Lakatosian question about the near term. The importance of the research is a Kuhnian one about values, and innovation sits folded into it. Nothing in the form asks Feyerabend's question, which is what the field would lose if this rival did not exist.

I have one test of my own for a panel, and it is mine and not from any of the three. For a renewal, ask what the rescue hypotheses of the last cycle predicted that nobody knew beforehand and that was then checked. For a rival, ask what the first result is that would embarrass it, and whether it can be obtained inside the award period. The first question is the Lakatos test turned into a sentence. The second is the instrument question from episode fifty-nine: Marshall's group did not win by being right, they won by being willing to run a trial whose outcome the other side would accept.

The case against all three accounts is the one the Vulcan hunters supply. Kuhn's portfolio argument justifies the variance in the field and cannot justify any one grant, so a panel that cites it to fund a weak proposal has misread it. It is a reason to fund a few proposals that the average reviewer would not, and it has nothing to say about which few. Feyerabend's proliferation is costly in a way that his essay does not price: the cost of the Vulcan expeditions was paid by the astronomers who did not take part in them. And Lakatos's test is retrospective by his own admission, which means a funder can apply it only to a record and never to a promise. The honest limit is that none of them provides a rule for the present tense. What the volume does show is that the three could be held together without contradiction by anyone who took each as an answer to a different question: Lakatos to what a record shows, Kuhn to why a field needs variance, Feyerabend to what the incumbent cannot find out alone.

I would also set out how much of this I have verified. The volume, including Lakatos's and Feyerabend's papers and Kuhn's reply, I read in full text this session, as I did the relevant essays of The Road Since Structure. The account of Neptune and Vulcan comes from a general reference page and from secondary summaries, and the date of Galle's observation and the pattern of Prout's chlorine figures are given from memory at hedged precision. The Stanford account of Feyerabend supplied his later book and Lakatos's plan to write it with him.

A person on a panel will have in front of her, some day, a proposal whose author has as good a reason to be wrong as to be right, and no record to tell the two apart. The question to carry into the next review is which of the two mistakes her panel is built to make, and who in the building has been assigned the other.
