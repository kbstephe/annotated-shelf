## Chapter One: The Garden That Got Sorted

A teenage girl stands in a garden in summer, everything in bloom, and for a moment she stops being able to sort one plant from another in the ordinary way. She isn't noticing rose or larkspur or hollyhock. She's simply looking at a wall of color and light, and if you asked her what she saw she wouldn't reach for a species. She might say something as flatly literal as: it's all just beautiful. Keith Johnstone tells a version of this scene from his own teaching life in Impro, and the theatre director's whole book is a long argument about what almost always happens next. The adult standing beside her corrects her. Kindly, probably, with the best of intentions: no, look, those are foxgloves, that one's a delphinium, this is a hollyhock, and see how the delphinium needs staking but the foxglove doesn't. Naming. Sorting. Turning a girl briefly capable of perceiving a whole garden into a girl equipped to pass a botany quiz on it.

Johnstone calls that correction a small act of violence, and he means the word almost literally rather than as color. Not because naming plants is bad, obviously it isn't, but because the correction arrives as though the girl's perception were a mistake needing fixing rather than a capacity worth protecting alongside the naming. Ask which of the two she'll get more practice at over the following twenty years of schooling and the answer isn't close. She'll get drilled in sorting. Nobody drills a child in seeing a garden whole, because nobody thinks it needs teaching, and by the time she's an adult the sorting has become so automatic that the wholeness it replaced is, for most people, simply gone, unremembered, filed under things children do before they learn better.

Johnstone's entire career, on stage and in the classroom both, was built on a bet that the wholeness never actually leaves. It gets buried under thirty years of being taught to sort, and it can be dug back out, but only by someone willing to treat the sorting itself as the thing that needs undoing.

## Chapter Two: The Man Who Read Everyone Else's Plays First

Donald Keith Johnstone was born in Brixham, a fishing town on the Devon coast, in February of nineteen thirty-three. By most accounts of his early life, he was a bright child who did badly at the schooling England offered a working-class boy in the nineteen forties, happier with comics and films and books he'd found for himself than with anything a teacher assigned him. He trained as a teacher regardless, entering college in the early nineteen fifties, and his first real job put him in front of a classroom in Battersea, teaching children the system had already written off, in the era's own ugly phrase, as ineducable. Johnstone found something in that room the label hadn't predicted: children nobody expected anything from turned out, once nobody was grading them down for it, capable of unguarded, alive imaginative work every institution around them was busy punishing everywhere else. It is close to the founding observation his whole career runs on.

In nineteen fifty-six the director George Devine, building the Royal Court Theatre into the address for serious new British playwriting, commissioned Johnstone, alongside Tony Richardson, to write a play. It wasn't much of a play by Johnstone's own later account, but it was enough to bring him inside an institution that would occupy him for roughly the next decade: chief reader of new scripts, running the Court's Writers' Group, directing its experimental Studio, eventually an associate director of the theatre itself, in a position to decide, more than almost anyone else in Britain at the time, which unproven playwrights got a stage at all. It is a strange origin for a man whose later reputation would rest on distrusting most of what conventional theatre training does to actors, and by his own account Johnstone spent that decade growing steadily more convinced that the polish the Court prized in a script was the wrong thing to be teaching a performer.

He left England eventually and, after some itinerant years teaching wherever a university would have him, settled at the University of Calgary, in western Canada, in the early nineteen seventies. There, before the decade was out, he and a fellow instructor named Mel Tonken turned a lunchtime improv group they'd been running for students into a standing company, Loose Moose Theatre, which Johnstone led as artistic director for roughly the next twenty years and stayed attached to, teaching workshops, for another twenty after that. He died in March of two thousand twenty-three, in a Calgary hospital, at ninety, having spent very nearly seventy years testing the same claim on three continents' worth of actors: that almost nobody arrives on a stage lacking imagination. What they arrive with is decades of practice rejecting it.

## Chapter Three: The Obedient Child

Johnstone opens the book's section on spontaneity with a reversal he states almost in passing and then spends the rest of the book building on: children, he argues, are not immature adults, gradually acquiring an imagination and confidence a finished adult already has. Adults are atrophied children, having lost, through thirty-odd years of systematic discouragement, capacities they were born with and once used constantly. Flip the usual developmental story on its head like that and the whole question of how to teach improvisation changes shape. You are not building something new into a student. You are undoing something that was done to them, mostly by people who meant well.

His flattest statement of what got done, and the line readers of the book still quote back at each other decades later, is seven words long: "the obedient child is a dead child." Johnstone doesn't mean dead literally. He means that a child trained thoroughly enough in doing what's expected has had the unpredictable, generative part of themselves switched off in favor of the part that can be relied on to behave, and that most schooling, most parenting, and most professional life afterward keeps rewarding the second part and steadily starving the first.

Elsewhere in the same section he makes a harder, more specific claim: that imaginative children are disliked by their teachers, and that research bears this out. He doesn't cite the research. I looked for it, the way this show usually tries to track down the studies and figures it repeats on air, and I couldn't find what he was pointing to, and as far as I can tell nobody writing about the book in the forty-five years since has traced it either. That's worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over: a book built on distrusting the systems that grade people down for being unusual makes, in passing, the kind of unfalsifiable appeal to unnamed research that this show's own clinician's lens is built to catch. The claim might be true. Some structured evidence out of drama-education research does point toward schools rewarding compliant, conventional responses over strange ones. But Johnstone states it as settled fact, and settled it is not.

What survives that specific overreach is the broader diagnosis, and it's harder to dismiss than the single unsupported line: whatever the mechanism, most adults really do arrive at an improv class, or a brainstorming meeting, or a garden, noticeably worse at unguarded first responses than they were at seven. Something happened in between. Johnstone's book is an extended argument that the something is not maturation. It's practice, thirty years of it, at rejecting your own first thought before anyone else gets to hear it.

## Chapter Four: The Seesaw

If spontaneity describes the wound, Johnstone's second big idea describes the machinery still running the moment two people are in a room together, spontaneous or not. Status, in his usage, has almost nothing to do with money, rank, or job title. It's a transaction, played out fresh in every exchange, and his own compact formula for the whole thing is a seesaw: I go up, you go down, or the other way round, and every conversational move either raises the speaker's position at the listener's expense, lowers it in the listener's favor, or, more rarely and more interestingly, does neither and simply keeps both people level. He goes as far as to argue that tragedy as a dramatic form runs on this same mechanism: a king, a general, someone an audience has watched occupy the top of a pecking order, gets knocked off it, publicly, irreversibly, and the specific horror of watching Lear on the heath, or Oedipus blinding himself, is what it looks like when the seesaw's far end finally hits the ground.

What makes the theory usable rather than merely clever is how physical Johnstone insists on making it. He claims status reads almost entirely through the body rather than through words: a head held still against one that fidgets and darts, gestures that spill loosely into the space around a person against gestures kept small and tucked in, hands kept away from the face against hands that keep touching it. Eye contact, in particular, gets a more specific rule than the folk version most people already believe. It isn't how long you hold someone's gaze that signals status, on Johnstone's account. It's what you do when they hold yours back: a flinch and a quick, repeated glance away reads as low status even from someone who was staring confidently a second earlier, while someone who can meet a returned stare without reacting to it at all reads as high status regardless of how the exchange started.

He teaches the whole apparatus through an exercise built to strip status down to its purest form: two actors, one playing master, one playing servant, told that every inch of the stage belongs to the master, with nothing to justify but the roles themselves, no plot, no props beyond what the actors mime into being. Students given nothing to do but move through that single constraint discover status transactions happening constantly and almost entirely without dialogue, a servant who drifts too far into the master's territory, a master who claims a chair the servant was heading for, each tiny incursion instantly legible to an audience that has never been told a single rule about what to watch for. That's the whole demonstration, and it's also Johnstone's real argument in miniature: nobody needs to be taught to read status. Everybody already reads it constantly, all day, usually without noticing they're doing it. What Johnstone teaches is noticing on purpose.

## Chapter Five: The Doctor and the Leg

Underneath status sits a smaller, more mechanical rule Johnstone treats as the actual engine of every improvised scene, and it has nothing to do with the phrase most people who've taken a single improv class would reach for first. He never writes "yes, and." That formulation, and the whole modern shorthand built around it, comes out of a different, related lineage: Del Close, a student of the Chicago teacher Viola Spolin, popularized it decades later at what became ImprovOlympic, and Charna Halpern, Close's collaborator, even named her production company after the phrase. Johnstone's own vocabulary, in the book he actually wrote, is blocking and accepting offers, and the distinction matters more than it looks: an offer, in his terms, is anything one performer hands another, a line, a gesture, an implied fact about the world of the scene, and a block is any response that refuses the offer outright, denies it happened, or changes the subject rather than building on what's now true.

Take a constructed version of the exchange, close in spirit to the demonstrations Johnstone actually ran in workshops, since it makes the difference easy to hear. One actor says: doctor, I've got the strangest pain in my leg, handing a partner a whole world to build in, a patient, a doctor, an office, a mystery. A blocking response denies all of it at once. I'm not a doctor. There's no leg here. What pain? Every one of those replies is a perfectly valid sentence, and each one kills the scene, because it refuses to let anything the other performer just built continue existing. An accepting response does something almost embarrassingly modest by comparison: it lets the offer stand and adds one true thing to it. Ah yes, the left leg, since the accident. Nothing clever has happened yet. But now there's a doctor, an accident, a shared history the two actors are both suddenly responsible for, built entirely out of one performer refusing to say no.

Johnstone doesn't stop at the stage. He extends the same diagnosis, more bluntly than most theatre teachers bother to, straight into ordinary unhappiness: people whose lives feel stuck, he argues, are very often blocking offers this way, constantly, outside any theatre, turning down invitations, dismissing ideas, refusing to register what's actually being handed to them, and mistaking the resulting narrowness for bad luck rather than a habit anyone could in principle unlearn. It's a large claim for a book about stagecraft to make about a person's whole life, and Johnstone makes it without much hedging. But it's also, once you've watched the doctor's-leg example work, hard to un-hear once you start listening for it in an actual meeting: how often the first response to a colleague's half-formed idea is some version of no, that won't work, or we tried that already, rather than yes, and here's what we'd need to make it work.

## Chapter Six: A Scoreboard and a Pie

By the late nineteen seventies Johnstone had a theory of what killed spontaneity and a theory of what a healthy improvised exchange looked like moment to moment. What he didn't have, at Loose Moose in Calgary, was an audience reliably willing to sit through experimental theatre for its own sake. His fix borrowed its shape from something about as far from experimental theatre as Calgary could offer: professional wrestling, which Johnstone had come to admire, without irony, as a skilled form of working-class performance, one built to generate the kind of loud, immediate audience reaction serious theatre had mostly trained itself out of wanting. If wrestling could get a crowd screaming for a body slam, he reasoned, the same crowd mechanics ought to work on two casts of performers instead of two men in tights.

Theatresports, the format he built out of that idea, pits opposing teams of improvisers against each other on audience suggestions, scored round by round by a panel of judges holding up cards, the totals tallied on a board the crowd can watch climb in real time. Early performances leaned into the wrestling framing rather than softening it: a losing team could expect the audience to pelt them with whipped-cream pies, and one of the format's original named games carried, with a bluntness Johnstone clearly intended, the same title as his entire theory of what kills a scene: No Blocking. It is, on paper, an odd way to stage a defense of unguarded creative risk, wrapping it in the trappings of a competitive spectacle built on winners, losers, and audience heckling. It also worked, immediately and durably, in a way Johnstone's more purely pedagogical experiments mostly hadn't. Give an audience a scoreboard and a reason to boo, and they will sit still for the kind of vulnerable, failure-prone improvisation Johnstone had spent two decades arguing actors needed to practice in public.

## Chapter Seven: The Invention Story That Isn't Quite True

The story usually told about Theatresports, including by Johnstone's own later organization, is a straightforward invention story: one director, one wrestling-inspired insight, one theatre in Calgary. The actual record is messier, and the mess is worth keeping rather than smoothing over, because it's the same kind of correction this show has made before about a clean origin story. Years earlier, in nineteen seventy-two, a New York theatre producer named David Shepherd, who had already helped originate the improv scene that eventually grew into Chicago's Second City, staged team-based competitive improvised theatre of his own; by one detailed history of the North American improv movement, a version of his format reached Toronto in nineteen seventy-six as something called the Improvisational Olympics, which later became the long-running Canadian Improv Games, years before a Loose Moose audience ever threw a pie. None of this makes Johnstone's Theatresports a copy. The wrestling framing, the judged-round structure, the specific games built around blocking and status were his own. But it does mean the tidy version, one man alone inventing competitive improv out of a wrestling match, understates how much of the raw idea, teams competing for an audience's favor, was already circulating in North American theatre half a decade before he formalized it into a licensable format.

It's worth adding a second correction while we're at it, because it's the one that gets repeated most confidently and least accurately: Theatresports is not the parent format of American improv comedy generally. Second City and the Upright Citizens Brigade trace their own lineage back through Viola Spolin and her student Del Close, a separate line that developed largely in parallel with Johnstone's Canadian one rather than descending from it. Where Johnstone's fingerprints do show up unmistakably, decades later, is in Britain's Whose Line Is It Anyway, whose short-form games draw heavily on formats built at Loose Moose, and, closer to home for him, in two former Loose Moose students, Mark McKinney and Bruce McCulloch, who took what they'd learned under Johnstone straight into the sketch troupe Kids in the Hall.

## Chapter Eight: The Routine and the Tilt

Johnstone's third section turns from single exchanges to whole stories, and it opens by discouraging the one instinct almost every beginning improviser reaches for first: trying to invent a plot. Consciously plotting a story while performing it live, in his account, is close to paralyzing, the improviser's attention split between playing the scene honestly and steering it toward some ending decided on in advance. His alternative asks for almost nothing at the start: establish a routine, a stable, ordinary situation with a clear who, where, and what, then interrupt it. He calls the interruption a tilt, the single moment that knocks the routine off its axis, and his claim is that a story assembled this way, routine then tilt then whatever consequences the tilt forces, tends to feel more inevitable to an audience than one an improviser tried to plan, because nobody, including the performer, knew where the tilt would send things until it happened.

The other narrative habit he teaches, reincorporation, asks performers to treat small details established early in a scene, an odd prop, an offhand line, a minor character mentioned once, as material owed a return later, the improvised equivalent of what playwrights call planting a gun in the first act so it can go off in the third. An audience that watches a throwaway detail come back transformed, doing real work it wasn't obviously built for, reads the whole improvised piece as more structured and more intentional than anything any single performer actually planned, purely because someone remembered to pick the detail back up.

What ties both techniques together is a paradox Johnstone seems to have made peace with rather than resolved: a book whose first half argues for unlearning discipline spends its narrative chapters teaching a fairly rigid set of habits, platform, tilt, reincorporation, in service of looking spontaneous. By one account from students who trained under him, he'd tell beginners the content of a scene barely matters and to trust the structure to generate it, a claim he seems to have made less because it was strictly true than because believing it was the only way most students would stop trying to control the content and let the structure actually do its job.

## Chapter Nine: The Mask and the Watcher

The book's fourth and final section is also its strangest, and it's where Johnstone's argument, followed all the way to its actual conclusion, lands somewhere the earlier chapters only gesture toward. He spends it on trance: first surveying, briefly, how different cultures induce altered, unselfconscious states through ritual, sport, and possession, then turning to his own technique for producing something adjacent to it in an ordinary rehearsal room, using masks. Put a plain mask over a trained actor's face, Johnstone claims, and something genuine changes in how that performer inhabits a role, a vividness and a commitment to impulse the same actor, unmasked, can rarely reach, because unmasked, he argues, most actors spend a significant share of their attention watching and judging themselves rather than simply acting. The mask, on his account, doesn't conceal the performer from the audience so much as mute the internal critic that usually watches every choice, and what comes out from underneath, freed of that surveillance, is closer to what a small child does before anyone's taught them to sort a garden into species.

Here, more than a hundred pages after the book opened on that same reversal, is the claim the whole thing has been circling: the block was never really about lacking an idea. Every exercise in the book, from the seesaw of status to the doctor with the mysterious leg pain to the routine waiting for its tilt, has been staging the same single obstacle from a different angle, a chronic, learned, entirely unconscious habit of rejecting the thought that arrives first in favor of a more careful, more defensible, less revealing one. An empty stage doesn't actually produce an empty mind. It produces, on Johnstone's account, the same crowded, self-censoring mind decades of schooling built, minus the schoolwork that used to justify it. Spontaneity training was never adding anything. It was clearing a habit of refusal out of the way of a capacity that had been sitting there the entire time.

## Chapter Ten: The Meeting as a Scene

Follow that argument off the stage and it scales the way the best ideas in this shelf's canon tend to: from one performer's private habit of self-censorship, to two people in a scene, to a room full of people deciding, together, whether an idea gets picked up or blocked before anyone's finished saying it. A meeting is, structurally, closer to an improvised scene than most people who run them would like to admit: someone makes an offer, half-formed, and the room's next move, accept it and build, or block it and move on, gets decided largely by status, who's speaking, who outranks whom, whose head stays stillest in the room, rather than by whether the offer itself was any good.

I recognize the pattern from a shape of meeting I sit in often: a monthly review call across a distance programme I help run, ten districts, lay counsellors and their supervisors and a small central team reading the numbers together. The people closest to an actual client, the counsellors and the frontline supervisors, are also, reliably, the lowest-status people on the call, and I've watched a useful field observation, something as simple as a supervisor seeing that a specific enrollment form keeps getting skipped for a specific, fixable reason, get blocked without anyone meaning to block it: a more senior voice moves the agenda along, a raised eyebrow reads as ah yes, we know, and the room accepts the senior non-answer over the junior offer, not for the quality of either one: status alone decided which one got treated as worth building on. Johnstone would recognize the mechanism instantly. He built an entire pedagogy around the fact that the best offer in the room and the highest-status voice in the room are two entirely different things, and that most groups, left to their own habits, will follow the second one and call it listening to the first.

## Chapter Eleven: Three Ways of Watching the Same Room

This shelf has now spent three episodes in a row inside the same small, crowded space: two or more people, a room, and a version of the self that gets produced rather than simply expressed. Goffman argued a person's presented self is a dramatic effect staged for an audience, built out of front region and back region, sign vehicles chosen and withheld. Berne argued two people can run the identical scripted argument for years, each getting a psychologically necessary payoff neither will admit to wanting. Johnstone, arriving at both problems from a rehearsal room rather than a hospital ward or a sociology department, supplies something the other two don't quite: a mechanism for how the performance gets built moment to moment, offer by offer, rather than just a diagnosis of why it exists.

Read status theory against Goffman and the two overlap almost exactly, right down to the vocabulary of performance, except that Goffman treats a front as something managed consciously enough to be studied as strategy, while Johnstone insists status plays out almost entirely below that level of awareness, in stillness and gaze and how far a gesture spills into someone else's space, closer to something the body does than something a person decides. Read blocking against Berne and the overlap runs the other direction: where Berne's games need two players who unconsciously need the identical bad ending, Johnstone's blocked scene needs only one player refusing to build on what's already true, which makes blocking, in a sense, the more common and more mundane failure, less a psychologically loaded script than a habit almost anyone can catch themselves doing in a single ordinary meeting, no childhood decision required to explain it. Put the three together and a fuller picture of the performed self starts to emerge: staged for an audience, running scripts written in childhood, and built, in real time, out of thousands of small moments where an offer either got accepted or got reflexively refused.

## Chapter Twelve: A Theory Everyone Knows and Nobody Reads

Impro has stayed in print continuously since Faber and Faber first published it in nineteen seventy-nine, and it remains, by most accounts of drama-school curricula, one of the most commonly assigned texts in actor training anywhere English is spoken, an unusually long shelf life for a book with almost no footnotes and a fair amount of undefended assertion in it. Its clearest crossover into a non-theatre audience came through Malcolm Gladwell, who leaned on Johnstone's status and eye-contact material in Blink in two thousand five, introducing readers who would never sit through an acting class to the seesaw and the flinch-and-glance-away rule as if they were established behavioral science rather than one theatre director's working intuition, refined over decades of watching actors rather than through anything resembling a controlled study.

That gap, between how the ideas get received, as settled insight, and how they were actually produced, as a practitioner's accumulated hunches, runs through most of the book's legacy. Loose Moose is still operating in Calgary decades after Johnstone stepped back from running it day to day. The Theatresports format he built has its own licensing body training instructors on several continents. And the two former students who carried what they learned from him into Kids in the Hall are about as clean a piece of evidence as this shelf tends to find that a pedagogy actually produced what it promised, tracked from one classroom in Calgary to a body of work millions of people watched without ever hearing Johnstone's name attached to it.

## Chapter Thirteen: The Cult Question

Weigh Impro's rigor against this shelf's usual standard and the book fails most of the tests it would normally have to pass before earning a defense. It isn't, on its own terms, a work of theory: no controlled comparison of trained-versus-untrained improvisers, no attempt to test the status claims against the psychology literature on nonverbal dominance, which does support some of what Johnstone describes but a narrower, more hedged set of behaviors than his confident taxonomy suggests. The imaginative-children research he gestures toward and never names remains, as far as anyone has traced online in the forty-five years since, untraceable. And the book's own internal contradiction, a manifesto against rigid habits that spends its second half teaching several fairly rigid habits, is one Johnstone never quite resolves so much as talks past.

The sharper critique lands on the book's final section rather than its evidence base. In a long, skeptical essay on Johnstone's mask-and-trance teaching, the writer Sean Goedecke argues that what Johnstone describes as liberating performers from self-judgment reads, followed to its logical end, as a technique for dissolving a student's ordinary sense of personal responsibility, summarizing Johnstone as telling students that once a person accepts they're no longer responsible for their own actions, there's no real need to keep maintaining a personality at all. Goedecke isn't alone in the underlying observation, even where he pushes it further than most: accounts from former Loose Moose students describe a company that ran, at times, closer to a devoted following around a single charismatic teacher than an ordinary theatre school, students who took his harshest workshop notes as settled truth rather than one man's opinion, the same unquestioning deference the book's own opening chapters spend so much energy diagnosing as the enemy.

The most detailed outside account of Johnstone's life and teaching, Theresa Robbins Dudeck's two thousand thirteen critical biography, is worth naming here for what it is rather than what its subtitle promises: written by someone who was also Johnstone's friend and, eventually, the literary executor of his estate, which makes it the fullest record available and, at the same time, not the arm's-length account the word critical implies. Between a largely admiring surface reception and one blogger's sharper reading, there is remarkably little independent, skeptical scholarship on a book this widely taught, and that gap is itself a finding worth stating rather than a hole worth apologizing for.

## Chapter Fourteen: The Dose

Give Impro the dosage test this shelf applies to every appealing framework and it splits cleanly in two. As a working diagnostic for a single stage scene, a single meeting, a single stuck moment in an otherwise ordinary conversation, it's hard to improve on: watching for the block instead of the missing idea, watching status instead of the words being said, catches something real that most people walk past without ever naming. As an identity, a whole philosophy of unlearned selfhood that a student adopts wholesale from one teacher and stops testing against anything else, the same material curdles into exactly what its harshest critics describe, an excuse to discard ordinary responsibility while calling the discarding liberation, with a charismatic instructor standing at the center deciding what counts as an authentic first impulse and what still counts as a block.

Read the first two sections, on spontaneity and status, and use them the way Johnstone's better students clearly did: as a way of noticing, mid-meeting, mid-argument, mid-scene, that you just turned down an offer worth building on, or that the room just let the stillest, highest-status voice win by default rather than the best one. Approach the masks-and-trance chapters with more skepticism than the man who wrote them ever seemed to apply to himself, and the book still holds up as diagnosis, no enrollment in the cult version required to get the benefit.

## Chapter Fifteen: The Reflex Before the Words

The question worth carrying out of this episode isn't which of your own recent ideas got blocked, though most people can already name one. It's whether you can catch the block in the half second it actually happens, the reflex no, that won't work, or we tried that already, arriving before you've even finished hearing the offer, and choose, once, to add one true thing to it instead and see what happens to the room.
