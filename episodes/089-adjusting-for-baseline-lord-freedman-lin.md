## Chapter One: Two Statisticians and a Dietician

In 1967 a psychometrician at the Educational Testing Service, Frederic Lord, published a note of about two pages in Psychological Bulletin. It tells a story with no data in it at all. A large university wants to know what the food in its dining halls does to its students, and whether it does something different to the men and the women. Every student is weighed on arrival in September and again the following June. Two statisticians are handed the numbers and work independently.

The first looks at the women. Their average weight in June is the same as in September, and so is the whole spread of weights; individual women gained and lost, sometimes a good deal, but the group as a whole did not move. The men are the same. He concludes that there is no evidence the diet did anything, and in particular no evidence that it did something different to the two sexes, since neither changed.

The second statistician runs an analysis of covariance. He regresses June weight on September weight within each sex, finds the two slopes essentially parallel, and compares the heights of the two lines. The gap between them is highly significant. His conclusion is that the men gained more than the women, once proper allowance is made for where each started. Pressed to explain, he says that if you pick a group of men and a group of women with the same spread of September weights, the men will gain more by June.

Lord adds the college dietician, who has to reconcile these. Both statisticians are visibly correct, he says, given the usual assumptions; if she had hired only one of them, she would have reached a different conclusion depending on which. What is the explanation, he asks, and answers that there are as many different explanations as there are explainers. Then he gives his own, which is that there isn't one. With the data usually available for such studies, he writes, no logical or statistical procedure can be counted on to make proper allowance for uncontrolled differences that existed before the comparison began.

The last episode ended with a covariate the trialist had measured because it predicts the outcome, and the instruction from Stephen Senn's dice that it belongs in the model, chosen in advance. Lord's note is the reason that instruction makes people nervous. If adjusting for where people started can turn no effect into a significant one, using only a ruler and a scale, what exactly does it do to a trial? This episode follows that question through three arguments: Lord's dining hall, David Freedman's proof that regression can bias a randomised experiment, and Winston Lin's reply, which ended up quoted by name in the Food and Drug Administration's guidance.

## Chapter Two: Who Was Right

For half a century the answer kept depending on who was asked. Paul Holland and Donald Rubin, in the account most often cited, concluded that if both statisticians were only describing the data, both were right, and that if they were making causal claims, neither was right or wrong, because each rested on an untestable assumption about what the students would have weighed under some other diet. Lord's story never says what the other diet was. That is a real flaw in it: a causal effect is a comparison, and no comparison was given.

In 2016 Judea Pearl, the computer scientist who built much of the modern graphical language of causation, tried again with that language. He read the question as being about sex, since the diet is the same for everyone. Then the September weight sits between the cause and the outcome. Being male makes you heavier on arrival, and your weight on arrival shapes your weight in June. A variable in that position is a mediator. The first statistician estimated the total effect of sex on weight change, which is zero. The second, by holding September weight fixed, estimated the direct effect, the part that does not run through arriving heavier, and that is positive. They answered different questions, and both answered correctly.

Pearl then took the version that the psychometrician Howard Wainer and his co-author Brown had made famous, where the two groups of students are two dining rooms rather than two sexes, and students chose their room. Now the September weight is not downstream of the room; it was measured before anyone ate there, and in the figure the heavier students tended to pick the second room. That makes September weight a confounder, a cause of both which room you ate in and what you weighed in June. Confounders must be adjusted for. In this version, Pearl writes, the second statistician was right and the first was flatly wrong.

The most recent and most careful treatment, by Peter Tennant and five colleagues in Leeds, London and Boston, first posted as a preprint in 2023, refuses both escapes. Saying the two statisticians answered different questions, they argue, leaves the puzzle standing, because a researcher still has to decide which question was asked. Their answer is that the two methods can aim at the same target and miss it in opposite directions.

The change score misses this way. Subtracting September from June assumes that, absent any effect, everyone would end where they began, so that each kilo of starting weight carries forward one for one. In real data it does not; heavy people tend to drift down and light people up. So the subtraction takes away too much from groups that started high, and whenever the exposure is related to the starting value, as sex is to weight, the change score is diluted or even reversed. The analysis of covariance misses the other way. It estimates how much September predicts June, but September weight is measured with error, the error flattens that slope, and too little is subtracted, which inflates the estimate.

All three readings need the same thing before they can begin: a group difference at the start that came from somewhere. Sex made the men heavier. The students' own choices sent heavier people to the second room. The whole argument is about how to remove a difference that a cause put there. So the question for a trialist is what is left of Lord's paradox when the cause that put it there is a coin.

## Chapter Three: When the Coin Chooses the Hall

Very little is left. Tennant's group say it in a sentence: in randomised data, the change score and the adjusted follow-up converge on the same estimand, and the second is preferred only because it is more precise. Their algebra shows why. The change score's extra subtraction term is harmless when the exposure is unrelated to the starting value, and a coin makes it unrelated, on average over all the allocations it might have made. The dining hall's dilemma is a dilemma about groups that formed themselves.

But the last episode's lesson applies here. The coin balances the arms on average, not in the one trial you happen to be running, and in that one the arms may start apart. Then the three analyses a trialist might choose, comparing final scores, comparing changes, or comparing the end point adjusted for the starting score, give three different answers, and only one of them treats the starting gap correctly.

Andrew Vickers and Douglas Altman set this out in a one-page statistics note in the BMJ in 2001, with a real trial. Fifty-two patients with shoulder pain were randomised to true or sham acupuncture, twenty-five and twenty-seven, and scored on a hundred-point scale of pain and function where higher is better. The coin had left the real treatment ahead before anyone was needled: its arm averaged 60.4 against 53.9 for the sham group, a head start of six and a half points.

Compare final scores and the acupuncture group is 17.3 points better. Part of that is the head start, carried into the final measurement, so that number is too large. The trial's authors compared changes instead, and found a difference of 10.8. This one errs low, for a reason the dining hall has already given. Patients who start low tend to improve more than those who begin high, partly because some of a low score was bad luck on the day of measurement, and bad luck does not repeat. In this trial, change was negatively correlated with baseline, about minus a quarter within groups. The acupuncture patients, starting higher, had less room to regress upward, and the change score charged them for it.

The analysis of covariance fits one line per group relating final score to baseline, with a common slope, here 0.71, and reads off the vertical distance between the lines. It came to 12.7 points, between the two simple answers, with a confidence interval from about four to twenty-one. Vickers and Altman put the property plainly: if baseline scores happen to be worse in the treated group, comparing final scores underestimates the effect and comparing changes overestimates it, while the adjusted analysis gives the same answer whether or not there is imbalance. That slope of 0.71 is the regression to the mean, measured. Looking only at the end assumes the slope is zero. A change score assumes it is one. The adjusted analysis estimates it from the patients.

The acupuncture trial has no paradox in it, only three estimators with different errors, and the question of what you should have chosen before you saw the table. Vickers and Altman are flat about that too. Choosing whichever analysis gives the more significant result is incorrect; the method belongs in the protocol.

## Chapter Four: Three Analyses at Four Correlations

The acupuncture trial was about bias from a chance imbalance. The bigger reason to adjust is precision, and it can be shown with arithmetic alone. Take an invented trial, with numbers chosen only to make the comparison clear. The outcome is measured at baseline and again at follow-up, with the same spread both times, and the arms start level on average. Suppose a comparison of follow-up scores alone needs two hundred patients for the power you want. How many do the other two analyses need? The answer depends on a single number, the correlation between a patient's baseline and follow-up.

At a correlation of 0.2, the measures barely track each other. Subtracting baseline mostly adds the noise of a second measurement, and a change-score analysis needs three hundred and twenty patients. Adjusting for baseline instead requires a hundred and ninety-two, barely better than ignoring baseline, because baseline has little to offer.

At 0.5 the subtraction breaks even at two hundred. The adjusted comparison gets by on a hundred and fifty, a quarter fewer.

At 0.8, typical of a stable chronic measure such as weight, the change score has become a good method, needing eighty, and the adjusted analysis needs seventy-two. Vickers and Altman give one row of the same table from a real calculation: at a correlation of 0.6, eighty-five patients for final scores, sixty-eight for change, fifty-four for the adjusted comparison.

Run across the range and the pattern is plain. The adjusted analysis is never worse than either rival in this setting, and the change score is worse than doing nothing with baseline whenever the correlation is below a half. The reason is that adjustment estimates how much of baseline to subtract, where the other two fix that amount in advance at all of it or none of it. Every other number in this chapter follows from that one choice.

Brennan Kahan and colleagues in London and Oxford took the question to real data in 2014, re-running twelve outcomes from eight trials across several specialties. Set each trial's power at eighty percent on the unadjusted analysis, adjust for covariates known to be prognostic, and the median power rose to 92.6 percent. Adjusting for covariates that predicted nothing cost very little: the worst case fell from eighty percent to 78.5. In one pleural infection trial, MIST2, adjusting for the baseline size of the effusion, which correlated 0.44 with the size at day seven, cut the standard error by about a third. And yet, they note, reviews had found that only about a quarter to a third of trials adjusted in their main analysis.

So the question becomes why trialists would leave that much power on the table. Part of the answer is habit. Part of it is a Berkeley statistician who spent the last years of his life explaining that regression adjustment in an experiment rests on nothing the experiment provides.

## Chapter Five: Freedman's Warning

David Freedman was one of the great sceptics of statistical modelling, a probabilist who wrote textbooks, testified about census adjustment and spent decades asking social scientists what licensed the regressions they ran. In 2008, the year he died, he published a run of papers on experiments. One, in Advances in Applied Mathematics, is titled On Regression Adjustments to Experimental Data, and its abstract says that since randomisation does not justify the models, almost anything can happen.

His framework is older than Fisher's. It comes from Jerzy Neyman in 1923. Each patient in a trial has two potential outcomes, the result she would have on treatment and the one she would show on control. They are fixed numbers, properties of her, and only one will ever be seen. The only randomness is the coin that decides which. The quantity you want is the average, over these patients, of the difference between the two. This is the framework in which the simple difference in means is exactly unbiased, a theorem that needs nothing but the coin.

Regression needs more. The textbook model says each outcome equals a constant, plus a treatment effect, plus a slope times the covariate, plus an error that is unrelated to everything and drawn afresh for each patient. Nothing in Neyman's setup produces such an error. Freedman shows that the error term in a regression on experimental data is a function of the coin, and so is strongly related to the treatment variable it is supposed to be independent of. What follows from that is trouble of three kinds. The adjusted estimate is biased, though the bias shrinks as the trial grows. It can be less precise than the unadjusted one, even in very large samples, when the arms are unequal in size. And the standard error the software prints can be wrong in either direction, by large factors, partly because the textbook formula assumes both arms are equally variable, which nothing in the coin guarantees.

How big is the bias? It shrinks in proportion to one over the number of patients, while the standard error falls with the square root of that number, so it fades into the noise as a trial grows. Freedman's simulations, which he describes but does not report, put rough marks on it: noticeable with one hundred or two hundred and fifty subjects, sometimes significant at five hundred though rarely large enough to matter, negligible at a thousand. For a medical audience, that is the uncomfortable range. A great many trials have a hundred patients.

His take-home message is narrower than the papers that cite him. The variables that need to be balanced, he writes, are the two potential outcomes themselves; covariates are only proxies, often weak, and when they are weak the gains and losses from adjustment are minor. In a real experiment, if adjustment made a substantial difference, he would suggest much caution in interpreting the results. He adds that practitioners will say they know all this, but then why do they so often fit models without discussing assumptions?

He had a case in mind. The Women's Health Initiative trials, which this show covered in the evidence series, reported their headline results from proportional-hazards models. Freedman reconstructed the plain intention-to-treat comparisons from the published counts and found almost no difference. The models had added nothing but an extra layer between the reader and the randomisation.

Freedman's warning and Senn's instruction now point in opposite directions. Senn says an observed prognostic covariate belongs in the model, and ignoring it treats the face-up die, a prognostic factor already seen, as if it were still under the cup. Freedman says the model is unjustified, and a large difference between adjusted and unadjusted results is a reason for alarm. One of them has to give, and in 2013 a Berkeley graduate student whom Freedman had helped wrote the paper that decided which.

## Chapter Six: Lin's Repair

Winston Lin's paper, in the Annals of Applied Statistics, is titled Agnostic Notes on Regression Adjustments to Experimental Data. As the title warns, Lin does not believe the regression model either. He asks what least squares does when the model is wrong, using Freedman's own framework and Freedman's own conditions.

His way in is survey sampling, where the same problem was solved long ago without anyone believing in a model. William Cochran's textbook has an example from 1937. A researcher wants the average surface area of the leaves on a plant. Measuring area is slow, but weighing a leaf is quick. So she weighs every leaf and measures area for a small random sample. The sample average area is unbiased, but it ignores the weights. If the sampled leaves are lighter than the plant's average, their area is probably below the whole plant's mean too, and you can correct for that using the relation between weight and area in the sample. Nobody needs leaf area to be a straight-line function of weight for this to help. It helps whenever weight predicts area at all.

A randomised trial is two such samples drawn from the same patients. The treated arm is a random sample of the patients' treated outcomes, the control arm a random draw of their control outcomes, and the covariate is measured on everyone. So adjust each arm's mean toward where it would be if that arm's covariate average matched the whole trial's, using a slope estimated within that arm. That is a regression with the treatment, the covariate, and their interaction, the covariate centred on its overall mean. Lin proves that in large samples this estimator cannot be less precise than the unadjusted difference, whatever the true relation between covariate and outcome. And he proves that a robust standard error, the kind econometricians call the sandwich, which does not assume the two arms are equally variable, is trustworthy or errs on the cautious side, with or without the interaction.

Then he explains why Freedman's version could go wrong. The usual adjustment uses one slope for both arms. Suppose, in an illustration of Lin's, that the outcome climbs ten units per unit of covariate on treatment and two on control. A pooled regression with equal arms finds a slope near six, under-corrects one arm and over-corrects the other, and the two errors cancel. Two wrongs, in his phrase, make a right. But make the treated arm nine times larger and the pooled slope sits near the treated arm's, while the correction falls mostly on the small control arm, which is far from the overall mean. The ideal adjustment there adds about twenty-eight to the estimate; the pooled one adds about ninety-two. That is how adjustment can hurt precision. Lin shows that for the usual adjustment to do worse than none with one covariate, a necessary condition is either that more than three-quarters of patients are in one arm, or that the covariate predicts who benefits more strongly than it predicts the outcome.

Lin's worked example is an education experiment run by Joshua Angrist and colleagues at a Canadian university, whose first-year students were offered support services, cash for good grades, or both. He took just the men in two of the arms, fifty-eight and ninety-nine of them, small and unequal on purpose. Adjusting for high-school grades moved the estimated effect of adding the cash a little, from slightly negative to a bit more so, and shrank the standard error from 0.158 to 0.146 on a four-point grade scale; the interacted version agreed. To check whether the standard errors could be trusted, he kept the men's actual grades, assumed the treatment did nothing, and re-randomised them a quarter of a million times. All three estimators came out approximately unbiased. He then estimated Freedman's bias term directly from the data, and it came to minus 0.0002 grade points, orders of magnitude too small to affect anything.

Then comes the concession. The strongest reasons for Freedman's preference, he writes, are transparency and the danger of specification search. The difference in means is the hands above the table estimate: nobody could have arrived at it by trying twenty models and reporting the best. So present it first. But he does not accept that adjustment needs belief in the model, and he puts it in a sentence with an unexpected comparison. You do not need to believe in the classical linear model to use regression adjustment, he writes, any more than you need to accept the Four Noble Truths to test whether meditation improves mental health.

His last paragraph is a tribute. Three-quarters of a century after Fisher invented the analysis of covariance, he writes, Freedman had deepened the understanding of it by deriving its behaviour without assuming a model, a constant effect or an infinite population, and the argument should be studied in detail. A younger Berkeley statistician had corrected Freedman's conclusions with Freedman's own machinery, and thanked him in the acknowledgements for generous help.

## Chapter Seven: The Estimand Moves

A regulator reading Freedman and Lin had to choose. If Freedman was right, a drug licence resting on an adjusted analysis rests on a model the trial cannot justify, and the safe rule is to demand the plain difference in means. If Lin was right, that rule throws away power which, in Kahan's reanalysis, would have lifted a typical trial from eighty percent to above ninety. In May 2023 the Food and Drug Administration issued a guidance for industry on adjusting for covariates in randomised trials, and it sided, by name, with Lin.

Even when the model is misspecified, the guidance says, adjustment through a linear regression is a valid method for estimating and making inference about the average treatment effect, and the citation is Lin, 2013. The printed standard errors are acceptable in two-arm trials with equal allocation, which was Freedman's own result, and otherwise a sandwich estimator should be considered. Freedman's theorem, Lin's repair and the regulator's paragraph line up almost clause for clause. Around them the guidance builds the fences Lin asked for. The unadjusted analysis remains acceptable. The covariates and the model must be specified in detail before any comparative data are unblinded, and review will emphasise that prespecified analysis over later ones. And any variables used to stratify the randomisation should generally be in the model, which is Fisher's rule from the Lanarkshire episode, analyse as you allocated, in a regulator's voice.

The guidance then moves from continuous outcomes to yes-or-no ones, and here the dining hall comes back. It gives a hypothetical trial in which half the patients carry a biomarker. Among carriers, eighty percent succeed on the new drug and a third on placebo. Among the rest, a quarter succeed on the drug and four percent on placebo. In each half, the odds ratio comes to eight. Put the halves together and the success rates are 52.5 and 18.7 percent, and the combined figure is 4.8.

Nothing is confounded here. The biomarker is equally common in both arms, as a coin would leave it on average. The drug has the same odds ratio in every subgroup, and still the figure for the whole population is smaller. The property is called non-collapsibility; odds and hazard ratios have it while the risk difference and the risk ratio do not. A logistic regression that adjusts for the biomarker reports eight. An unadjusted one reports 4.8. Both are correct. They describe different comparisons, one between a treated and an untreated patient with the same biomarker status, the other between the treated and untreated populations as a whole. So the guidance asks sponsors to say in advance which of the two they mean.

Lay the three arguments side by side now and they become one. Lord's statisticians disagreed because nobody had said what comparison was wanted, and when Pearl made the dining hall a question about sex, the baseline became a mediator and adjusting for it changed the question. Freedman's adjusted estimate could lose precision because, with unequal arms, one pooled slope corrected each arm toward the wrong place, and Lin fixed that by giving each arm its own slope, so that both adjusted means answer the question the unadjusted comparison asked. In another paper from the same year, Freedman made the same complaint about logistic regression in experiments, that its coefficient is generally the wrong estimator of the population log odds, whether or not the model is right; he suggested averaging each patient's predicted probabilities instead, and gave the practical rule, cross-tabulation before regression.

So this is what a covariate is allowed to do in a randomised trial. It may reduce the noise around a question the coin already fixed. Adjusting for September weight in a trial whose students were assigned by lottery changes the precision, not the target, and that is why the guidance can say yes to it. What a covariate may not do, unannounced, is change the question: by sitting downstream of treatment, as baseline weight sat downstream of sex, or by switching the effect from the population to the individual, as an adjusted odds ratio does. Lord's pessimism was right for groups that formed themselves and wrong for groups a coin made. The coin does not tell you which number to adjust for. It tells you that, in a linear analysis, adjusting for a number measured before it was tossed cannot move the target.

## Chapter Eight: The Case Against the Settlement

The settlement has critics on every side, and some of them are right.

Begin with Pearl, since he claimed a resolution. Holland and Rubin's view, that the answer rests on untestable assumptions, is not refuted by drawing a graph, because the graph is itself the assumption. Pearl's reply is that the graph states its assumptions in a form a scientist can argue about, which beats leaving them implicit, and that the dining-room version has a definite answer once you grant that heavier students chose the second room. Tennant's group press a different point: Pearl's both-are-right reading of the original story overlooks how ill-defined change was in it. A clean answer to an ill-posed question is still an answer to some other question. For the trialist none of this matters much, since a coin removes the arrow from baseline to treatment. For anyone analysing an observational cohort it matters a great deal, and Tennant's paper is a list of the ways the analysis of change goes wrong there.

Next, Lin's repair is asymptotic. It proves that adjustment cannot hurt in sufficiently large samples, and his own remarks admit that with a small sample or a few influential points, the sandwich standard error can be biased downward and unstable. His grade example, at a hundred and fifty-seven men, was chosen to be small and came out well, but one example is not a theorem about small trials. Freedman's simulations put noticeable bias at a hundred subjects. Neither man reports a trial of forty.

Then the guidance's scope. Freedman's theorems explicitly exclude stratified and blocked designs, and the guidance is written for parallel-group trials and never mentions clusters. A cluster trial that randomises schools or clinics and adjusts for each cluster's baseline average sits outside both: its effective sample size is the number of clusters, often twenty or forty, which puts it in the range where Freedman's bias was noticeable and Lin's guarantees have not yet arrived. The mixed models usually fitted there carry their own assumptions about how clusters vary, which the coin does not supply either. I could not find a treatment of that case with the clarity of Lin's, and the gap is real.

Finally, the strongest objection is the one Lin himself made. Prespecification answers the specification search only if the specification was made well, and nobody has a theorem for choosing the list. Senn, in the last episode, admitted the same. The guidance says to pick the covariates most strongly associated with the outcome, which assumes the trialist knows them. A list padded with plausible factors costs little, by Kahan's numbers, but a list that leaves out the strong predictor because the pilot was too small to find it leaves most of the gain behind. A reader in Freedman's mould would add that a regulator who grants permission to adjust has also, inevitably, granted a way to adjust badly with a clean conscience.

And there is the reverse case, which is easy to forget. Suppose the adjusted and unadjusted estimates differ substantially in a trial of three hundred. Freedman's rule says be cautious. Senn's dice say the adjusted figure is the honest one, because a prognostic die has been seen face up and lies further from average than the coin usually leaves it. Both are right about different things. A large gap means either a large chance imbalance on a strong predictor, in which case adjust and say so, or a model that is doing something other than correcting for chance, in which case look harder. The gap cannot tell you which. The baseline table can.

## Chapter Nine: Verdict

Lord asked how to allow for differences between groups that existed before the comparison. For groups a coin formed, the answer is now known and has been written into regulation. Adjust, in a linear model, for a short list of baseline measurements chosen before unblinding because they predict the outcome, always including the baseline value of the outcome itself if one was measured, and including whatever the randomisation was stratified on. Use the interacted model or a robust standard error when the arms are unequal. Report the unadjusted difference first, so the reader can see the hands above the table. For self-selected groups, Lord's pessimism survives in a modern form: the answer depends on a causal structure that has to be argued for, and the analysis of change is not the safe default it looks like.

The odds ratio is the open flank. In a trial with a binary outcome, the adjusted and unadjusted odds ratios are different quantities even when nothing went wrong, and the plan has to say which one the trial is about.

Reading prescription. Start with Vickers and Altman's statistics note in the BMJ, one page, which holds the acupuncture table and the sample-size row. Then Lin's paper, about twenty-five pages; read the introduction, the leaf-weight section and the last two pages, and leave the proofs for a second sitting. Freedman's 2008 paper is short and dense; its seventh section, with the simulations and the take-home message, is the part to keep. For the dining hall, read Lord's story as Pearl quotes it at the start of his 2016 report, and the Tennant preprint for the analysis of change in observational data. The FDA guidance is eight pages and reads in twenty minutes.

There is a check anyone can run on the last finished trial with a yes-or-no outcome, using the data already on file. Fit the logistic model with the strong prognostic factors and without them, and compare the two odds ratios. If the drug did anything, the adjusted one will usually sit further from one, with a wider interval, and Kahan's group explain why the adjusted test can still be the more powerful: the two numbers estimate different effects. Then average the adjusted model's predicted probabilities over all the patients, once with everyone treated and once with no one, as Freedman proposed. That gives a population effect, which Freedman's simulations found nearly unbiased, and it answers the question a reader comparing the two arms supposed the paper was asking.
