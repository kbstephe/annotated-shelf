title: "From Third World to First (Lee), Part Two: who audits a clean government"
description: "The second half of Lee Kuan Yew's memoir, read through its corruption bureau, ministers' pay, the press laws, the libel suits and detention without trial: how Singapore stayed clean, what it removed in order to do so, and whether the citizen can check the result."
---

In November 1986 an old associate of Singapore's minister for national development told anti-corruption investigators that he had handed the minister two cash payments of four hundred thousand dollars each. One was to let a development company keep land the government had earmarked for compulsory purchase. The other was to help a developer buy state land. The minister, Teh Cheang Wan, denied it, and then tried to bargain with a senior investigator to have the case dropped. He asked to see the prime minister. Lee Kuan Yew sent word that he could not see him until the investigation was over.

On the morning of the fifteenth of December, Lee's security officer reported that Teh was dead and had left a letter. Lee prints it. It is four sentences long, it accepts full responsibility, and it says that an honourable gentleman should pay the highest penalty for his mistake. Lee went to the house and saw the body. The widow asked whether a coroner's inquiry could be avoided. It could not. The inquiry found an overdose of a barbiturate, the opposition asked for a commission of inquiry, Lee agreed at once, and the widow and daughter left Singapore and never came back.

Lee writes that he never understood why Teh took the money, since he was an able architect who could have made many millions honestly in private practice.

Last time this show read the first half of From Third World to First, and found a pattern in it. In the years after 1965 the state asked its citizens for a series of surrenders, the right to strike without a ballot, a growing slice of each pay packet, and it paired each one with an asset the citizen held in his own name: a job, and then a flat with a deed. The trade could be checked at the kitchen table. This episode takes the other half of the ledger. Singapore's citizens also gave up a press that could oppose the government, an opposition that could afford to accuse it, and for some hundreds of them the right to be tried before being locked up. What Lee says they got in return is a government that did not steal. So the question is whether that is the same kind of trade. I held the chapters on corruption, the media, the opposition and the communists in full, and what follows about those comes from the pages. I read no reviews, and the people on the other side of these stories reach this episode only through Lee and, where I say so, through memory.

## Chapter One: White Shirts

Why did a government of English-educated lawyers and economists decide that honesty was a survival question and not a virtue? Lee's answer starts with his competitors. In the 1950s the Chinese-school students of Singapore were going over to the communists, and he thinks the reason was disgust: the Nationalist leaders in China had looted the country and the communists looked like people who slept in the back rooms of union offices and took the bus. The communists mocked Lee for his air-conditioned office, his Studebaker, his golf and his beer. What they could not say was that he had made money out of the unions he represented. He treats that as the one card his side held.

The chapter gives a second reason, and it concerns timing. In 1959 Lee's party was not sure it wanted to win the coming election. It expected the communists to turn on it once it governed, and it did not feel organised enough to survive that. But the sitting government had started to go bad. Its education minister had taken a million dollars from an American source to fight the election, and there was talk in the market of smaller sums changing hands for less ideological reasons. Lee's reasoning was that the civil service was still mostly honest, and that five more years under those ministers would corrupt it, after which no successor could work the machine. So they fought to win, and took their oaths in white shirts and white trousers.

The costume is the part people remember. The machinery is the part that worked. The British had left behind a Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau, founded in 1952 to deal with police, hawker inspectors and land bailiffs, the officials whose job was to issue a summons or look away. Lee's government made two decisions about it. It pointed the bureau at the big takers. And for the small ones it changed the job instead of chasing the man: clear published guidelines, fewer permits, and in minor matters no permit at all. An inspector with nothing to decide has nothing to sell.

Then, in 1960, it rewrote the law of evidence. A gratuity now meant anything of value. Investigators could open the bank books of a suspect, his wife, his children and his agents. The tax office had to answer their questions. A court could accept the word of an accomplice. And the change Lee calls the most effective: proof that an official was living beyond his means, or held property his salary could not explain, could be treated as corroboration that he had taken a bribe.

A bribe is a transaction between two people who both want it hidden, so a system that waits for a complaint will wait a long time. A house is hard to hide. The rule moves the check from the act, which nobody will report, to the outcome, which the neighbours can see. Andy Grove's advice in High Output Management was to pair every indicator with a second one that catches the gaming of the first, and this is the same idea applied to a civil servant: the salary is one number, the standard of living is the other, and the law now lets a judge read the gap. In 1971 the bureau broke a ring of more than two hundred and fifty policemen in the mobile squad who were collecting between five and ten dollars a month from lorry owners, whose trucks they knew by the addresses painted on the sides. Owners who did not pay were buried in summonses. A sum that small leaves no mark on any single ledger. Two hundred and fifty men with small unexplained comforts is a pattern.

Lee says the organised rackets were the easy part. The hard cases were single opportunistic acts by people near the top, and the chapter turns to those, because a rule about living beyond your means only matters if somebody is willing to apply it to a friend.

## Chapter Two: Four Friends

He gives four cases, and three of the men he had known since the union days of the 1950s.

The first is the oddest, because nobody was convicted. Tan Kia Gan had been a minister until he lost his seat in 1963, and the government then put him on the board of the airline it shared with Malaysia. At a board meeting in August 1966 he objected strongly to buying Boeing aircraft. A few days later a business friend of his approached Boeing's bankers offering his services, for a consideration. The bank knew the government's reputation and reported the approach. The friend would not implicate Tan, and without that there could be no prosecution. Lee writes that he was convinced Tan was behind it. So he issued a public statement that Tan had not discharged his duties beyond reproach, and removed him from the board and from every other appointment. A colleague later reported that Tan was down at heel and ostracised. Lee says he was sad and saw no other course.

The second, in 1975, was a minister of state who took a family holiday in Indonesia paid for by a housing developer, then a bungalow worth half a million dollars, then overdrafts guaranteed by the same man. He was convicted and jailed. The third, in 1979, was the president of the national trade union congress, a member of Parliament for Lee's party, charged with criminal breach of trust over eighty-three thousand dollars. The fourth was Teh.

The third case has a scene in it. The union congress's secretary-general, Devan Nair, believed his colleague was innocent and said so vehemently to Lee over a Saturday lunch: an innocent man was being destroyed. Lee had seen the investigation reports. He picked up the telephone at the table, rang the director of the bureau, and told him to show Nair the evidence, in confidence, straight after lunch. Nair read it and did not raise the subject again. The accused jumped bail, and Lee notes that the two men who stood surety lost fifty thousand dollars.

The system worked: the prime minister did not protect his party's union chief, and he let a sceptical friend see why. Look at how it worked, though. The director of the bureau operated from the Prime Minister's Office, and was one telephone call away from that lunch table. Tan Kia Gan lost his career without a trial because Lee was convinced. In every one of these cases the check functioned because the man at the top wanted it to. A clinician would ask what the test cannot see, and here the blind spot has a clear outline: the bureau can see every officer and every minister, and it reports to the one office nobody else is placed to investigate.

Lee knows this. The rest of his corruption chapter is an attempt to answer it, and the first part of his answer is about money.

## Chapter Three: The Price of a Minister

His theory of why Asian governments rot is an accountant's theory. Elections cost money; the winner must recover the outlay and fund the next campaign; so office becomes a business. He gives the prices as he knew them in the 1990s. Some candidates for Taiwan's legislature were spending ten to twenty million American dollars. A Japanese member of the Diet needed over a million a year to keep up his staff and his gifts for constituents' weddings and funerals, and several times that in an election year, which is why he depended on a faction boss. A Thai prime minister was nicknamed after a cash machine.

Singapore's elections were cheap, and Lee credits two rules he had pressed for in 1959 as leader of the opposition: voting was made compulsory, and candidates were forbidden to ferry voters to the polls by car. If everyone must vote and nobody can be driven, there is not much left to buy. The party spent well under the legal limit and had no debts to clear afterwards.

That removes the need to steal. The next step is the one that made him unpopular: remove the excuse. After independence he froze ministers' salaries. In 1970, with unemployment gone, he raised them and kept his own pay below theirs. Then for twenty years he watched the private sector pull away. His example is a banker he recruited in 1978 from a job whose scale would have reached nearly a million dollars a year, to become a minister of state on less than a third of that, without the car and driver. In 1994, as senior minister, he proposed a formula, and the following year the government adopted it: ministers, judges and top civil servants would be paid two-thirds of what their private equivalents declared on their tax returns. Lee reports an enormous stir, especially among professionals.

His defence comes with an image. For decades in Mao's China everyone wore the same jacket. A provincial official explained to one of Lee's ministers that the jackets only looked alike, and unbuttoned his own to show the fur lining. Lee's claim is that every government pays its leaders the market rate; the only choice is whether the payment is a salary anyone can read or a house, a car, a staff and a retirement villa that nobody totals. He preferred one lump sum in the open.

And then he says something that undercuts his own legend, which is why I believe him. The founding ministers, he writes, were honest by habit. They had risked jail to take power and were not in it for money. That group could not be replicated, because the conditions that made them could not be recreated. Their successors would be people for whom a ministry was one career option among several and not the best paid. He is telling you that the clean government of the first twenty-five years rested on character, that character was an accident of history, and that he was now trying to replace it with a pay scale.

He added a second replacement. In 1984 he proposed an elected president with the power to guard the reserves, to veto appointments such as chief justice and police commissioner, and to override a prime minister who held up a corruption investigation into himself or his ministers. It became law in 1992. Read that against the lunch with Devan Nair. Lee had seen the hole in his own design, the bureau that reports to the office it cannot investigate, and he tried to close it with another official.

One more story belongs here because he tells it as proof. In 1995 his wife, acting for him, and his son, then deputy prime minister, each bought a property from a developer at a discount of five to seven per cent. The developer had given the same terms to a small share of buyers at an early launch, prices then jumped, and Lee's brother sat on the developer's board. A rumour started. The prime minister ordered an investigation, the monetary authority found nothing improper, and Lee asked for a full debate in Parliament, where opposition lawyers agreed such discounts were normal. Father and son gave the value of the discounts, a million dollars, to charity. Lee's conclusion is that a system able to investigate its own founder is impersonal and effective.

It is a fair point as far as it goes. But list the checks so far: a bureau under the prime minister, a president vetted for the job, an investigation ordered by Lee's successor, a debate in a Parliament where his party held almost every seat. Each one sits inside the government. The checks that sit outside a government are usually a press, an opposition and a court. So what happened to those?

## Chapter Four: Birds of Passage

Lee's view of the press was formed before he held power, and the book is candid that it was formed by a grudge. In the 1959 election the main English paper, the Straits Times, British-owned, campaigned hard against him. He threatened openly to settle scores if he won, and called its expatriate editors birds of passage who would fly to Kuala Lumpur and declare from there their readiness to die for press freedom in Singapore. The paper's senior local editor replied in print that he was no bird of passage and that his home would be in Singapore whatever happened. He left for Kuala Lumpur before polling day. After independence the paper came back and supported the government, and Lee remarks that this did not increase his respect for it.

From that he drew a theory: press freedom was the freedom of owners to advance their own interests. A British company promoted British commerce. The Chinese papers belonged to rich merchant families and sold China to their readers. So the question to ask of any newspaper was who was paying.

Twice the answer was damning. A paper called the Eastern Sun, had taken a loan of three million dollars from an agency of the People's Republic based in Hong Kong, at an interest rate of 0.1 per cent, on the understanding that it would not oppose Beijing on major issues. When the government exposed this in 1971 the owner admitted it, the editorial staff resigned in humiliation, and the paper closed. The Singapore Herald was foreign-owned through a Hong Kong partnership with dummy names, ran through its capital, and was then kept alive by nearly two million dollars in unsecured loans from the local branch of Chase Manhattan. Lee pressed for an explanation and got a telephone call from David Rockefeller, who said the branch manager had been unaware of the bank's rule against lending to newspapers. Lee says he was sceptical. He cancelled the Herald's printing licence and then flew to Helsinki, where the International Press Institute was meeting, to say to the assembled editors that the freedom of the news media had to be subordinate to the needs of Singapore and the purpose of an elected government.

The single witness shows at this point. The media chapter tells the Eastern Sun and the Herald at length, with loan terms. It does not tell a third story from the same months. For that you have to go back to the chapter on language policy, where one sentence, which I read on the page, records that when the big Chinese daily, the Nanyang Siang Pau, attacked the government for suppressing Chinese education, the government arrested its general manager, its editor-in-chief and its senior editorial writer. The offence given is glamorising communism and stirring up chauvinist feeling. No foreign loan is alleged. The two cases with a paper trail go in the chapter on the press; the case with three arrests and no paper trail goes somewhere else.

The permanent settlement came in 1977. No person could hold more than three per cent of a newspaper's ordinary shares. A special class of management shares was created, the minister decided who held them, and he gave them to the four big local banks, which Lee says would stay politically neutral because their business depended on stability. Nobody is censored. The ownership is arranged so that the people with the final say are people with every reason never to use it against the government.

## Chapter Five: The Right of Reply

The foreign press could not be handled through share registers, and the government devised a different method for it.

A 1986 law let the government restrict the circulation of any foreign publication that engaged in Singapore's domestic politics. The test of engaging was specific: having printed something the government said was wrong, did the publication refuse to print the government's reply? The first case was Time magazine, that October, over a report on an opposition member's conviction. Lee's press secretary sent a letter correcting what he said were errors of fact. Time offered edited versions. The press secretary wanted it unedited. Time refused, and its permitted sale was cut in two steps from eighteen thousand copies to two thousand. Time printed the letter in full. The restriction stayed on for eight more months.

The Asian Wall Street Journal went from five thousand to four hundred after it declined to print a rebuttal from the monetary authority. What happened next is a small comedy that Lee enjoys telling. The Journal offered to give the paper free to its cut-off subscribers, in a spirit of service to businessmen. The ministry agreed, provided the copies carried no advertisements, to prove that the motive was information and not advertising rates. The Journal said removing them would cost too much. The ministry offered to pay half. The Journal declined, and the ministry wrote back that what it wanted was the freedom to sell advertisements. Asiaweek, the Far Eastern Economic Review and, in 1993, the Economist each took a turn.

The case for the method first. Nothing was banned. Lee points out that a reader who could not buy a restricted magazine could photocopy or fax it, so the reports still circulated; only the advertising revenue fell. The demand itself, print our answer and let readers judge, is hard to object to, and the government published the full correspondence each time. One magazine had taken an official's letter, cut it, added more than four hundred and seventy words of its own, and run the result under his name. If you believe, as Lee did, that a correspondent with a slant should have to face the facts, this is a civilised way to make him.

Now look at who decides. The government determines that an article was wrong. It writes the correction. The correction must run unedited. Refusal is punished by that same government, on a sliding scale it controls, and the restriction can outlast compliance by eight months or a year, as a reminder. The mechanism has the form of a debate and the structure of a licence. Lee's own summary is that when the foreign press found that if they twisted his arm he could tweak their noses, biased reporting became less frequent. What else grew rarer the book cannot say, because the only one counting is the party with the hand on the nose.

## Chapter Six: The Witness Box

With opponents at home the instrument was the libel suit, and Lee's defence of it is the passage I would most like to hear cross-examined.

His theory runs like this. In much of Asia, accusations of corruption are thrown at every election and never answered, because a minister who sued would have to survive cross-examination. Lee always sued. He took advice from counsel in Singapore and in London first, since he would carry the costs if he lost, and he went into the witness box himself; in the suit against the Far Eastern Economic Review he was questioned by the other side's barrister for more than two days, and the editor then declined to testify. Each suit, on this account, is a voluntary audit. He is laying his finances open to hostile counsel, and nobody has ever sued him, because he says nothing he cannot prove. He adds that the damages went to charity.

Then he lists the defendants, and the list reads differently from the theory. In 1972 opposition candidates who had accused him at rallies were sued; most, he writes, were men without assets who did not defend themselves and accepted bankruptcy. J. B. Jeyaretnam, a lawyer and for years the only opposition member of Parliament, was sued over a 1976 rally and again over one in 1988, and lost both. A candidate named Tang Liang Hong was sued in 1996 and left the country. An American lecturer wrote in the International Herald Tribune of regimes that rely on a compliant judiciary to bankrupt opposition politicians, without naming Singapore. Lee sued him for that sentence, and the lecturer left too.

Lee's reading of each departure is that the accuser dared not face a court. He applies it to the editor of 1959, to Tang and to the lecturer. Run together they make a different pattern. Albert Hirschman's old distinction was that a member of a declining organisation can use voice, which means staying to complain, or exit, which means leaving, and that where leaving is easy the complainers go first and voice dries up. Singapore under Lee made voice expensive by law and left the airport open. The people most willing to accuse the government were, one by one, converted into people living abroad, and each conversion was entered in the record as evidence that the accusation had been empty.

Then there is the man who stayed. Lee calls Jeyaretnam all sound and fury, sloppy on facts, and then explains why that suited him. A weak opponent was a useful sparring partner for young ministers who had never fought communists. And he occupied the opposition's space, and in Lee's words, "probably kept better men out". An author who writes that sentence is not hiding anything. He is describing an opposition managed like an ecosystem, with a tolerated species filling the niche.

How did bad news reach a government with almost no opposition? Lee had learned from the communists that elections are won by networks, not walkabouts, so he built one: community centres, citizens' committees that spent small public-works money, and in the new housing estates a residents' committee for every six to ten blocks, the whole chain leading, as he puts it, to the Prime Minister's Office as nerve centre. It doubled as the state's ear. When the party lost a by-election in 1981, in a constituency called Anson, the cause Lee identifies was a group of port workers moved out of their flats with no replacement housing because two agencies each blamed the other, and what disturbed him was that no warning had reached him. That is a real feedback system, and it runs upward through the party's own people. In 1997 the government added a rule that public housing in a constituency would be upgraded in order of its support for the ruling party, and Lee shrugs that Americans know pork when they see it. The flat from part one, the asset in the citizen's name, had become a lever.

## Chapter Seven: Without Trial

The last surrender is the one with no asset attached at all.

The chapter on the communists opens in Changi prison in November 1965, with Lim Chin Siong. He had been the most gifted of the left's leaders, once Lee's own party colleague, and was held without trial since a security sweep in 1963. That morning the superintendent found him trembling and dishevelled, saying his fellow detainees would beat or poison him. Two days later, in hospital, he hanged himself with his pyjamas from a cistern and was cut down and revived. Lee's chapter title says the communists self-destructed, and he reads the episode as a man broken by his own side's quarrels. In 1969 Lim asked to see him, renounced politics and left for London. When he died in 1996 Lee wrote his widow an open letter of respect for his honesty and dedication.

Lee does not pretend detention was incidental. He asks the question himself: could the communists have been beaten if the government had allowed habeas corpus and given up detention without trial? He doubts it, because nobody dared give evidence against them in open court. Thousands were held in Malaya and hundreds in Singapore. In the 1950s the grip of the underground on the Chinese-speaking population was real, and he describes meeting its leader by candlelight in an unfinished flat. This is his strongest ground, and for the fifties and early sixties it has force: a movement that kills witnesses cannot be tried by witness.

The trouble is how long the instrument outlived the emergency. One detainee, Chia Thye Poh, was held for more than twenty years, by the book's own count, then confined to a resort island in 1989 and freed of all restrictions only in 1998. He denied to the end that he was a communist. Lee says security officers had it from party members that he was. And Lee writes that keeping him, in spite of Western pressure, served to discourage other cadres from reviving their cause. That is detention as a message to third parties, stated by the man who ordered it.

Then 1987, twenty-two years after separation. The security department recommended detaining a group of English-educated activists, church workers and lawyers among them, as an incipient problem. Lee accepted. His stated worry was that a couple of cadres abroad would rebuild their influence using people he himself describes as innocent but disaffected. That same year a former solicitor-general who had become president of the Law Society, Francis Seow, was detained and interrogated over contacts with an American diplomat; the government published the admissions he swore to under interrogation, released him before the election, and he lost and later left for the United States. I know from memory, and have not held the documents, that several of the 1987 detainees afterwards said their statements had been coerced, and that Seow wrote his own account of the interrogation. The book mentions neither.

What Lee offers in place of evidence is a vote. When an American human rights official urged him in 1978 to abolish the detention law, he answered that the opposition had campaigned against it at every election and the voters had backed the government every time. If Washington would underwrite Singapore the way it did Puerto Rico, he would take her advice. It is also an argument that citizens who have never been detained approve of detaining those who have, in elections reported by a press whose controlling shares the minister allocates.

## Chapter Eight: The Deed Nobody Can Read

Give him the results first. The rankings he cites, a business school's survey that scored Singapore 9.18 out of ten in 1997 and an index that placed it seventh in the world the next year, measure what businessmen experience, and what they experienced was a customs officer who did not need paying. That is no small thing. Lee is also right about his neighbours: the free and noisy presses of India, the Philippines, Thailand and Japan had not stopped corruption there. A free press is not sufficient. He has shown that. He has not shown it is unnecessary, and the difference is the whole second half of his book.

Go back to the trade from part one. A worker gave up the unballoted strike and got a factory job. He gave up a fifth of his wages and got a flat. He could see the job and read the deed. Now run the same test on this half. The citizen gives up a press that might catch a minister, an opposition that might accuse one, and the certainty of a trial. He receives clean government. How does he know he has received it?

He knows because the government tells him, through the bureau's prosecutions, the published correspondence, the libel verdicts, the parliamentary debate on Lee's property discounts. Every item of proof is an audit the government ran on itself, in a forum it chose, and then made public. I think the audits were real. Four ministers fell, and one of them is dead. But the instruments by which a citizen could have checked independently are the very things he handed over. The asset is registered in his name and kept in the issuer's safe.

Lee's own pages supply one more piece of evidence before the verdict. He reversed a 1984 policy giving graduate mothers priority in school places only after the women it favoured protested and an election went badly, and at Anson, the by-election with the port workers, the bad news reached him by losing. His system corrected itself when voters could still hurt it. Each lever in these chapters made that a little harder.

That is a different trade from the flat, and Lee half says so. He writes that the founders' honesty could not be reproduced. He builds a salary formula and an elected president against the day a dishonest group wins office, a freak election in his phrase. So he believed the arrangement was only as good as the people at its top, and his remedy was more insiders. An outside check would have survived a bad prime minister. He never restored one. The design he left is safe for exactly as long as it does not need to be.

To his credit the last word in the politics chapter is a doubt. Will the system he and his colleagues built work unchanged for another generation? He doubts it.

## Chapter Nine: Reading It

Read the corruption chapter complete and before anything else: statute, four cases, the theory of election costs, the pay formula. Follow it at once with the media chapter, and then turn back to the single sentence on the Nanyang Siang Pau in the pages on language policy to see what was left out. The chapter called Straddling the Middle Ground holds the libel suits and the committee network. The one on the communists is short and is the place to decide how far you trust him. Read these four as testimony from the defendant who is also the only witness called, which does not make it false. The chapters on foreign leaders, more than half the book, can wait for another year.

Clinical research has been through this argument, and it settled it against Lee. The checks on a study fall into two piles: those that need the investigator's consent to begin, and those that do not. An internal data review is in the first pile. An analysis plan registered before unblinding is in the second, because any stranger may lay it beside the paper, and so is a safety board that sees the unblinded numbers on its own schedule. The field moved things into the second pile after concluding that honest investigators were not a design. Lee's Singapore kept nearly everything in the first, and it stayed clean while he was there to make the telephone call.

Next time, Admiral Hyman Rickover and the nuclear navy: the man who interviewed every officer himself and insisted that responsibility carry a name. Thanks for listening.
