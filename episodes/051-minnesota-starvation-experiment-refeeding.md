## Chapter One: A Recruiting Poster With an Honest Title

In the autumn of 1944, camps across the country that housed conscientious objectors doing alternative wartime service began circulating a brochure with a title that did not soften what it was asking. "Will You Starve That They Be Better Fed?" It was not a metaphor. A University of Minnesota physiologist named Ancel Keys was looking for men willing to be starved, on a controlled schedule, under medical observation, for close to a year, so that the country planning to feed a liberated but famine-struck Europe and Asia would know what starvation actually does to a human body and what it takes to undo it. Four hundred men wrote back. One of them, a young objector named Neil Hartman, explained the pull in terms that had nothing to do with nutrition science: "I was young and I wanted to show that I was not a coward. So when they offered me this chance of being a guinea pig, it fit right in with my scheme of things of proving that I was willing to take risks on my own body, but I just did not want to kill someone else."

That sentence names the paradox this hour is going to keep circling. These were men who had refused, at real personal and social cost, to put their bodies at risk for their country in combat. Many of them were routinely called cowards to their faces by neighbors and even strangers for that refusal. And a large share of the four hundred who volunteered for a yearlong hunger study did so, by their own account, in order to prove that the refusal had never been about fear of pain or risk at all. Keys and his staff narrowed the pool to about a hundred men, examined them for physical and psychological stability, and settled on thirty-six: all men, all in their early twenties to early thirties, fifteen from the Historic Peace Churches, Mennonite, Brethren, and Quaker, the rest a mix of other Protestant traditions, one Jewish volunteer, and two with no declared religion at all. On November nineteenth, 1944, they moved into a laboratory built into the south stands of the university's football stadium, entered through Gate twenty-seven, and began. The space itself was a converted maze of offices, kitchens, and testing rooms tucked under the stands where, ten Saturdays a year, a very different kind of Minnesota endurance was being cheered on overhead; underneath, through a Minnesota winter and the spring and summer that followed, thirty-six men would spend most of a year finding out how much of their own bodies they could give up and still keep functioning.

## Chapter Two: Why a War Department Wanted to Know How to Feed the Starving

The study existed because a specific, dated problem was coming and nobody yet knew how to solve it. By early 1944 it was clear that when Allied forces advanced across Europe and into Asia, they would find millions of civilians, and eventually liberated concentration camp survivors and prisoners of war, in advanced starvation, and that feeding them back to health badly could kill as many as feeding them well would save. Ancel Keys was thirty-nine years old that year, already an unusual figure even among wartime scientists: a Berkeley-trained physiologist and oceanographer who had run a fatigue-research laboratory at Harvard before Minnesota hired him in 1937 to build the university's own Laboratory of Physiological Hygiene, and the inventor, at the Army's request in 1941, of a compact, shelf-stable combat ration that soldiers came to call the K-ration, for the initial of his own name. Colleagues who worked under him described a demanding, restless man who ran his laboratory the way he had run his own physical training, certain of his own judgment and impatient with anyone who could not keep pace with it, a temperament that would later carry him, for better and for worse, through decades of public argument over what diet actually does to a heart. In 1944 that same certainty pointed at a single, practical target. He proposed a study that would answer two questions with the same population: what does prolonged semi-starvation do to the body and mind, and what is the most efficient way to reverse it. The funding ran through the Office of Scientific Research and Development's Committee on Medical Research, channeled via the Surgeon General's office, the same wartime contracting body this shelf met two episodes into the Collison series, there scaling Pfizer's penicillin fermentation under A. N. Richards in time for D-Day. Vannevar Bush's contract-instead-of-conscription machine that built radar and the proximity fuze also built the science behind postwar famine relief, one more wartime bet that a civilian scientist with a clear question could move faster than an existing bureaucracy built for something else.

The intended customer for the results was UNRRA, the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration, the agency about to be handed the actual job of feeding a continent. An aid worker with a truck full of donated food and no data faces an unforgiving question: give the starving too little and recovery drags on for months longer than it needs to; give them too much, too fast, and the refeeding itself can kill. Nobody in 1944 had a rigorous answer to how much food a starved body actually needed to recover, how fast it could safely take it, or what mix of nutrients mattered most. That gap is what thirty-six men in a stadium basement were about to spend a year closing.

## Chapter Three: A Different Shape of Consent Than the Last One This Shelf Examined

This shelf's last episode closed on a question about consent: whether a Mississippi prison pardon, offered to eleven convicts in exchange for months on a diet designed to produce pellagra, was ever free enough to count as real agreement, given how little the men had to bargain with and how much they stood to gain. The Minnesota volunteers present, on the surface, close to the opposite case. Nobody offered them early release from anything. Civilian Public Service was not a fixed sentence with a defined end date that participation could shorten; it ran for the duration of the war, and volunteering for Keys's laboratory did not buy any of these men a day off that clock. It simply became their assigned CPS work, in place of whatever forestry, firefighting, or psychiatric-ward labor they would otherwise have been doing. There was no pardon on the table, no reduced term, nothing structurally equivalent to what Governor Brewer offered the Rankin prisoners. What these men chose, they chose without anyone dangling their freedom in front of them as the price of agreement.

That is a real and important difference, and it should be stated as plainly as the Rankin story's coercion was. But a 2021 doctoral history of conscientious objectors as research subjects, written under the pointed title "Compelled to Volunteer," complicates the picture without erasing that difference. The choice these men were making was never simply free-floating idealism. Selective Service law gave them exactly two paths once they registered as objectors, noncombatant military service or civilian work of national importance, and men who chose the second path did so inside a culture that routinely called them yellow-bellies and cowards to their faces, sometimes with real hostility attached: objectors in some towns were spat on, thrown out of shops and diners, or had their homes vandalized by neighbors who read pacifism, in wartime, as an accusation against everyone who had not refused to go. Volunteering for a study advertised as heroic, physically dangerous, and useful to the war effort was, for a share of these men, a way of answering that accusation on the only terrain their convictions allowed them to use: their own bodies, freely offered, rather than someone else's, taken by force. The choice was voluntary in the specific, meaningful sense that Rankin's convicts' choice was not: no one was released early, no sentence was shortened, nothing scarce and desperately wanted was traded for compliance. It was also, for at least some of the volunteers, a choice shaped by social pressure sharp enough to leave men feeling they had something to prove. Both of those things are true about the same four hundred men at once, and the study's own record does not ask which one of them was the real reason anybody signed the brochure.

## Chapter Four: Three Phases, and a Diet Built to Resemble a Real Famine

The protocol ran in three stages. Twelve weeks of a control diet, roughly thirty-two hundred calories daily, brought every man to his individually calculated ideal body weight before anything else began, giving Keys's team a clean, personal baseline for every measurement that would follow rather than a population average borrowed from someone else's chart. Then, starting in February 1945, twenty-four weeks of semi-starvation: roughly fifteen hundred seventy calories a day, built deliberately around the foods a hungry wartime European household would actually have been eating rather than a nutritionally optimized deficit, heavy on potatoes, rutabagas, turnips, dark bread, and macaroni, thin on meat, milk, eggs, and fat. Each man also had to walk twenty-two miles a week and hold down whatever CPS work assignment he had carried into the study, a requirement built into the design on purpose rather than left to chance: UNRRA's own future clients would be refugees and displaced laborers still walking to relief stations and still working fields and rail lines while underfed, not patients resting in a ward bed, so the deficit in this study landed on bodies that were still being asked to move and labor, the way a real famine's victims are rarely given the mercy of bed rest. By the end of the twenty-four weeks, the average man had lost close to a quarter of his body weight.

Every week the men filled out diaries, took the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, an instrument invented on the same campus only a year or two before the study began, and submitted to a battery of physiological measures under the psychologist Josef Brozek, who ran the study's mental half with the same rigor Keys brought to the physical one. The design gave the study something the era's medicine rarely had: a controlled, continuously measured record of exactly what a human body and mind do, week by week, as real hunger sets in, rather than a retrospective account pieced together after the fact from famine survivors too sick or too traumatized to be reliable narrators of their own decline.

## Chapter Five: What Six Months of Hunger Does to a Body That Keeps Working

The numbers the study produced read, in retrospect, like a catalogue of everything a body sacrifices to survive a caloric deficit it cannot escape. Basal metabolic rate fell by roughly forty percent, the body shedding something close to six hundred calories a day worth of its own baseline furnace simply to stretch what little fuel it was getting. Resting heart rate, which had averaged around fifty-five beats a minute at the study's start, fell in some men as low as twenty-eight, a rate that would earn an urgent cardiology consult in any hospital today, produced here not by disease but by a body economizing on its own pump. Heart size itself shrank by roughly a sixth. Blood volume dropped by about a tenth. Edema crept into ankles and faces as the body's protein economy buckled. Cold tolerance collapsed almost entirely: men who had shrugged off Minnesota winters in shirtsleeves a year earlier now layered every piece of clothing they owned indoors, watched their lips and fingernails turn faintly blue, and counted a hot shower among the actual highlights of a week that otherwise offered almost nothing to look forward to. Anemia set in as blood-cell production slowed along with everything else, and polyuria, an increased need to urinate, joined the list of complaints, one more small tax a starving body levies on itself while it strips every system down to what survival still requires.

None of this required a laboratory to predict in outline; starvation's physical toll had been observed, in uncontrolled form, in every famine and siege in recorded history. What the study added was exactness: a number attached to each collapse, measured against a known starting point in the same bodies, rather than inferred after the fact from whatever survivors happened to be examined once relief finally arrived. A relief worker in 1946 could now be told, with real confidence rather than a guess, roughly how much a starved adult's resting pulse, blood volume, and heat tolerance would have fallen by a given point on the same curve these thirty-six men had already walked.

## Chapter Six: The Mind Comes Apart on a Schedule Its Own Diary Can Track

The psychological record is the part of the study that outlived its original famine-relief purpose and found a second, much longer career. Brozek's MMPI scores showed steadily rising depression, hysteria, and hypochondriasis as the weeks of restriction wore on, in men who had screened as psychologically stable and resilient before the study ever began. Apathy set in alongside the depression: several men reported losing interest in activities, friendships, and plans that had mattered to them months earlier, replaced by a narrowing of attention that had exactly one focus. That focus was food, and the diaries the men kept describe an obsession that the researchers had not designed the study to produce and could not have talked the men out of once it arrived. One volunteer, Carlyle Frederick, began collecting cookbooks and recipes and had accumulated close to a hundred of them by the study's end, though he had shown no particular interest in cooking before it started. Another wrote, at two in the morning partway through the restriction phase: "Stayed up until five a.m. last night studying cookbooks. They are so absorbing I can't stay away from them." Harold Blickenstaff, reflecting on the experience decades afterward, said there was little else in his own life he had looked forward to being finished with quite as much, and that the trouble was never really "the physical discomfort" alone; it was what six months of hunger had done to his own sense of what mattered.

Two men were dismissed for breaking the diet in secret, caught eating food they had smuggled past the study's own controls. Two more were hospitalized for acute psychiatric symptoms and withdrawn from the study's data, their breakdowns severe enough that Brozek's team judged continued participation unsafe. The single most extreme incident belonged to Samuel Legg, who, during the refeeding phase that followed the worst of the restriction, brought an axe down on his own hand while splitting wood and amputated three of his own fingers. He was never certain, telling an interviewer decades later, whether he had done it on purpose or not; "I admit to being crazy mixed up at the time," was as far as he would commit to an explanation. What happened next says as much about the state hunger had left him in as the injury itself: walking home from treatment, he described feeling unexpectedly, disproportionately elated, stopping at soda fountain after soda fountain along the way, joking with the counter staff, finding the streetlights unusually beautiful, and later telling researchers the world had briefly felt like a wonderful place to be in. A body and mind that had spent months rationing every available resource toward bare survival, confronted with sudden crisis and sudden attention, produced a reaction that belonged to neither ordinary grief nor ordinary relief. Of the thirty-six men who began the semi-starvation phase, thirty-two reached its end with usable data intact, a completion rate that would be unremarkable for a study of this length and severity and was, in this case, closer to a testament to the men's own endurance than to any leniency in the protocol they had agreed to.

## Chapter Seven: A Name for the Pattern, Decades Before Anyone Coined It

The condition the diaries were documenting did not have a name in 1945. Researchers studying eating disorders in the following decades gave it one retroactively, semi-starvation neurosis, once clinicians treating anorexia nervosa noticed that a large share of its psychological features, the food obsession, the irritability, the narrowed emotional range, the social withdrawal, the loss of libido, looked less like a primary psychiatric illness and more like exactly what the Minnesota men's diaries had already described in otherwise healthy volunteers with no eating disorder at all. That recognition mattered clinically in a specific, useful way: it meant a share of what looked like the psychopathology driving anorexia might actually be the consequence of the starvation itself, a distinction with real implications for whether the first treatment priority is a patient's psychology or a patient's weight. The study these thirty-six men produced by accident, aimed originally at postwar Europe, ended up reshaping how clinicians understood a disease none of the study's designers had been thinking about at all. It underwrites a treatment argument still active in eating-disorder programs today: that weight restoration has to come first and psychotherapy second, because a brain running on fifteen hundred calories cannot do the psychological work a talking treatment asks of it, whatever the patient's own insight or motivation looks like on a good day. A healthy, well-screened volunteer population produced that argument decades before any clinician trying to treat a starving anorexic patient had the data to make it with confidence.

## Chapter Eight: Four Ways to Come Back, and a Body That Did Not Much Care Which

The rehabilitation phase, the part of the study this hour's own sign-off promised as its real subject, ran twelve weeks and split the surviving volunteers into four groups, each allowed a different increment above the semi-starvation baseline: an extra four hundred calories a day, eight hundred, twelve hundred, or sixteen hundred, layered on top of roughly fifteen hundred seventy. Two of the four groups received that increment mostly as bulk carbohydrate, potatoes, bread, and cereal grains; the other two received more of it as protein and fat, butter, cheese, and meat. Nobody told the men which group they were in, and, in a detail the researchers noted with some surprise, not one of them guessed correctly that he was in the highest-calorie group, even the men actually receiving it; several assumed they had been placed among the most restricted, unable to feel, from the inside, the difference between a diet that was merely inadequate and one that was actively generous by the study's own standard. Hunger this deep apparently erases a person's ability to judge, from the inside, how much they are actually being fed.

What the four-way comparison found mattered more for its shape than for any single number. Total calories, not the split between carbohydrate and protein, drove almost all of the difference in how quickly strength, mood, and physical measures recovered. The men on the smallest increment barely improved at all across the full twelve weeks, some actually losing further ground before the study's staff raised their ration; those on the largest recovered noticeably faster, and the protein-heavy diets offered little advantage over the carbohydrate-heavy ones at a matched calorie count. That result, that the quantity of food mattered more than its specific composition, would sit oddly against the next few decades of nutrition science, which spent a long stretch chasing the opposite assumption about which nutrient a hungry population needed most, a mistake this shelf will meet directly in its next episode. Twelve of the original volunteers, three drawn from each of the four groups, stayed on for an additional eight weeks of unrestricted eating once the eight controlled weeks ended, and even they took longer than anyone on the research team had expected to feel psychologically normal again: appetite and body weight came back well ahead of mood, energy, and the food obsession the diaries had spent six months documenting, a gap between the body's recovery and the mind's that the rehabilitation groups' careful calorie counting could not close by design alone.

## Chapter Nine: The Same Refeeding, Run Without a Laboratory Attached

Six years after Keys closed his study, a very different account of coming back from starvation reached the medical literature, and it showed what happens when nobody controls the pace. In 1951, a physician named Schnitker and colleagues published detailed case records from roughly eight thousand Japanese soldiers found in an advanced state of starvation at the end of the war, a subset of twenty-four studied closely, most having lost close to forty percent of their original body weight, considerably more than the Minnesota men had been allowed to lose. Refed on a high-calorie, high-vitamin diet as fast as their bodies could take it, without the graduated staging Keys had built into his own protocol, five of that closely studied group died during what should have been the recovery period, their hearts, livers, and pancreases found at autopsy to have atrophied under the starvation and then failed outright under the sudden metabolic demand of rapid refeeding. The mechanism at work, a catastrophic shift of phosphate and other electrolytes into cells suddenly flooded with glucose and insulin, would not get a name of its own for another thirty years, when Weinsier and Krumdieck coined refeeding syndrome in 1981. Keys's own study never produced a death of that kind, not because his team understood the underlying chemistry any better than Schnitker's did, but because a protocol built around small, graduated calorie increments and close daily medical observation happened to avoid the exact failure mode a faster, less staged refeeding walked straight into. The two studies, six years and one ocean apart, amount to a natural experiment in how much a hunger cure can kill if it arrives too fast, run on two different populations by two different sets of doctors who were not comparing notes, one of them lucky enough to have built caution into the design before anyone could name what the caution was protecting against.

## Chapter Ten: The Same Logic, Formalized, in Every Feeding Center Treating a Starving Child Today

That lesson did eventually get formalized, and it is still in daily use. The World Health Organization's current protocol for treating a severely malnourished child begins with a starter formula called F-75, deliberately low in protein and calories, seventy-five kilocalories per hundred milliliters, designed to stabilize a body too fragile for anything richer without risking the exact cardiac and metabolic collapse Schnitker's patients suffered. Only after several days of that cautious starting phase does treatment shift to a catch-up formula, F-100, built to deliver enough calories and protein to drive rapid weight gain once the body can actually tolerate the load, or to the peanut-paste ready-to-use therapeutic food that has largely replaced hospital-based F-100 feeding over the last two decades, energy-dense enough at roughly five hundred forty-five kilocalories per hundred grams that a child can eat toward recovery at home rather than staying admitted for weeks. Observational work following children through the actual transition from F-75 to that take-home paste, in hospital wards in Uganda among other settings, keeps returning to the same practical question Keys's rehabilitation groups were built to answer: how much, how fast, and in what form, before the transition itself becomes the risk rather than the cure. It is Keys's own four-group insight, worked out with cookbooks and blinded rations in a stadium basement eighty years ago, running today under a WHO protocol number rather than a laboratory report: introduce a starved body back to food gradually, in a sequence, rather than all at once, because the sequence itself is part of what keeps a starving patient alive through their own rescue. This is the corner of nutrition science that runs closest to work being done right now in Sierra Leone and Malawi treating exactly this condition in children, therapeutic food formulations tested for how fast and how safely a severely malnourished body can be brought back, the same staged-refeeding question Keys's four groups were answering for adult famine survivors instead of children, carried forward under a different acronym and a different population but the identical underlying physiology.

## Chapter Eleven: The Data Outlives the Consent That Produced It

Reception for this study has mostly been warm, and largely earned: Todd Tucker's 2006 popular account, "The Great Starvation Experiment," treats the thirty-six volunteers as close to what Neil Hartman wanted to be seen as, men who took on real physical risk for a cause larger than themselves, and reviewers generally agreed the story earned that framing even where they found the book's later chapters less compelling than its opening. But one recent critique complicates the tidy version of this story in a direction that has nothing to do with how the original men were treated, and everything to do with the way their data has since been used on people who never volunteered for anything. In 2021, a paper in the Journal of Bioethical Inquiry examined a line of argument, advanced to justify the force-feeding of prisoners on hunger strike at Guantanamo Bay, that leaned on Minnesota's own findings about starvation's cognitive effects: as a hunger strike progresses, the argument runs, a striker's judgment degrades enough that his refusal of food can no longer be treated as a competent decision, which is offered as grounds to override it. The paper's authors, Bhatt, Lederman, and Voo, argued this borrowed the wrong data for the job twice over, once on the science and once on the ethics the science was being asked to settle. Keys's thirty-six subjects were young, screened, medically monitored volunteers eating a controlled semi-starvation diet they could have ended at any point simply by asking; a detainee refusing food in protest is doing so under confinement, with no comparable option to simply step outside the study. Using evidence gathered from men who chose their own hunger, under a design built around their own consent, to argue that a different, coerced population's refusal of food should not be respected, inverts the very thing that made the original study defensible in the first place. Seventy-seven years later, thirty-six conscientious objectors' voluntary hunger became a footnote cited in an argument for force-feeding men who wanted nothing of the kind, the same reversal 050's own closing chapter warned a trialist to watch for: a method proven ethical for one population later repurposed to license something very different done to another.

## Chapter Twelve: What Thirty-Six Men Actually Bought With a Year of Their Lives

A follow-up investigation tracked down nineteen of the original thirty-six in 2002, fifty-seven years after the men first walked into the stadium basement, and reinterviewed each of them by phone; the results themselves reached print only in 2018, a sixteen-year gap between the last interview and the published paper that the study's own title, "A 57-Year Follow-Up Investigation," now permanently outdates. What the interviews found was reassuring on the question that mattered most for the men themselves: most had gone on to live long, ordinarily productive lives with no evidence the experience had left lasting psychiatric damage behind, an answer to a question their own consent had raised in 1944 that nobody, at the time, could have guaranteed in advance. What the follow-up did find was a gap between how fast bodies recovered in the study's own twelve rehabilitation weeks and how long full psychological and metabolic normalization actually took outside the laboratory: most men needed well over a year to feel fully like themselves again, and at least one took closer to five. Marshall Sutton, one of the surviving volunteers interviewed decades later, put his own recovery at about a year, long enough to strain a new marriage and a first year of teaching at once. Sutton went on to do relief work in Gaza much later in life, and there, watching people living through real and ongoing food scarcity, he recognized behavior his own diary had described seventy years earlier: the narrowing, the fixation, the particular flatness that hunger produces in a mind otherwise capable of anything. The study's own most durable finding may be that one: that recovery from real hunger runs on a slower clock than the body's weight chart alone can show, a fact eating disorder clinicians now build into how long they expect real recovery to take, and a fact that outlived every institution that funded the original study to learn it.

Ancel Keys went on, after 1945, to a much longer and more contested career than this single study, one that will return to this shelf when it takes up the Seven Countries Study and the diet-heart hypothesis that made him, briefly, one of the most famous scientists in America, his own face on a magazine cover in 1961, sixteen years after his starvation study had already shaped how the Allies tried to feed a liberated Europe and Asia back to health. That is a different argument for a different hour, about a different kind of evidence than a stadium full of men keeping diaries about cookbooks. What this hour leaves standing is narrower and, in its way, more useful: a demonstration, still running today in feeding centers treating starvation nobody chose, that how carefully you bring a body back from hunger matters as much as whether you feed it at all, proven first by thirty-six men who were allowed, in a way the convicts and asylum patients two episodes of this shelf ago were never allowed, to actually agree to what was about to happen to them. Whatever else the historical record complicates about how free that agreement really was, no one in this story had to pardon a single one of them to get it.
