## Chapter One: Four Questions from a Dying President

On the seventeenth of November, nineteen forty-four, Franklin Roosevelt signed a letter to Vannevar Bush that asked four questions. They were short enough to memorize. When may the wartime findings be made public, security permitting? What would carry the war of science against disease into peacetime? What should the government do to help research by public and private organizations, and how should the roles of the two be divided? And can a program be built to find and train scientific talent in American youth, at a level matching the war effort?

The second question carried a piece of arithmetic that the letter put in a single sentence. The annual deaths in this country from one or two diseases, Roosevelt wrote, were far in excess of the lives lost by Americans in battle during the war. He did not name the diseases. Bush's report named them later: cardiovascular disease, with chronic kidney disease and stroke counted in, accounted for forty-five percent of American deaths, infectious disease came second, and cancer third.

Roosevelt died on the twelfth of April, nineteen forty-five. Bush's answer went to Harry Truman in July, under a transmittal letter that did two things the rest of the story depends on. It said the report was Bush's own, while the facts and recommendations rested on four committees, whose full reports were bound in as appendices. It also said that when Roosevelt wrote of science, Bush took him to mean the natural sciences, including biology and medicine, and so the social sciences and the humanities were left out, with a sentence granting that progress in those fields was likewise important. Hold on to that choice of boundary. A good deal of the later fighting over this document was fighting over what the boundary excluded.

This episode reads the report itself, the whole forty or so printed pages of body text, and then reads it against the thing it is cited for. Those are different things, and the gap between them is the subject. The question underneath is older than Bush and has not been answered since: when a society decides to pay for knowledge that nobody can promise will be useful, who decides what gets paid for, and what is the society entitled to expect in return?

An earlier episode in this series, number forty-two, covered Bush's memoir of the war, in which a rejected submarine detector in nineteen seventeen taught him that good ideas die in the gap between the person who has them and the person who can say yes. That episode ended with the contract-instead-of-conscription method that ran the wartime Office of Scientific Research and Development. This one is about what Bush tried to carry from that method into peacetime, and what survived the carrying.

## Chapter Two: The Ledger

The report opens with a ledger, and a reader trained to ask for denominators should read it as evidence. The death rate from all diseases in the Army, overseas forces included, had fallen from fourteen point one deaths per thousand men in the previous war to six tenths of a death in this one. Life expectancy in the United States had gone from forty-nine years to sixty-five over forty years, largely because fewer infants and children were dying. Deaths from the diseases of childhood had fallen eighty-seven percent in twenty years. Diabetes was controlled by insulin, pernicious anemia by liver extract, and the deficiency diseases, which this series spent most of September on, had been much reduced by what the report calls accessory food factors. Anyone who followed the scurvy, beriberi and pellagra episodes will recognize the line as a one-sentence summary of Lind, Eijkman and Goldberger.

From the ledger Bush draws a claim about where the winning moves came from. The advances of the war, he says, were possible only because of a large backlog of data accumulated through basic research in many fields in the years before the war. The war itself had mostly applied what was already known to particular problems. Then comes the sentence that the whole report leans on, and it is a statement about the past, not a forecast. The country had been living on its fat. For more than five years its scientists had been thrown, like troops, into narrow projects initiated and controlled by one office, and nobody had been adding to the stock of general knowledge those projects drew down.

The next step is the one that most readers remember, and the text is plainer than its reputation. Basic research, the report says, is performed without thought of practical ends. It results in general knowledge and an understanding of nature and its laws. That general knowledge provides the means of answering a large number of practical problems, though it may not give a complete specific answer to any one of them. The function of applied research is to supply the complete answers. Then Bush calls basic research scientific capital, a fund from which practical applications must be drawn, and says that today it is truer than ever that basic research is the pacemaker of technological progress.

The pacemaker line has a sentence before it that explains why he thought it was true now. In the nineteenth century, he writes, Yankee mechanical ingenuity, building largely on the discoveries of European scientists, could advance the technical arts. The situation had changed. A nation that depends on others for its new basic knowledge will be slow in industrial progress and weak in competition, regardless of its mechanical skill. The war had cut the supply from Europe, and nobody could say when it would resume. That is a strategic argument about a supplier, and it is aimed at a particular decade.

Then comes the table that I think is the report's real evidence, and it is easy to skim past. Bush reconstructs, with an apology for arbitrary definitions, what the country spent on research between nineteen thirty and nineteen forty. Industry and government together, almost entirely on applied work, went from about one hundred and forty million dollars to about three hundred and nine million. The colleges and universities went from twenty million to thirty-one million. The endowed research institutes, the Rockefeller-style places that did pure work, went down, from five point two million to four point five. In nineteen thirty, he notes, the applied side outspent the basic side six to one. By nineteen forty it was nearly ten to one.

Read as an argument, this is a claim about a market. Industry is inhibited, he says, by preconceived goals, clearly defined standards, and the constant pressure of commercial necessity. The universities, at their best, give a scientist freedom from the adverse pressure of convention, prejudice, or commercial necessity, and the report adds a clause that anyone who has served on a panel will hear twice: much of new knowledge is certain to arouse opposition because it challenges current beliefs or practice. The medical section reaches the same place from the clinical side. The traditional sources of support for medical research in the schools, endowment income and foundation grants and gifts, were diminishing, with no change in sight, while costs rose. Industry, it says flatly, is a source of funds for basic medical research only to a limited extent.

So the structure of the argument is a ledger, a claim about the gap between the ledger's two columns, and a remedy. The remedy is public money. The remedy is also missing something. It contains no plan for turning discoveries into products, and no promise of what any particular dollar buys. The report concedes as much. Statistically it is certain, Bush writes, that important and highly useful discoveries will result from some fraction of the undertakings in basic science, but the results of any one particular investigation cannot be predicted with accuracy. That sentence is a bet on a spread of projects, and it is the honest form of the case. It also contains the weakness that every later critic went looking for, which is that a spread bet cannot be refuted by any single failed grant and is therefore awkward to test at all.

## Chapter Three: A Detour Through Penicillin

The report uses penicillin twice, once as a ledger entry and once as an illustration of method, and the story behind it, as the standard histories tell it, is a better test of Bush's model than anything he wrote about it. I am giving it from general knowledge of those histories, at the level of detail a reader can verify in any account of the drug, and it runs better to its end than to its beginning.

In nineteen twenty-eight, in a London laboratory, Alexander Fleming noticed that a mold had contaminated a plate of staphylococci and that the bacteria near it had died. This was research on bacteria, and nobody had asked for an antibiotic. For about a decade nothing useful came of the observation. The mold juice was unstable and hard to purify, and Fleming did not pursue it as a drug. Then, at Oxford in the late nineteen thirties, Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and a team that included the biochemist Norman Heatley went looking at natural antibacterial substances more or less for the sake of knowing how they worked, found Fleming's paper, and managed to purify enough material to protect infected mice, and by nineteen forty-one to treat a handful of patients. The supply ran out. They recycled the drug from patients' urine to stretch it.

What changed the quantity, in the standard account, was American and wartime. Florey and Heatley came to the United States in the summer of nineteen forty-one, and the problem of growing the mold at industrial scale was taken up by government and drug company laboratories, with a search for better strains and for a better culture medium. There was enough for the Normandy landings that June. The report's own phrasing of this is that the development moved from the early laboratory stage to large-scale production and use in a fraction of the time it would have taken without such leadership, and that the government provided overall coordination and support without dictating how the work was done inside any cooperating institution.

Put the whole sequence on a timeline and the shape is clear, and it does not match the cartoon of Bush that later writers argued with. The discovery came sideways, from someone studying something else, and the report says exactly that, in its remark that discoveries pertinent to medical progress have often come from remote and unexpected sources and that progress against refractory diseases will probably come from fundamental discoveries in subjects unrelated to them. The long gap between observation and drug was a gap in the work of finding out what the substance was, which is basic work in any sense Bush used. And the last stage, the one that made enough drug to matter, needed government direction of a kind the report says should continue: a coordinated attack on a special problem, with the government as organizer and the institutions as performers.

So the penicillin example, which Bush picked to be the lead exhibit, contains both halves of his position. Free inquiry produced the lead, and organized development produced the drug. The report says in so many words that Government initiative, support and coordination can be very effective in the development phase of newly discovered therapeutic materials. Keep that sentence in mind through the next chapters, because the main complaint against Bush for sixty years has been that he believed in a one-way road from the lab bench to the market with the state needed only at the bench end. The text he signed does not say that.

## Chapter Four: Who Said Linear

If Bush did not say it, who did? The question has a scholarly literature, and the evidence I have read this session comes mostly from summaries of it, so what follows is attributed accordingly. Benoit Godin, a historian of science and statistics, published a paper in two thousand and six in the journal Science, Technology and Human Values on the construction of what he calls the linear model of innovation: the sequence in which basic research leads to applied research, then development, then production and diffusion. The abstract's central finding is that the model's source has never been documented, and that the people who used and criticized it for fifty years rarely cited an original. Some say it comes straight from Bush. Godin's reading is that the sequence was added after the fact, and is only partly and imperfectly reflected in what Bush actually wrote. In his account the first formalization came in the nineteen forties and fifties from the economist Rupert Maclaurin, building on Schumpeter, and later from business school writers and from the statisticians of the OECD, who needed a classification for surveys.

There is an irony that Godin's summary does not mention and that the report's own appendix supplies. W. Rupert Maclaurin, professor of economics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, appears in the report's list of the committee that answered Roosevelt's third question, the one about the proper roles of public and private research. His title there is secretary. The man credited with first writing the pipeline down as a model was inside the room, in the junior seat, taking the notes. I would not build a theory on that, since a secretary is a secretary. I mention it because it shows how short the distance was between a committee's loose chain of claims and an economist's tidy diagram of them.

David Edgerton, a historian of technology, took the argument further in a paper whose title is its thesis: the linear model did not exist. His point, as the summaries have it, is that no one in industry or in the sciences ever worked from such a model in the first place, and that history written in its shadow overlooks chemistry and industrial research, among other things. Roger Pielke Junior, a political scientist who has written on science policy for decades, reviewed the report in Nature on its sixty-fifth anniversary. According to the search summaries of his piece, the report's actual contribution was to call for a centralized approach to government-sponsored science, largely shielded from political accountability, and to push the phrase basic research into everyday use.

I take the revisionists to be correct about the text and incomplete about the effect. The text of the report, as shown in the last chapter, has the pipeline and the exceptions in one document. But a document is read by people who have to fill in a line of a budget, and a line of a budget wants a category. The report gave Congress and the universities a category, basic research, defined by the absence of a thought of use, and a one-sentence defense of paying for it, that it is the pacemaker. A phrase of that shape travels with the money. Whether Bush believed in a one-way road matters less to the history of research funding than the fact that the report's most quotable sentences are one-way sentences, and that the paragraphs next to them, the ones about teamwork and development and the intermediate character of government science, are not quotable.

There is a second thing the report did that the pipeline argument obscures, and it is a claim about incentives, not about causation. The report says basic research is essentially noncommercial in nature and will not receive the attention it requires if left to industry. That is a market-failure argument, and it holds without a pipeline. The economist Richard Nelson, reviewing Kealey's hostile book, which comes up again in Chapter Seven, made the point in his own terms. One does not need to believe in the linear model, he wrote, to be a strong advocate of public support for science, because knowledge is nonrivalrous in use, meaning that one user does not exhaust it for the next. If that is the real foundation, the pacemaker image was a decorative dome on a building that stands without it.

## Chapter Five: The Design

The second half of the report is a design for an institution, and it is the half Bush cared about most, since he spent the next five years fighting for it. He called it the National Research Foundation. Its justification is a short argument by exclusion. Government laboratories did applied work and should stay where they were, in Agriculture and Commerce and Interior. Basic research could not sit inside any of them, because an operating agency has immediate goals and is under constant pressure to produce in a tangible way, and the report says research will always suffer when put in competition with operations. And the agency had to be one, not several. Science, he writes, is fundamentally a unitary thing, medical progress will often come from advances in chemistry, and tight compartments would retard it.

Then the five fundamentals. Whatever the extent of support, there must be stability of funds over several years, with commitments of five years or longer, because research ceases to be basic if immediate results are expected on short-term support. The agency's governors should be citizens chosen for their interest in and understanding of research. The agency should fund through contracts and grants to outside organizations and should operate no laboratories of its own. The internal control of policy, personnel, and the method and scope of research must remain with the institutions themselves, a point Bush marks as being of the utmost importance. And, last, the agency must be responsible to the President and Congress, because only through such responsibility can science be kept in proper relation to a democracy.

The detailed proposal turns those into a machine. Nine members, not otherwise employed by government and not representing any interest, appointed by the President for four-year terms, with no immediate reappointment, serving without pay except expenses, choosing their own chairman, and appointing a director. Below them, Divisions, each of at least five members, for medical research, natural sciences, national defense, scientific personnel and education, and publications and international exchange, with the National Academy of Sciences asked to nominate. The Division members would be paid a per diem, capped at ten thousand dollars a year. The Division of National Defense would add one representative of the Secretary of War and one of the Navy.

Then there are the clauses that read like the work of someone who had been stopped by a purchasing clerk. The Foundation should be free to place contracts by negotiation, without advertising for bids. It should be authorized to make and amend contracts with or without legal consideration, and without performance bonds, and to pay in advance when needed. The usual itemized vouchering by the auditors should be relaxed, because universities, unlike commercial contractors, are not equipped for it. The measure of a successful research contract, he writes, lies not in dollar cost but in the contribution to knowledge, and that depends on the creative spirit in the laboratory. Anyone who has submitted a grant through a university grants office will recognize the register of those sentences. Bush had just spent five years running a contract machine for a war, and he knew where the friction lived. On patents he proposed that the government take a royalty-free license for its own purposes and that the institution face no obligation to patent anything and no requirement to hand rights over, with the board free to require assignment in special cases.

The budget the report gives is modest by any later standard. The overall program of federal support for basic research in the institutions and for important applied work in the public interest, he estimates in Chapter Three, would cost about ten million dollars at the outset and might reach about fifty million annually after five years. Medical research would absorb not more than five million dollars in the first year and, with the program underway, perhaps twenty million a year. The report is more specific about the people than the money. It warns of a deficit of about one hundred and fifty thousand science and technology bachelor's degrees that the war had cost and projects a shortfall of seventeen thousand advanced degrees by nineteen fifty-five, and it asks for scholarships and fellowships, chosen on ability and not on family fortune. It then adds a sentence that no admiring summary seems to quote: the plan should attract into science only that proportion of youthful talent appropriate to the needs of science in relation to the other needs of the nation for high abilities. Bush was rationing as well as recruiting.

There is one passage in this section that bears on every later argument about whether public money displaces private. The authors of the report asked themselves how federal funds could be arranged so as not to drive out funds from local governments, foundations and private donors. They believed their proposals would minimize that effect, and then they wrote, in the report's own words, that they did not think it could be completely avoided. The nation's need for more and better research was such that the risk must be accepted. Remember that sentence. A critic who says Bush ignored crowding out is arguing with a man who named it, priced it, and decided to take it on.

## Chapter Six: The Veto

The design needed an act of Congress, and the act took five years. A short account of the fight tells you most of what the report left out.

The opposing camp was led by Senator Harley Kilgore of West Virginia, who had been proposing a federal science agency since nineteen forty-two, earlier than Bush. Kilgore's version, as the historians describe it, was a New Deal institution: a director appointed by the President, a patent policy that would stop large firms from abusing patent law, a concern for spreading research money across the country and not into a few rich institutions, and a mandate that included social and economic goals. The disagreement is the oldest one in the subject. Bush wanted scientists to choose the agency's leaders, with the President's role confined to naming a board. Kilgore and his allies thought an agency spending public money ought to take its goals from democratic politics, with the President accountable for its head. Bush and Kilgore collaborated on a bill in nineteen forty-four, according to the account I read, and Bush rejected the result. Daniel Kevles, in his nineteen seventy-seven article in Isis on the debate, names the same fault lines: how concentrated the money should be, who owned the wartime patents, and who ran the agency.

In the summer of that year, with Republicans in control of Congress for the first time in many years, a bill friendly to Bush's design passed Congress. On the sixth of August, Truman refused to sign it. His memorandum of disapproval is a political document and a clever one, and its objections read, from a distance of eighty years, like a charge sheet against the study section. The foundation as designed, he wrote, would be divorced from control by the people to an extent that implies a distinct lack of faith in democratic processes. It would place the determination of national policy, the spending of large public sums, and important governmental functions in a group of essentially private citizens. The President would be deprived of any effective means of discharging his responsibility. And the board members, he added, would likely be employed by institutions eligible for grants, creating a conflict of interests that would inevitably give rise to suspicions of favoritism.

Set the last objection beside Bush's clause allowing Foundation members to hold private gainful employment, on the condition that no compensation came from a profit-making institution funded by their own division. Bush had seen the problem. He had solved it for companies and left it open for universities. A person who has sat on a review panel and later applied to the agency whose panel she sat on knows that the conflict Truman named is a permanent feature of expert review in a small field. It is not an accident of a particular bill.

The settled act came in May of nineteen fifty. It created a National Science Board of twenty-four part-time members and a director, and the director was appointed by the President. That was Kilgore's point, and it was the point the Truman veto had been about. Kevles, as the secondary sources quote him, called the result largely a triumph for Bush, since what the agency did matched what Bush had wanted done. Both descriptions are fair. The functions went Bush's way and the control went Truman's.

The money went neither way at first. The first appropriation, voted that April, was five hundred thousand dollars, under a ceiling of fifteen million a year. By the summer of nineteen fifty-one Congress had voted only about two hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars toward the fourteen million that had been hoped for. The first research grants came that winter, thirty-five awards averaging fourteen thousand five hundred dollars. Its first full year ran on a bit over three million, against a request for ten times that. Alan Waterman, who had been the Navy's technical director of research, ran it from nineteen fifty-one.

And meanwhile the country did what Bush had said it should do, through other doors. From my general knowledge of the period and not from a source I read this session, the Office of Naval Research began funding university physics in nineteen forty-six, the National Institutes of Health built a grant program that became the center of academic biomedicine, and the Atomic Energy Commission and later the Department of Defense paid for a large share of basic science. Bush's single agency, the one that was to avoid separating the sciences in tight compartments, became one funder among many, and the medical Division of his Foundation turned into something larger than the Foundation. A reader who runs a lab in this system lives inside the architecture Bush opposed with the principle he proposed. The principle was that universities get grants and decide what happens inside the grant. The architecture was one agency answering to a board of unpaid eminences.

## Chapter Seven: The Case Against, in Four Voices

The strongest objections are not about the veto. They are about whether the argument works.

The first is Terence Kealey's, in a nineteen ninety-six book called The Economic Laws of Scientific Research. Kealey is a biochemist and later a university vice-chancellor, and his thesis is that government need not fund science, because public funding crowds out private. His three laws, as Nelson summarized them in his review, were that research spending rises with national wealth and does not cause it, that public funding reduces private support, and that the net effect is to shrink both the amount and the efficacy of research. His main comparisons were national: Britain, with heavy public civilian research funding and a weak industrial record, against Japan, with little of the former and a strong record of high technology. He also rejected the linear model as the justification for the postwar settlement.

Nelson's answer has two parts, and both are good. The first concedes the ground a fair reader would concede: government programs for civilian markets in applied technology have virtually all been expensive failures. The second goes after the base: publicly funded basic research, in agriculture and in biomedicine especially, has enormously increased the power of privately funded applied research. The first part is Kealey's best card, and it is a card against government picking technologies, not against Bush's design, which opposed government operation of laboratories. The second part is the answer to the crowding-out law, and it holds only if the extra knowledge actually gets used by someone else, as the nonrivalry point says. I would add that Kealey's comparison is a comparison of two countries, and the diet-heart episode, number fifty-three, spent its length on what country-level tables can and cannot establish. Britain and Japan differ in much besides who pays for research. And the crowding-out claim does not fall when one country fails to match the story. Bush wrote that it could not be avoided. The empirical question is its size, and I have not seen a study that settles it.

The second voice is Donald Stokes, a political scientist at Princeton, whose nineteen ninety-seven book Pasteur's Quadrant takes on the vocabulary rather than the funding. Stokes says the basic-applied line is a false dichotomy that harms science policy, and he replaces the line with a pair of axes. One asks whether the work is driven by a quest for fundamental understanding. The other asks whether it is driven by considerations of use. Niels Bohr's search for a model of the atom sits in the corner with understanding and no use. Thomas Edison's laboratory sits in the opposite corner, use with no wish to explain. Pasteur sits in the third corner with both, since he founded microbiology while being asked to fix the problems of wine, beer and vinegar makers. Stokes's argument is that the postwar compact encouraged scientists to claim the first corner, and that the most productive work in biomedicine happens in the third.

For a physician-researcher this is the objection that comes closest to home, since the third corner is where clinical science lives. It is also the objection that Bush's text anticipates least badly. The report says government science is intermediate in character between basic and applied. It describes the coordinated attack on special problems, with teams, as a legitimate government role. It gives penicillin as the model. Its fault is a definition: basic research is performed without thought of practical ends. Stokes is right that this definition, once it became the official name of the category, taught people to hide use. A grant applicant who knew that Pasteur's corner paid would still have to write the application as if she lived in Bohr's.

The third voice is empirical and aimed at the first half of Bush's claim. In nineteen seventy-six Julius Comroe and Robert Dripps, two physiologists, published in Science a retrospective on ten clinical advances in cardiovascular and pulmonary medicine over the previous three decades, and traced back the papers on which each had depended. Their finding, as it is usually cited, was that about forty percent of the essential papers were not clinically oriented when they were written, and that over sixty percent reported basic research. This is a result with the shape Bush's portfolio argument needs. It is also a retrospective with a hindsight problem. The judges who chose the essential papers knew the outcome. The objection is the one every retrospective faces: nobody counted the roads that led nowhere. A trial cannot be run, as with diet, since there is no second country in which the money was withheld. That leaves a pile of case studies, each of which is read by its author.

The fourth voice is the one that Truman had in nineteen forty-seven and that Pielke repeated in two thousand and ten. If the report's design was meant to shield science from political accountability, the instrument was an unpaid board of elite scientists, nominated by their own academy, handing out money to their own institutions. Bush could answer, and did in the fifth fundamental, that the agency was answerable to the President and Congress. The Truman memo's reply is that an agency whose head is not appointed by the President is not answerable to him in any usual sense of the word. The nineteen fifty act settled the structure, and the structure is the reason the National Science Foundation looks as it does. It does not settle the question of how a society checks the judgment of the people it has asked to be free of its judgment.

## Chapter Eight: What the Report Is For

My own verdict comes in three parts, because the report makes three kinds of claim and they have different fates.

The first claim is about markets: basic knowledge is a thing that private actors will underproduce, because the benefits scatter and the results are uncertain. That is the report's strongest argument and the best supported, from the spending table on the front end to the nonrivalry argument that economists have since put under it. Kealey's reply has not displaced it. The crowding-out risk is real and was priced in at the outset, and nothing I read this session sizes it well enough to justify withdrawing public money. The claim stands.

The second claim is about the sequence, that discovery feeds development which feeds products, and here the report is more careful and less correct than its reputation suggests. It is careful because it never says the road runs one way. Its penicillin story needs the government in the middle, and its Chapter Two says so. It is less correct because it defined basic research by the researcher's motive, and a test of motive is easy to game and hard to audit. Stokes has caught the vocabulary, and Godin and Edgerton have the text, though they are weaker on the afterlife.

The third claim is about governance: put the money in the hands of a board of the best scientists, give the universities control inside the grant, keep the agency independent of any operating department. The second and third of those propositions survived, in the arm's-length grant and the agency with no operating mission. The first, rule by a board of eminent scientists, is the part the Truman veto removed, and the conflict-of-interest objection has not been answered by anything I read. If this part of the report was meant as a bet, it was a bet that the community of scientists was small and trustworthy enough to govern itself. Weber's nineteen seventeen lecture, in episode thirty-six, was a picture of the academic career as a gamble run by a guild. The report asked a government to underwrite the guild's gamble and then to stay out of the room.

The reading prescription is a short one, because the document is short and in the public domain. The National Science Foundation reprinted it for the seventy-fifth anniversary. Read the Roosevelt letter, the summary at the front, Chapter Three on the importance of basic research, and Chapter Six on the foundation and its five fundamentals, about an hour in all. Skip the appendices unless the talent committee's work on scholarships interests you. The medical section in Chapter Two is a good place to see how the report treats penicillin and the rest of the ledger. Reading a summary will not do, because the summaries leave out the clauses on vouchering and bids, and those clauses are the report's most practical parts.

For a standing test that belongs to the narrator and not to Bush, I would apply the following to any argument for funding research, whether a grant application or a government white paper. Find out which of three claims it is invoking. The market claim says nobody else will pay for this knowledge. The pipeline claim says this will become a product. The mission claim says we need this by a date. The three call for different review criteria, different time horizons and different tests of success, and the damage comes from presenting one as another. A proposal that makes the market argument and is reviewed for its commercial prospects has been misfiled, and so has a mission program reviewed for its elegance.

The generative question for the coming week is for anyone who writes or reviews proposals. In the next piece of research you are asked to judge or defend, say which of the three claims is being made, say which one the review form actually measures, and see whether they match. If the form asks for innovation and impact on a field and the proposal is a market argument for a slow, unglamorous piece of basic work, the proposal will lose on the form's terms even if Bush's argument says it should win.
