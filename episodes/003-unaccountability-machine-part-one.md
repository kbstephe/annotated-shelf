## Chapter One: A Shipment from Beijing

In April of nineteen ninety-nine, a cargo flight landed at Schiphol airport outside Amsterdam carrying four hundred and forty squirrels. They had been shipped from Beijing and they were bound for Athens, and somewhere between those two facts a piece of paper had gone missing – the permit that would let them continue. The plane that brought them left. The squirrels stayed, in their crates, in a warehouse, while the airline's ground staff consulted the applicable policy. The policy, set by the Dutch agriculture ministry, offered two options for live animals arriving without documentation: return them to their place of origin, or destroy them. For reasons that no account of the incident fully explains, return was ruled out. And so staff of KLM Cargo, following the procedure, fed four hundred and forty squirrels into an industrial shredder built for poultry. When the Dutch public found out, the outrage was immediate, and the airline's response has a place in history. KLM expressed sincere regret for an outcome it agreed was unethical, and noted that its employees had acted, in its words, formally correctly. Every part of that sentence is true. The staff followed the rules. The rules came from a government department whose officials never saw a squirrel. The officials had written those rules for sound reasons of disease control, years earlier, with no particular animal in mind. Somewhere in this chain a decision was made to shred four hundred and forty healthy animals, and yet if you walk the chain looking for the person who made it, you arrive back where you started, empty-handed. That question – who decided? – is the one Dan Davies hangs an entire book on. It is worth sitting with how strange the answer is. Not hidden. Not covered up. Just absent.

## Chapter Two: Walking, Not Touring

This series is three episodes on one book: The Unaccountability Machine, published in twenty twenty-four. Episode two of this feed toured Davies's whole body of work at speed – the fraud book, the blog, the newsletter, the synthesis with Rao. Twenty minutes for twenty-five years of writing. The tour was honest but it moved the way tours move, and this book in particular repays a slower walk, because it is really two books wearing one cover: a field guide to a structure Davies calls the accountability sink, and a rescue mission for a dead British management thinker who believed organizations could be rebuilt around information instead of blame. Part one is the field guide. Part two is the rescue mission. Part three asks what repair would look like and lets the book's critics have their full say. The question the whole series carries, and the one to hold while listening: when something terrible happens inside a big organization and no one can be blamed, where did the decision come from – and what would it take to get it back?

## Chapter Three: What a Sink Is

Start with what the squirrel story has in common with an experience you have had this year. You call your insurer, or your bank, or an airline. The phone tree routes you to an agent. The agent is polite, and sorry, and completely unable to help, because the thing you need is against policy, and the agent did not write the policy, and neither did the agent's supervisor, and the person who did write the policy does not take calls. That is an accountability sink. Davies's definition has two parts, and the second one is the load-bearing one. First, a decision has been delegated to a process – a rulebook, a script, an algorithm – so that no individual is deciding anything; they are implementing. Second, and this is what separates a sink from ordinary bureaucracy: the feedback link has been severed. The person affected by the decision cannot reach anyone capable of changing it. Information about the decision's consequences has no path back to its source. The severing can be physical – a phone tree with no extension that reaches the policy owner – but it is more often organizational: the channel exists on paper, and everything that travels up it is summarized, aggregated, and reclassified until what arrives carries no trace of what was sent. The squirrels' situation was individual – these animals, this paperwork, this option of holding them a week while Athens faxed a form – but the policy could only see a category, and the staff could only see the policy, and the ministry that owned the policy had built no channel for a warehouse worker's doubt to travel up. Davies offers a diagnostic that takes ten seconds to apply. Ask of any bad outcome: was there a person with the authority to override the policy? If the answer is yes, you have found accountability, whatever else has gone wrong. If the answer is the policy is the policy, you are standing in the sink. Carry that test through the rest of this episode, because every story that follows is a chance to run it.

## Chapter Four: The Conference Desk

The test works on small things, which is how you know it is describing a structure and not just a scandal. Tim Harford, the Financial Times columnist, reviewed this book and opened with a story of his own that is almost embarrassingly minor, which is the point. He arrived at a conference to give a talk and could not get in. The pass system had failed somehow; the security staff could not admit him without a pass; the organizers' desk could not issue one, because passes were issued elsewhere; and every single person in the chain was friendly, apologetic, and powerless. Nobody in the building had the authority to solve a problem that everybody in the building could see. Run the test: was there anyone with override authority? There was not – not on site. A conference is a temporary organization a few weeks old, and it had already, without anyone intending it, reproduced the structure that shredded the squirrels. That is the observation that moves Davies's idea from anecdote to mechanism. Sinks are not what happens when organizations go wrong. They are what organizations build by default, at every scale, the way water finds low ground – unless someone deliberately builds otherwise. Which raises the question the next chapter takes seriously rather than rhetorically: if every organization builds them, might they be there for a reason?

## Chapter Five: The Case for the Sink

Here is the steelman, and Davies himself makes it, which is part of why the book is better than an airport polemic. A modern organization processes more complexity than any human being can hold. An airline moves a million objects a day. If every anomalous crate required a manager's fresh moral judgment, nothing would fly. Delegating decisions to a rulebook is how a system scales; it is also, and this deserves its own weight, how a system becomes fair. The rulebook treats the well-connected passenger and the anonymous one identically. Discretion is what corruption eats first – ask anyone who has watched a permit office where every rule is negotiable, for the right person, at the right price. A sink, whatever else it does, cannot be bribed, and a society that has suffered enough discretion will build sinks on purpose and call them the rule of law. And there is a stronger case still, which Harford presses: some sinks are built deliberately, in the open, because severing a feedback link is the entire point. An independent central bank is an accountability sink by design. Politicians have handed interest rates to a committee precisely so that no elected person can be blamed for the pain of raising them – and the well-documented result is lower inflation than the politicians used to deliver. Britain's habit of blaming Brussels for decades of domestic choices worked the same mechanism in reverse: a fictional sink, invoked to launder decisions someone had in fact made. So the honest version of Davies's claim is not that sinks are evil. It is that a sink is a piece of infrastructure with a specific failure mode. The central bank kept its feedback: it publishes forecasts, testifies, gets judged against a target, and can change course. The squirrel policy kept none. The difference between a designed sink and an accreted one is whether anybody is still listening to what the decision does after it leaves the building. Hold that distinction, because the next story is about what happens when a sink runs for sixteen years with the listening switched off.

## Chapter Six: The Post Office

Between nineteen ninety-nine and twenty fifteen, the British Post Office prosecuted more than seven hundred of its own sub-postmasters – the small shopkeepers who run village post office counters – for theft and false accounting. The evidence, in case after case, was a shortfall reported by Horizon, the Post Office's new Fujitsu-built accounting system. The pattern at ground level was almost identical every time. A sub-postmaster balances the till; Horizon says money is missing; the sub-postmaster calls the helpline and says the computer must be wrong; the helpline says no one else is reporting problems – which was false, but each caller was told it – and that the contract makes them personally liable for shortfalls. Pay, or be prosecuted. And the asymmetry of knowledge ran deeper than the callers could have imagined: it later emerged, in litigation and then in the public inquiry, that engineers at Fujitsu could access branch accounts remotely and alter them – a capability whose existence the Post Office's side denied in court for years, while prosecuting shopkeepers on the theory that only the shopkeeper could have touched the numbers. People remortgaged houses to cover phantom deficits. Some went to prison; one woman, Seema Misra, was sent to prison while pregnant, on Horizon evidence alone. Some died before their names were cleared. The convictions began to fall in twenty twenty-one, and in twenty twenty-four Parliament took the extraordinary step of exonerating the group by statute – a step it took, after a quarter century, in the weeks after a television drama about the scandal aired, which is its own commentary on which feedback channels still reach the British state. Now run Davies's test on the years in between, because that is where the sink is. The prosecutors relied on the system's data; the system's operators asserted its robustness; the executives relied on the assurances of the operators; the helpline was scripted to deny a pattern its own logs displayed. At every desk, a person doing their job, formally correctly. The feedback – hundreds of independent, unconnected shopkeepers, all reporting the same impossible arithmetic – was arriving continuously, for sixteen years, at an organization structurally incapable of hearing it, because every channel it arrived through had been routed into liability management. The Post Office scandal is usually told as a story about a faulty computer. Davies's framework says the computer was the least of it. Computers are faulty everywhere. What did the damage was the machine around the computer, which had been configured, no doubt one defensible decision at a time, so that the truth had no path to anyone with the authority to act on it.

## Chapter Seven: The Ward and the Fax Machine

One more example, this one constructed – assembled from patterns familiar to anyone in American medicine, not taken from Davies's book – because it is worth confirming the framework travels. A physician orders a treatment; the insurer requires prior authorization; the authorization is denied. The denial letter is signed by no one, in the sense that matters: it cites criteria, maintained by a committee, applied by a reviewer who may spend under two minutes on the file. The appeal process offers a peer-to-peer call, in which the treating physician explains the case to a reviewing physician who did not write the criteria, cannot amend the criteria, and in a specialty case may not share the specialty. Both doctors on that call frequently know the denial is clinically wrong. Neither has override authority. The patient, meanwhile, experiences the delay as a decision someone made about their body – and is told, accurately, that no one made it. Run the test and the result is instant. And one detail of the constructed case deserves its own sentence: the fax machine. These processes very often still run on faxes and hold queues, and the friction is not entirely accidental, because in a sink, delay itself does work – some fraction of appeals are simply abandoned, and abandonment is recorded, by the process, as resolution. A sink does not only absorb blame. It can quietly convert exhaustion into consent.

## Chapter Eight: The Law Underneath

So: an airline warehouse, a conference lobby, a national postal service, an insurance file. Four different industries, three real cases and one constructed, and one structure appearing in all four without any of the participants intending to build it. The claim Davies plants under this pattern is the one this whole first part has been walking toward, and it reads like a law of nature stated in the dialect of an economist. Unless conscious steps are taken to prevent it, any organization in a modern society will restructure itself, over time, so as to reduce the amount of personal responsibility attributable to its actions – and the process continues until interrupted by crisis. He calls it the principle of diminishing accountability. The mechanism needs no villain, which is what makes it a law rather than an accusation. Every individual step is rational. A manager who converts a discretionary call into a documented policy has protected herself and standardized the outcome – both real goods. A legal department that scripts the helpline has reduced liability, which is its job. A committee that owns a criterion diffuses what a named signature would concentrate. Each step is defensible; the sum of the steps is the Post Office. Listeners who remember the Moral Mazes episode will also remember why the steps only ever run one direction: in Jackall's corporations, creating a document about a problem was understood as an aggressive act, because a document pins responsibility to a name. The sink is what that instinct builds when it is given thirty years and a compliance budget – an architecture in which no document ever has to pin anything to anyone, because the process signs everything. Listeners of the Moral Mazes episode will recognize the family resemblance, and it is worth being precise about what has changed. Jackall's managers, in the nineteen eighties, pushed blame down the hierarchy onto designated people – the subordinate holding the bag. That system was cruel but it conserved blame: someone, in the end, wore it. The sink is the next generation of the technology. Blame is not pushed down; it is dissolved. There is no bag-holder, no scapegoat with a grievance and a lawyer and a story to sell. The machine has improved, in exactly the way machines improve.

## Chapter Nine: The Question Under the Floor

Which leaves a problem that the field-guide half of the book cannot solve on its own, and you may have felt it accumulating through this episode. If the staff didn't decide, and the ministry didn't decide, and the prosecutors and the executives and the reviewers didn't decide – and yet squirrels were shredded and postmasters imprisoned and treatments denied, consistently, directionally, for years at a time – then something was deciding. Outcomes that consistent are not weather. Davies's answer, and the reason this book is stranger and more ambitious than its viral first chapter suggests, is that the organization itself decides. Not as a metaphor. His claim is that a large organization is a genuine decision-making entity – a machine built out of people, whose choices are real choices, made by no human inside it, and which can no more be explained by studying its employees' intentions than a traffic jam can be explained by interviewing one driver. If that is true, then blaming individuals for institutional outcomes is not just unfair; it is a category error, like arresting a piston for speeding. And the discipline that could describe such machines – that once built working models of them, and even wired one up to run a national economy from a hexagonal room in Santiago – has been dead so long that Davies has to perform an exhumation to use it.
