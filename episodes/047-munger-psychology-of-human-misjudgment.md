## Chapter One: Twenty People Hurt Twice in One Day

Before a new model of passenger airliner can be sold to an airline, federal regulators require its maker to prove that a full planeload of ordinary passengers can get out of the cabin and onto the ground inside ninety seconds, using nothing but the inflatable slides bolted to each exit. The rule exists because a fire on a runway kills people by the minute, and a company that has just spent years and billions of dollars designing a plane has every reason in the world to want that test to succeed the first time it is run.

One aircraft maker scheduled its certification evacuation test in a darkened hangar, meant to simulate a nighttime emergency, using elderly volunteers rather than athletic twenty-year-olds, because regulators had specifically required a realistic passenger mix. The cabin mockup sat some twenty feet above a concrete floor, reachable only by the same flimsy rubber chutes a real emergency would use. The morning test produced about twenty serious injuries among the volunteers and still failed the ninety-second clock. Rather than stop, rethink the site, or pad the floor, the company ran the identical test again that same afternoon. The second attempt failed the clock again, produced another twenty or so serious injuries, and left one volunteer permanently paralyzed.

Nothing about that company was unusually reckless. Its engineers were some of the most capable in American aviation, working inside one of the most heavily regulated industries in the country, on a program that existed for the specific purpose of protecting human life. And yet a room full of intelligent, well-intentioned people looked at the wreckage of a failed morning test and decided the correct response was to run it again, on the same people, in the same dark hangar, over the same concrete floor. Nobody in that hangar was stupid. Something else was doing the deciding.

That case, retold decades later by the investor Charlie Munger as one of his favorite illustrations, sat inside a much longer project: a checklist of twenty-five separate, ordinary tendencies built into every human brain, all pulling on that afternoon's decision at once, each one individually mild, all of them together strong enough to put a man in a wheelchair. Munger built the checklist to explain cases exactly like this one, and before this hour is done, it will explain this one specifically, tendency by tendency, the way Munger himself explained it. First, the question the whole list exists to answer: why would anyone need twenty-five different names for something as simple as being wrong.

## Chapter Two: An Amateur Trespassing on Someone Else's Ground

Charles Munger was born in Omaha, Nebraska, in nineteen twenty-four, left the University of Michigan to serve in the Army Air Corps during the Second World War, and was sent by the military to study meteorology at the California Institute of Technology, never finishing an undergraduate degree anywhere. He went on to Harvard Law School instead, graduated in nineteen forty-eight, and spent the next decade as a working real estate attorney in Los Angeles, doing well enough at development deals on the side to become financially independent by his forties. In nineteen fifty-nine, at a dinner in Omaha, he met a young fund manager named Warren Buffett, and the two men found they thought about business in something close to the same language. Munger eventually left law to run his own investment partnership, and by nineteen seventy-eight had become vice chairman of Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway, the post he held for the rest of his life.

What makes Munger worth an hour on a shelf otherwise stocked with historians and organizational theorists is not the investing record by itself. It is that somewhere in the middle of that career, with no training in psychology beyond three textbooks he skimmed in middle age, he built his own working theory of why smart, well-meaning people keep making the same kinds of mistakes, and he built it explicitly as an outsider raiding a field with no course credit to his name. He said so himself, with the self-mockery turned up: writing the talk that became this hour's subject would mean, in his own words, "popping off" on a subject in which he had never taken a course, and he named the risk directly rather than hiding from it. Two things pushed him past that risk. The first was reading, in middle age, a popular book called *Influence* by the social psychologist Robert Cialdini, whose experiments on how easily a stranger's mind can be manipulated convinced Munger that this material was too useful to leave to academics who published only for each other. The second was a habit he had practiced since law school, borrowed from the eighteenth-century mathematician Carl Jacobi's advice to always invert a hard problem and study it backward: rather than ask what good judgment looks like, Munger spent decades collecting cases of bad judgment, then asking what those cases had in common.

What he found, again and again, was that a smart brain does not fail by being stupid. It fails by running a small number of ancient, generally useful shortcuts in situations those shortcuts were never built for, the way an ant that follows the ant ahead of it will sometimes walk in a circle until it starves, using a program that works perfectly well everywhere except the one place it has gotten stuck. Munger's list names twenty-five of those shortcuts. Most of this hour does not have room to walk through all twenty-five in order, because a checklist read aloud item by item is the fastest way to lose a listener with no page to glance back at. Instead, this hour follows five of the shortcuts that did the most damage in Munger's own case studies, in roughly the order he found they tend to gang up on a person, ending with the hangar full of injured volunteers this hour opened on.

## Chapter Three: The Cash Register as a Moral Instrument

Munger placed incentives first on his list because, in his own telling, almost everyone already believes they understand how powerful incentives are, and almost everyone still underestimates that power anyway. His favorite case involves the overnight package-sorting hub that any next-day delivery company depends on: every plane has to be unloaded, sorted, and reloaded inside one narrow window each night, or the whole promise to customers collapses by morning. One delivery company tried everything to get its night shift moving faster, including straightforward appeals to duty, and nothing worked until someone realized the crew was being paid by the hour, which rewarded slowness rather than speed. Once the company switched to paying the shift a flat rate and letting everyone go home the moment the job was done, the problem solved itself overnight.

The same mechanism runs uglier when the incentive rewards something other than the customer's interest. Early in the history of the photocopier maker Xerox, its own leadership could not understand why the company's improved new machine was selling worse than its older, inferior one, until someone checked the sales force's commission structure and found it paid a fatter bonus for pushing the old machine. Munger traced the same pattern to a surgeon who, for years, sent healthy gall bladders down to the hospital pathology lab, not out of any calculated scheme, as Munger's own doctor friend explained it, but from a sincere belief that the organ caused most medical trouble, a belief that happened to keep the man's surgical calendar full. Munger's own name for this drift is incentive-caused bias: not conscious fraud, but a decent person's slow rationalization of whatever behavior the reward system happens to be paying for.

Munger's own prescription runs the same direction twice. First, install mechanical and procedural guards that make dishonest behavior hard rather than merely discouraged, the way an early cash-register manufacturer built an entire moral technology out of a machine that simply made it harder for a store clerk to pocket the till. Second, get the accounting itself right, since bad accounting can turn an honest employee into an unwitting liar; Munger pointed to an industrial conglomerate whose lending subsidiary reported eye-popping paper profits on a wave of new construction loans, calculated using the safe loan history of an entirely different kind of lending, and lost billions once those loans went the way risky construction loans usually go. "Get the incentives right," Munger said, is close to the first rule of management, and every case in this chapter is what happens when nobody does.

## Chapter Four: The Mind's Automatic Shut-Off Valve

Two more items on Munger's list work as a pair, and they explain why a wrong first guess is so much harder to fix than it should be. The first is what he called doubt-avoidance: a brain under pressure to decide, built by an evolutionary history in which a prey animal that hesitates too long gets eaten, will rush toward almost any conclusion just to stop the discomfort of not having one yet. The second is inconsistency-avoidance: once that conclusion exists, the same brain treats changing it as a kind of self-betrayal, and will defend a bad early guess with more energy than it ever spent forming it.

Munger's clearest illustration of the trap is Charles Darwin's own working method. Darwin trained himself to hunt specifically for evidence that contradicted his favorite hypotheses, and to write that evidence down before it could fade from memory, because he recognized in himself the same anti-change reflex Munger later catalogued. The opposite habit, Munger noted, earned its own name in psychology: confirmation bias, the far more common practice of noticing only what agrees with a conclusion already reached. Even great scientists are not exempt. Munger described the physicist Max Planck's own observation that new ideas in physics rarely converted the physicists who held the old ones; instead, an entire generation had to age out of the field before its replacement could think freely. Albert Einstein himself, dazzlingly willing to demolish his own early ideas at the height of his career, later resisted the fuller implications of the quantum mechanics his own work had helped unlock.

The same anti-change reflex, Munger argued, is what makes a small early concession so dangerous in the hands of a skilled manipulator. Benjamin Franklin, rising from nothing in colonial Philadelphia, used to maneuver powerful men into doing him one small favor, like lending him a book, knowing that once a man had done Franklin a kindness, that man's own mind would resist believing he had been kind to someone unworthy of it. The identical mechanism, working step by tiny step, was the actual engine behind the wartime brainwashing techniques used on captured soldiers during the Korean War, techniques that Munger says outperformed torture at producing a mind that stayed altered. A quick decision and a stubborn one are not two separate weaknesses. They are the same valve, opening once and then refusing, ever after, to reopen.

## Chapter Five: A Crowd That Faces the Wrong Way

Put a stranger into a crowded elevator where every other passenger, planted there in advance, is silently facing the back wall instead of the doors, and the stranger will very often turn around too, for no better reason than that everyone else is doing it. Munger called this social-proof tendency: an automatic willingness to think and act as the people nearby are thinking and acting, strongest exactly when a person feels the most uncertain or the most stressed. It is a reasonable shortcut most of the time, since following a crowd out of a burning stadium usually beats trying to calculate your own exit alone. It becomes dangerous the moment the crowd itself is wrong, because the crowd's wrongness is now doing the convincing.

Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments at Yale in the early nineteen sixties remain the starkest laboratory version of the tendency's close cousin, authority-misinfluence. Ordinary volunteers, told by a lab-coated experimenter to deliver what they believed were increasingly painful electric shocks to another participant, kept going for a disturbing number of them: in the original published run, twenty-six of forty volunteers pushed the switch all the way to a label marked with a lethal-sounding voltage, even as the supposed victim on the other side of the wall screamed and then went silent. Munger's own reading of the result is that no single tendency explains a number that stark. The volunteer's own hesitation was met by an experimenter calmly insisting the procedure was fine, an authority figure's word carrying weight it had not earned; and by other bystanders in the room offering no objection at all, social proof doing the rest of the convincing. Two shortcuts, each mild by itself, together produced something neither would have produced alone.

Social proof cuts the opposite way too, when what a bystander imitates is another bystander's inaction. The 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York became the textbook opening case for exactly this idea, told for decades as the story of thirty-eight witnesses who watched a woman be stabbed to death and called no one, each one's silence read by the others as proof that no response was needed. Later reporting, including the paper's own 2016 correction of its original account, found that number badly inflated and the witnesses' actual view of the attack far more limited than the legend claimed; a real killing still happened, but the tidy morality tale built on top of it earns the same skepticism Munger asks of every other lesson in his checklist. Where the crowd's shape held up better was inside police departments themselves: an officer named Frank Serpico, refusing to join the routine corruption of his own New York precinct, was nearly killed by gunfire from men who should have been protecting him, a case where social proof and financial incentive worked together to make an entire unit complicit in the one form of misbehavior meant to be unthinkable in it. Catching the moment when everyone around you is simply repeating everyone else's mistake is, in Munger's own ranking, worth more than almost any other single skill on his list.

## Chapter Six: A Favor That Comes Back Doubled

Reciprocation tendency is Munger's name for something every culture on record has independently discovered: a favor given creates a debt in the receiver's own mind, one that gets settled whether or not the receiver ever consciously decides to settle it. The psychologist Cialdini demonstrated the mechanism's strength with an experiment run on his own university campus. Volunteers posing as researchers first asked passing strangers for something absurd, two years of weekly volunteer supervision of juvenile delinquents, and were refused by everyone. They then followed the refusal with a far smaller ask, one afternoon spent taking the same delinquents to a zoo. Asked cold, without the earlier refusal, only about one stranger in six agreed to the zoo trip. Asked as a concession after the bigger request had already been turned down, the acceptance rate roughly tripled, to about one in two, because the stranger's own mind registered the researcher's retreat to a smaller ask as a favor calling for one in return.

Munger traced the same mechanism into far higher stakes. The retailer Sam Walton, he noted, forbade his own purchasing agents from accepting so much as a free hot dog from a vendor, a policy Munger judged exactly right once reciprocation tendency is taken seriously: a five-dollar lunch can tilt a hundred-thousand-dollar purchasing decision, not through bribery in any crude sense, but through the same subconscious debt an automobile buyer feels toward a salesman who has just poured him a free cup of coffee. Cialdini himself connected the identical mechanism to the resignation of a sitting American president. In the run-up to the Watergate break-in, an aide pitched the nation's top law enforcement officer an outlandish scheme for undermining the opposing campaign; when that request was refused, the aide retreated to what now looked, by comparison, like a modest favor, authorization for a single burglary, and got it. The man who signed off was not a fool and was not for sale. He was settling a concession he had never consciously agreed to owe.

What makes reciprocation tendency worth a full chapter of its own, rather than a footnote to incentives, is how cleanly it operates below the level a person can catch by simply trying harder to be rational. Cialdini's zoo volunteers were not lying to themselves about money changing hands, because none did; the compliance drug went straight past their conscious calculation, and it works just as well on people who have every reason to think themselves too careful to fall for it. Munger's own antidote, borrowed wholesale from Walton, is not vigilance but structural refusal: never enter a transaction with even the smallest favor already owed on one side, because by the time the debt becomes visible, the debt has already done its work.

## Chapter Seven: A Reason Attached to Every Order

Not every item on Munger's list describes a failure. Reason-respecting tendency describes something closer to a lever, a trait strong enough to improve outcomes on purpose once a manager understands it. People, Munger argued, comply with an instruction far more readily, and remember it far longer, when they are told why the instruction exists rather than simply what to do. He credited the engineer Carl Braun, who built oil refineries around the world with what Munger called spectacular skill and integrity, with turning that observation into a company-wide rule: every written order inside Braun's firm had to specify who was to do what, where, when, and why, and an employee who left out the why risked being fired for it, whatever the technical quality of the rest of the instruction.

The tendency has an unflattering underside Munger did not gloss over. Because people respond to the presence of a reason almost as strongly as they respond to a good one, a supplied reason that is weak, or even nonsensical, still raises compliance well above giving no reason at all. Psychologists demonstrated this by having an experimenter cut in line at a busy photocopier with nothing more than the explanation "I have to make some copies," a reason that adds no actual information, since everyone in that line already needed to make copies, and people let the line-cutter through anyway. Munger's own conclusion cuts against the flattering half of the tendency: the habit that makes good management possible, a subordinate trusting an order that comes with its reasoning attached, is the identical habit that lets a hollow reason work almost as well as a sound one. That is why the phrase "because I said so" fails as management even when the order behind it happens to be correct.

## Chapter Eight: The Dog That Bites the Hand Holding Its Dinner

Munger illustrated one of his sharper tendencies with his own family's pet: a friendly dog that would bite, reliably and without malice, anyone who tried to take food out of its mouth once it already had the food, even its own owner. He called the human version deprival-superreaction tendency, the observation that losing something, or even having something nearly certain snatched away at the last second, wounds far out of proportion to how much a matching gain would have pleased. A person with ten million dollars in a brokerage account will still fume over a lost hundred-dollar bill.

Munger's own investing history supplied the plainest example of the tendency working against its own author. A broker once offered him three hundred shares of a badly underpriced, thinly traded oil stock, and Munger bought them outright with cash on hand. The next day, the same broker offered fifteen hundred more shares at the identical price, and Munger, a comfortably wealthy man with no debt and no real obstacle in the way, declined, later admitting the refusal made no rational sense at all. Within two years that stock was bought out at roughly thirty-two times what he had paid, a decision he estimated cost him something like five point four million dollars, a mistake he attributes squarely to his own psychology rather than to bad luck. It sits inside his own list as proof of the list's usefulness rather than an embarrassment to hide: even a mind trained to watch for the tendency can still be caught by it, once, expensively, and then used as its own best teaching case.

A companion tendency, excessive self-regard, explains why Munger thought face-to-face job interviews warrant far less trust than most hiring committees give them. People systematically rate their own judgment, their own possessions, and their own children above an outside observer's honest assessment, in the same way that ninety percent of Swedish drivers, when surveyed, rated their own driving as above average. Munger once sat on an academic search committee and pushed successfully to cancel further interviews entirely, appointing instead the candidate with the strongest documented record, on the theory that a charismatic ten-minute conversation reliably outweighs a candidate's actual history in the mind of an interviewer flattering itself that it can read character on sight. He later pointed to Hewlett-Packard's hiring of the polished, persuasive Carly Fiorina as the kind of decision his own antidote was built to prevent.

## Chapter Nine: Twenty People Hurt Twice, Explained

Return now to the darkened hangar this hour opened on, because Munger himself used that case to show what his checklist is actually for: not spotting one tendency at a time, but tracing how several compound into something none of them would produce alone, a result he named lollapalooza tendency. Reward-and-punishment superresponse pushed the aircraft maker to move fast, since no sale could close until the plane passed its test. Doubt-avoidance pushed the same team toward a quick decision rather than a slow, uncomfortable one. The regulator's own insistence on a realistic test triggered authority-misinfluence, driving the company toward a test method more dangerous than the letter of the requirement actually needed. Once that plan existed, inconsistency-avoidance helped keep it in place. Inside the dark hangar itself, any engineer's private unease met a room full of colleagues and supervisors showing no objection at all, social proof swamping the queasiness before it could turn into a raised hand.

Then came the morning test's own failure, with its injuries, which should have stopped everything. It did not, and the reason is the same deprival-superreaction tendency that made Munger's dog bite the hand pulling food from its mouth: the company stood to lose a certification program it had already sunk years and money into, and having come this close, the impulse to force the same plan through rather than absorb that loss overpowered the plain evidence sitting on the hangar floor. No one tendency on Munger's list would have produced a second test that afternoon. Five or six of them, pulling in the same direction at once, did. That, in Munger's own words, is what his checklist exists to catch: not a single named villain, but the ordinary case where a handful of mild, everyday shortcuts happen to line up.

## Chapter Ten: The Checklist Admits Its Own Seams

Munger did not present his list as a tidy, closed system, and that admission comes through best in something close to his own words. Asked, in the question period that closed his own talk, whether the twenty-five tendencies overlapped with each other and could just as easily have been organized some other way, he answered without hedging: "The answers are yes, yes, and yes." He went further, conceding that "a significant amount of messiness is unfixable in a soft science like psychology," a sentence that would sound like surrender from almost anyone else and instead reads, in context, like a man refusing to oversell his own tool.

That admission matters more than a throwaway aside, because it is also the cleanest opening for taking the checklist less on faith than its many admirers tend to. A list built by working backward from cases the builder already found persuasive will always fit those cases well. The real test of a list like this one is not whether it can explain, after the fact, why a hangar full of volunteers got hurt twice. Munger himself has already shown that it can. The test is whether it would have told anyone, in advance, which decisions were about to go wrong, and there the list's own architecture makes that a harder question to answer than its comfortable retellings usually let on.

## Chapter Eleven: Three Objections

The most exact version of that first objection is a purely logical one. A checklist assembled by collecting decades of bad decisions and then naming a tendency to match each one will, by construction, fit every bad decision fed into it, the same way a key cut after the lock has already been picked will always seem to fit. Munger's own talk supplies no case in which the checklist predicted a specific bad outcome before it happened, only cases in which it explained an outcome everyone already knew about. That is not a small gap. A tool that only ever explains the past, however satisfyingly, has not yet proven it can do anything the past could not already teach on its own.

A second, more technical objection comes from the German psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer, who has spent much of his career arguing against the entire framing Munger inherited from Kahneman-and-Tversky-style research: the habit of calling a mental shortcut a bias whenever it departs from an idealized model of pure rational calculation. Gigerenzer's own research on what he calls fast-and-frugal heuristics shows that many of these shortcuts perform better than the supposedly rational alternative once they are tested against the actual, uncertain environments people live in rather than against a clean laboratory puzzle built to expose their weak spot. Calling social proof or authority-deference a defect, in Gigerenzer's account, only makes sense if the alternative, weighing every decision from scratch with no reliance on other people's signals, would actually produce better outcomes on average, and for most of human history it would not have. Munger's own checklist rarely pauses to ask what these twenty-five tendencies get right before cataloguing what they get wrong, and a reader who only meets the list through Munger's own selection of cases will come away thinking the tendencies are closer to defects than to the tradeoffs Gigerenzer's research suggests they actually are.

The third and hardest-to-dismiss objection is Munger's own record. In the two years spanning nineteen seventy-three and seventy-four, Munger's investment partnership fell more than fifty percent from its peak, driven substantially by a leveraged, concentrated bet on two related stocks, the exact combination of overconfidence in his own judgment and an unwillingness to admit a losing position that his own checklist would later name and warn against. Decades later, in twenty twenty-one, he built a large stake in the Chinese technology company Alibaba for the small investment firm he chaired, added to the position as its price kept falling, and eventually admitted, in his own words at that firm's annual meeting, "I regard Alibaba as one of the biggest mistakes I ever made." By his own account, he had let himself be won over by the company's story rather than its actual business, the same influence-from-mere-association trap his own talk warns other people to watch for. A checklist that catches its own author only after the money is already gone is still useful. It is not the vaccine its most devoted readers sometimes mistake it for.

## Chapter Twelve: What the Checklist Is Actually For

None of those three objections erases what the list gets right, and it helps to be precise about what that is. Seven episodes on this shelf's run through Stripe Press's catalogue have each asked some version of the same institutional question: which structure, which funder, which peer-review process, which successor named in advance, keeps good work alive once the person who started it is gone. Vannevar Bush answered with a university department's own review structure. Donald Braben answered with one executive's personal shelter, which evaporated the week that executive left. Licklider named two successors before he needed to. Robert Taylor's own lab named none, and collapsed the day he quit. Martin Gurri, closing out that run, showed what happens once an institution can no longer control its own account of itself at all.

Munger's checklist answers a smaller, stranger question none of those seven books quite reached: not which institution should hold the trust, but what a single mind, sitting alone with a decision no committee will review, can do to catch itself before the mistake happens rather than after. It travels where an institution cannot, because it asks nothing of an employer, a funder, or a peer-review board, only of the person doing the deciding. That portability is also its whole limitation. Bush's peer-review structure did not need Bush to keep working once Bush was gone. Munger's checklist needs its user's attention every single time, with no institution standing behind it to catch the times attention lapses. That gap is where his own worst mistakes fell through.

The reading prescription that follows from that gap is a narrow one. Munger's talk earns a full, careful read, ideally with a pen in hand rather than a highlighter, since the list works only as a habit of re-checking, not as a fact to be filed away once absorbed. It earns that read with the same caveat Munger applied to his own dog: knowing exactly why a hand gets bitten does not, by itself, keep the hand out of the way next time. The generative habit to take out of this hour is not memorizing twenty-five names. It is picking one recent decision, ideally an uncomfortable one, and asking honestly which of Munger's shortcuts might have been doing more of the deciding than the reasons given for it out loud.
