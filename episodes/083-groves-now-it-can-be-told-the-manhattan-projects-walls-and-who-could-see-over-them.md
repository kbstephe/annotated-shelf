## Chapter One: Washington

In the middle of September 1942 a colonel in the Army Corps of Engineers was offered a posting overseas, and said yes with pleasure. He had spent two years in Washington running all of the Army's construction inside the United States, the camps, airfields, depots and ordnance plants, and he had just about finished building the Pentagon. Like every regular officer he wanted to command troops in a theatre of war. He needed only his superior's consent, and promised an answer by noon the next day.

The next morning, coming out of a congressional hearing on military housing, he caught his superior, General Brehon Somervell, in the corridor. Somervell told him he could not leave Washington. The Secretary of War had picked him for a special job, and the President had approved. If he did it right, Somervell said, it would win the war. The colonel knew at once what that meant. He had heard of the uranium project because his office built things for it, and he had not been impressed. It was expected to cost about a hundred million dollars in all, which he notes in his memoir was less than the Corps of Engineers spent in an ordinary week. Later that morning Somervell's chief of staff gave him the brief: the research was done, he had only to take the rough designs, build some plants and organise an operating force, and the war would be over. It took him several weeks, he writes, to see how far that was from the truth.

The colonel was Leslie Groves. Within three years he had spent something near two billion dollars, as the figure is usually given, built a city in Tennessee and another in the Washington desert, set up a laboratory on a mesa in New Mexico, and delivered two bombs to the Pacific.

This series has been asking of each of its doers who was placed to check them. Robert Moses was checked by nobody. Lee Kuan Yew checked himself. Hyman Rickover was checked by a reactor, and Norman Borlaug, last time, by a harvest that reported on tonnage and was blind to most of what his critics cared about. Groves offers a fifth answer, and a stranger one. He was checked by an enemy whose position nobody could see, in a race whose other runner was a guess. And he spent much of his energy building walls that kept everyone else from seeing him. So the question for this episode is what can audit a man in that position, and what his walls cost the people working inside them.

## Chapter Two: The First Week

The account I have read is Groves's own. Now It Can Be Told came out in 1962, sixteen years after he left the project, and it is the memoir of a man who is rarely wrong in his own pages. Beside it I have the Army's official history, Manhattan: The Army and the Atomic Bomb, by Vincent Jones, published in 1985 from the project's own files, which is more careful and less flattering, and the transcript of Groves's day as a witness at the 1954 hearing on Robert Oppenheimer's security clearance. Richard Rhodes's history of the bomb and Robert Norris's biography of Groves, subtitled The Manhattan Project's Indispensable Man, I could not open.

Start with his first week, because it shows the method whole.

On the afternoon he was appointed he called on Vannevar Bush, the engineer who ran the government's wartime science office. Nobody had told Bush about the appointment. He did not know who this blunt officer was, or why he was asking what he was asking, and refused to answer. As soon as Groves left, Bush got in touch with the Army to find out, and then wrote to the Secretary of War's assistant that he doubted the new man had enough tact for the job and feared they were in the soup. Groves reprints the note himself. He adds that he and Bush soon became fast friends and never once disagreed. That is the memoir in a sentence.

Two days later Groves went to see Donald Nelson, the head of the War Production Board, which decided who got scarce materials first. The project had a middling priority rating, and Groves had built enough camps to know that a middling rating meant waiting behind everybody else for steel and copper. Nelson said no. Groves replied that he would then have to recommend to the President that the project be abandoned, because the Board would not cooperate with the President's wishes. Nelson changed his mind on the spot, and Groves left with a letter in his pocket letting the project assign itself the top rating whenever it judged it needed one. He says he never knew why Nelson gave in so easily, and that he would have hated to take the matter to the President, since it was his problem and admitting frustration that early would have been distasteful. He had bluffed, and the bluff held for nearly a year.

The same week, one of his officers, Kenneth Nichols, went to see a Belgian mining executive named Edgar Sengier in New York. Sengier ran the company that owned the richest uranium mine in the world, in the Belgian Congo. Fearing a German invasion of Africa, he had discreetly shipped more than twelve hundred and fifty tons of ore to New York in 1940, and it was sitting in about two thousand steel drums in a warehouse on Staten Island. He had told the State Department about it three times that spring. Nothing had happened. When Nichols arrived, Sengier asked whether he had come to talk or to do business. Nichols left an hour later with the terms of a deal written on a sheet of yellow scratch paper, sealed with a handshake, the contract to follow.

In each of those three episodes the obstacle was a person or an office whose job was to say no, or to say not yet: the scientist who did not know who he was, the board that rationed materials, the department that had sat on the ore. Groves's method was to find the shortest path around each of them. He did not argue with the priority system; he threatened to go over it. He did not ask the State Department to reconsider; he sent a colonel with authority to buy. Before he even took over formally, he arranged to wait a few days for his promotion to brigadier general, because he expected the academic scientists to defer more readily to a general than to a promoted colonel, and he remarks that rank turned out to matter more among professors than among soldiers.

Every checkpoint he bypassed was also, in a modest way, an auditor. That is fine in a week. Over three years it adds up to a man answerable to very few people. Before following that, though, look at what the speed bought.

## Chapter Three: Buying Time

When Groves asked what the project actually knew, he was horrified. That is his word. The whole enterprise, he writes, rested on possibilities rather than probabilities, with a great deal of theory and not much proven knowledge. Nobody knew which of several ways of making bomb material would work: separating the light isotope of uranium with magnets, or by pushing a gas through porous barriers, or making a new element, plutonium, in a reactor. Nobody had built a reactor yet. The first chain reaction, in a Chicago laboratory, was still three months away.

The orthodox engineer's answer is to pick the likeliest method, build a pilot plant, learn from it, then build at scale. Groves did something else. He built production plants for several methods at once, and for the magnetic method he dropped the pilot plant entirely, so that research, development, construction and operation all ran at the same time. He says plainly that this would have been reckless in normal times. The bet was that money could buy time. Suppose, to make up numbers for the arithmetic, each method had an even chance of working: run three in parallel and the odds that at least one comes through rise to seven in eight.

A letter from the physicist Arthur Compton, two days before Christmas 1942, shows what he was managing. Compton, who ran the Chicago laboratory, wrote that plutonium production was ninety-nine percent likely to succeed, a working bomb ninety percent likely, and the first one could be delivered in 1944. The chemical company du Pont, which was to build the plutonium plant, was far gloomier, and Compton suggested replacing it with General Electric and Westinghouse, firms he had worked for and could persuade. Groves dismissed the letter. He judged the optimism unfounded and the reshuffle impractical, and in a footnote he records that the du Pont man's slower schedule proved right.

Then he gave du Pont a contract that shows the other half of the method. The company refused any fee or profit and wanted no patent rights, and the government took on every risk, since no insurer could price hazards nobody understood. For legal reasons the contract named a fee of one dollar. When du Pont was paid the whole dollar soon after the war ended, government auditors noticed that the contract's term had not fully run and asked for thirty-three cents back. Groves tells this with relish, and it is the right story to tell, because it shows the project working through existing organisations under contract rather than building its own. Bush, in the episode on his memoir Pieces of the Action, made that the founding principle of wartime research. Groves extended it from laboratories to industry and listed it, at the end of his book, among his five reasons for success.

Skipping the pilot plant had a price, and it arrived in Tennessee in November 1943. The magnetic separation plant at Oak Ridge used enormous oval racks of magnets and vacuum tanks the staff called racetracks. Because copper was short, its coils were wound from silver borrowed from the Treasury, which, when Nichols asked for several thousand tons, informed him that the Treasury did not speak of tons, its unit being the troy ounce. On the first test runs the tanks crept apart under the magnetic pull, and the coils began shorting. By early December several had failed outright. The official history traces the fault to mill scale and rust in the cooling oil, damp insulation and wire wound too tight. The coils went back to the factory to be rebuilt. A small trial installation would have found the fault for less money, and cost a year.

So the parallel bet had a cost structure you could see coming: whatever a pilot plant would have caught, the full-size plant caught instead, expensively and in public. The question was whether anything in the system could catch the problems that nobody had thought to test for. The answer came at Hanford.

## Chapter Four: The Extra Tubes

The first full-size plutonium reactor at Hanford, on the Columbia River in Washington State, was a block of graphite pierced by two thousand and four aluminium tubes, each to be filled with uranium slugs and cooled by river water. On the night of the twenty-sixth of September 1944, with something over nine hundred tubes loaded, the operators began withdrawing the control rods. Enrico Fermi, from the Chicago team, had guided the loading; this was to be the first plutonium made on an industrial scale. Power rose as planned. Then, without any change in the controls, it began to fall, and by the evening of the next day the reactor had shut itself down.

Nobody knew why. The operators suspected that water had leaked into the graphite and found none. The answer came from Chicago, where a smaller research reactor, run at full power for twelve hours for the first time, rose and then fell in the same pattern. One of the products of fission, a radioactive form of the gas xenon, swallows neutrons far more greedily than anyone had measured. The scientists at a test reactor in Tennessee had studied two other fission products for exactly this effect, found them harmless, and missed the third. At a meeting in Chicago on the third of October, the official history says, Groves was highly critical of the scientists for failing to discover something that might stop plutonium being made in time for the war.

What saved the plant was a disagreement. When du Pont's engineers designed the reactor, they had added more tubes than the physicists' calculations required, against the scientists' advice. The physicists had worked out how much uranium the reactor needed, and saw no reason to pay for capacity beyond it. The engineers, who had never built a reactor and, I take it, did not entirely trust anyone who claimed to know how one would behave, wanted room to spare. With every tube loaded, including the ones nobody was supposed to need, there was enough uranium to burn through the xenon. After weeks of cautious tests, on the twenty-eighth of December the Hanford reactor went into full production with all two thousand and four tubes filled.

That story is told often as a parable of engineering humility, and it is one. But put it in this episode's terms. The check that worked at Hanford was not Groves, and it was not a test. It was a second profession's distrust of the first, written into steel. The physicists and the engineers disagreed about how much to trust a calculation, and instead of settling the argument, the design kept both answers: the physicists' number of tubes in use, and the engineers' number in the graphite. The spare capacity was the disagreement, preserved. Nobody had predicted xenon. The margin did not need anyone to.

For a check of that kind to exist, the two sides had to be in the same room, arguing about the same object. Most of the Manhattan Project was arranged so that they would not be.

## Chapter Five: The Rule

Groves's rule for information takes two lines in his security chapter and is the thing most people remember about him. "Compartmentalization of knowledge, to me, was the very heart of security," he writes. Each person should know everything he needed to do his job and nothing else. The rule, he adds, did more than protect secrets. It made people stick to their own work, and it made clear that the project existed to produce one thing, not to let individuals satisfy their curiosity.

Take the steelman first, and it is a strong one. The Manhattan Project at its peak, at the end of June 1944, employed nearly a hundred and twenty-nine thousand people, most of them construction workers who were building plants whose purpose they did not know. A secret held by a hundred thousand people is not a secret. Groves knew within a week or two of taking over that the Russians were already spying on the Berkeley laboratory through American sympathisers, and his book lists three aims for security: keep the Germans from learning anything, make the bomb's first use a total surprise, and keep the Russians from learning the designs. He notes that nobody above him ever named a particular country as the target of his security effort.

Compartments also made management possible at that scale. In his final chapter Groves gives five reasons for the project's success, and they are a fair summary of an industrial theory of how to do something nobody has done. A single, unmistakable objective. A specific task for each part, allocated so that the parts add up. Clear authority, delegated completely along with responsibility. Maximum use of organisations that already existed. And full backing from government. Dividing the work that way is compartmentalisation, and he credits it with an efficiency, in his phrase, without precedent. The use of existing organisations came with a promise: because the objective was finite, he writes, the organisation was not designed to last forever, and so nobody had reason to build an empire.

And where the rule threatened the work, he broke it himself. The Army's security officers would not clear Oppenheimer, whose past associations were on the political left. Groves read the files himself, decided the man's value outweighed the risk, and on the twentieth of July 1943 wrote to the district engineer that Oppenheimer was to be cleared without delay, irrespective of the information against him, because he was absolutely essential to the project. That is the steelman at full strength: a rule, enforced hard, with one person entitled to make exceptions and answerable for them by name.

The wall kept out more than spies, though. In June 1943 a Senate committee chaired by Harry Truman, set up to investigate the conduct of the defence programme, began asking why the War Department had bought so much farmland in Washington State. The Secretary of War, Henry Stimson, contacted Truman and asked him to drop the inquiry for reasons of security, and Truman agreed, on the understanding that Stimson would take full responsibility. The following February, when the project needed at least six hundred million dollars for the coming year, Stimson, Bush and General George Marshall briefed the Speaker of the House and the two party leaders, who promised to pass the money through committee in a way that would not disclose its purpose. A year later a congressman on the appropriations committee who had heard rumours of waste demanded to inspect the plants. He was talked out of objecting on the floor by being promised a visit to some outside installations.

The State Department, Groves notes, was not told of the project until early 1945. So by the middle of the war the committee meant to audit military spending had stood down, the appropriations committees were voting on a number they could not examine, and the diplomats who would have to manage the bomb's consequences did not know it existed. Each step had a defensible reason. Together they meant that the man running the largest secret enterprise in the country had very few people placed to check his work, and those few, Stimson and Bush and Marshall, were busy running a war.

## Chapter Six: From Inside

What did compartments feel like from inside? The best witness is Oppenheimer, testifying in 1954 about the summer of 1942, before Groves arrived. He had gone round the scattered laboratories working on pieces of the bomb, and found them suffering from their isolation. There was supposed to be security, he said, and there was good compartmentalisation, so that nobody knew what was going on anywhere else; work was duplicated, and there was almost no sense of hope or direction. Los Alamos, in his own account of its founding, was meant as the cure: one central laboratory where people could talk freely and where the waste and error of all those separate studies could stop.

So the laboratory that designed the bomb was the project's great exception to its own rule. Inside the fence at Los Alamos, ideas moved between groups, and its scientists held regular colloquia where the work was discussed across specialities. Groves allowed it, reluctantly. At the hearing in 1954 he said he had thought it a serious mistake, at the time and since, to let the openness run down the ranks; the leaders, perhaps twenty or thirty of them, yes, but not as many as were allowed.

His testimony that day is more candid than his book. The academic scientists, he said, had spent their lives disseminating knowledge and found the rule a terrible upset, and he never held it against them. He told of riding the train to Los Alamos with Niels Bohr and spending about twelve hours explaining what Bohr was not to say there, and of Bohr, within five minutes of arriving, saying all of it. He told of Ernest Lawrence standing at a blackboard and beginning with the remark that he knew General Groves did not want him to say this. And then, asked whether this had been a military organisation, he said that it had not, and could not have been. People got him into corners where he had to approve things they knew he did not want. "If I had a group of yes men we never would have gotten anywhere."

That sentence is the steelman's undoing, and Groves says it himself. The organisation succeeded, by his own account, because the people in it kept breaking the rule he credits with its success.

There is a case that makes the cost concrete. Since 1941 a young physicist named Philip Abelson, working for the Navy and with Navy money, had been separating uranium isotopes by a third method, thermal diffusion: a tall column with a hot pipe inside a cold one, in which the lighter isotope drifts toward the hot side and rises. On its own the process was hopeless for a bomb. Groves estimated that running it as a complete process on its own would cost at least two billion dollars, mostly in steam. So nobody pursued it, and because it belonged to the Navy, which had been deliberately kept out of the project, nobody inside the wall paid it much attention.

In the spring of 1944 Oppenheimer, reading some year-old reports, saw what everyone had missed. Thermal diffusion could not make finished bomb material, but it did not have to. It could enrich uranium a little, and that slightly richer uranium could be fed into the magnetic plant in Tennessee, which would then produce more. On the twenty-eighth of April he wrote to Groves that this might raise the magnetic plant's output by some thirty or forty percent, many months earlier than the gas plant would come on line. Groves let a month pass before acting.

The official history, written from the Army's own files, gives two reasons for the delay in seeing the idea at all. Everyone had thought of each method as a complete process on its own. And because the work was being done by an organisation outside the Army-run project, it had not attracted the interest of the project's scientists and engineers, while the Army's administrators feared the security problems of bringing outsiders in. Groves himself said later that he did not know why nobody had suggested it a year earlier.

Once he moved, he moved in his usual way. In late June he approved a plant. His advisers thought six months an optimistic schedule; he ordered it in full operation in four, its parts copied from the Navy's own design. The first racks were running that autumn, with steam escaping from the joints, and staff learned to work through the noise. Its product was only slightly richer than natural uranium, but the plan on which Groves approved it promised about a fifth more output from the magnetic plant.

The idea needed one person to see two compartments at once, the Navy's columns and the Army's magnets. The rule was designed so that almost nobody would. The person who saw both was Oppenheimer, who by Groves's own testimony had become the scientist he relied on for advice across the whole project. And the person who then sat on the idea for a month, because it meant opening the wall to outsiders, was the man who had built the wall.

Then there were the spies. Klaus Fuchs, a German-born physicist on the British team, learned much of the bomb's design at Los Alamos and passed it to Soviet agents from June 1945 until his arrest in 1950. David Greenglass, an Army sergeant there, passed material through his brother-in-law Julius Rosenberg. Groves's book blames the British for vouching for Fuchs without investigating him. At the hearing he said the Greenglass case could not have happened if junior staff at Los Alamos had kept the rules. Both may be true. But the leaks came from inside the one open compartment, from people who were cleared and in the right room. The wall had been reliable against Congress, the State Department and the Navy. It had not stopped the enemy listed third among its aims.

## Chapter Seven: A Race Against a Guess

A race is a kind of audit. If you know where your rival is, you know whether your pace is good enough, and anyone watching can judge you against the rival. For most of the war Groves's rival was Germany, the only enemy, as his book puts it, with the industry and scientists to use what it might learn. But nobody knew where Germany was. The race was against an estimate.

So Groves set out to find out, and one of the more surprising parts of his book is that he ran his own intelligence mission. It was called Alsos, and it followed the Allied armies into Europe looking for German nuclear scientists and their papers. On the twenty-fifth of November 1944 its advance party entered Strasbourg with the troops, occupied the laboratories of the German-run university, and detained seven physicists and chemists. The men themselves said little of use. Their files said a great deal. Pieced together from notes of meetings, fragments of calculation and personal letters, they showed that Hitler had been told of the possibility of a bomb in 1942, and that at the end of 1944 the German effort was still at the experimental stage. Groves writes that this greatly increased his belief that there was little chance of any sudden nuclear surprise from Germany. He wanted more confirmation, and got it in the spring.

The rival had vanished. Nothing in the project slowed. The bombs were already being built for Japan, and at the hearing, asked whether there had been extra urgency at the end, Groves said nobody in a responsible position had imagined the Japanese war ending as soon as it did. That may well be right, and the decision to use the bomb is a separate argument this episode will not try to settle. Once Germany was out of the race, the project's pace was no longer measured against anyone. It was measured against a date Groves and his superiors chose.

That leaves one auditor, and it reported once. At half past five in the morning of the sixteenth of July 1945, in the desert near Alamogordo, the first bomb was tested, and it worked. It examined the whole system at once, it could not be argued with, and it did not care who had designed which part. It was also binary and final. It could say the bomb worked. It could not say whether the thermal diffusion plant should have been built a year earlier, what the weeks lost to xenon at Hanford had cost, or whether the compartments had saved more time than they lost.

Here, then, is my answer to the series question, revised from the one I started with. I began by saying Groves was checked by an invisible rival. Strasbourg removed even that. What the compartment rule did, when you follow it through, was to make the man at the top the only person who could see the whole project, and therefore the one man positioned to audit it. Rickover, in the episode before last, drew a boundary of responsibility: everything inside his line had his name on it, and what failed was what lay just outside. Groves drew boundaries of knowledge, and placed himself above all of them. The project could be checked part by part, by test runs and failures, and as a whole only by Groves and by the explosion. The two catches in this story, at Hanford and on thermal diffusion, both came where his design had been overridden. The rest depended on what one head could hold, and Groves's head, by every account including his critics', held a great deal. The trouble with that design is not that the head was too small. It is that nobody else could tell when it was full.

## Chapter Eight: The Finite Objective

Groves promised that his organisation was not built to last, because its objective was finite. It lasted.

The Army had expected to hand the project to a civilian body soon after the war. In fact, as the official history puts it, its trusteeship was unexpectedly prolonged, to the end of December 1946, while Congress argued over what that body should be. Groves ran the plants, the stockpile and the secrets in the meantime. In September 1945 Stimson wrote to the President that the Soviet Union would build its own bomb within four to twenty years, and urged a direct approach to Moscow on control, without negotiating with the bomb on America's hip. Groves says he agreed. Nothing came of it. In September 1949 the American government announced that the Soviets had tested a bomb.

The compartments outlived the war too, and here the Oppenheimer story closes the circle. In March 1947, when the new Atomic Energy Commission was reviewing the people the Army had cleared, Groves wrote a statement for it. He had cleared Oppenheimer in 1943 knowing that no agency concerned only with security would ever do so, because he had judged the man loyal and his value overwhelming. He added that the Commission should not automatically accept anyone on his list, but should exercise its own judgment under the new law.

Seven years later, at the hearing, counsel asked him about that law, and he read out the clause. It said that a person could have access to secret data only if the Commission had determined that this would not endanger the common defence. Groves then explained how he read the word endanger. Not proof that someone was a danger, he said, but a reasonable presumption that he might be, five or ten percent, with no consideration whatever given to his past performance or his usefulness, however imperative. On that reading of the law, he told the board, he would not clear Oppenheimer today.

Put that beside the letter of 1943. Then the rule was his, and the exception was his, and he signed it, irrespective of the file, because the man was essential. By 1954 the rule had been written into a statute, and the exception had not. Nobody in the new system had the standing Groves had given himself, to look at the whole file and the whole value and decide. What remained was a standard that weighed only the risk. This series already met what happens when a decision a person used to make and answer for is moved into a rule that nobody answers for: the accountability sink, from Dan Davies in the episodes on The Unaccountability Machine. Groves's compartments had always depended on a person above them entitled to break them. The law kept the compartments and dropped the person.

The board voted against Oppenheimer's clearance. Groves's testimony was one strand of that outcome, not the cause, and he had said in the same breath that he would be amazed if Oppenheimer had ever knowingly done anything disloyal. But he was the most authoritative witness to the system Oppenheimer had worked inside, and he described it accurately: a rule that worked only while someone stood above it.

## Chapter Nine: Dose

Groves's book is long and uneven, and the chapters to read are few. The first two, on his appointment and his first week, give the method in miniature, the soup note included. The fourth, on the plutonium decision, has Compton's letter and the one-dollar contract. The tenth, on security, is where the rule is stated, and the last, A Final Word, has the five reasons. The Alsos chapters are good adventure and add little except Strasbourg. The memoir's great limitation is that it is written to be right; read the official history beside it. Jones's book is free from the Army's Center of Military History, and its chapter on thermal diffusion shows what a wall costs in a dozen pages. For Groves without the polish, his day at the 1954 hearing runs to some fifty pages of transcript, posted by the Energy Department's technical information office, and he is franker there than anywhere in print.

The trial is the one kind of organisation in medicine built on Groves's rule on purpose. The blinding plan says who may know what: the participant does not know her allocation, the investigators do not see the accumulating comparison, and a monitoring committee above them sees everything and may stop the trial. Those walls exist for good reasons, and they share the thermal diffusion plant's weakness. Some questions live in a pair of rooms nobody is asked to hold together: the adverse-event reports and the pharmacy's dispensing records, say, or the screening logs and the consent forms. The committee with the widest view is usually chartered to look at outcomes, not at the trial's own plumbing. A protocol team that drew its blinding plan as a chart of boxes, with a line wherever a named person may see into two of them, would find the unlined pairs in an afternoon, and could give each one an owner before recruitment opens rather than after the first audit.
