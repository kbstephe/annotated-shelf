## Chapter One: The Question at the Chemistry Table

A mathematician walks into a chemistry lab at lunchtime and asks to sit down. He has worked with one of the men there before, and the man is courting the department secretary, so it would be awkward to say no. For a week the mathematician just eats and listens. Then he starts asking a question: what are the important problems of your field? He asks it again a few days later, phrased slightly differently: what important problems are you working on? After a week or two of this he asks the version that ends the friendship: if what you are doing is not important, and you don't think it will lead to anything important, why are you at this laboratory working on it? Nobody invites him back to that table.

Five months pass. It is autumn now, and one of the chemists, a man named Dave McCall, stops the mathematician in the hallway. That remark got under his skin, he says. He thought about it all summer. He hasn't changed his research, but the thinking had been worthwhile. The mathematician says thank you and walks on. Within a few years McCall is head of his department. Later he is elected to the National Academy of Engineering. The mathematician never again hears the names of the other men who sat at that table, the ones who found the question rude rather than useful.

The mathematician is Richard Hamming, and on the seventh of March, nineteen eighty-six, he stood in front of roughly two hundred people at Bell Communications Research in Morristown, New Jersey, and told that story as evidence for a claim he had spent forty years assembling: that the gap between a scientist whose work lasts and one whose work is forgotten by the time he retires is not, mostly, a gap in raw ability. Hamming had earned the right to make the claim from the inside. Born in Chicago in nineteen fifteen, he took a doctorate at the University of Illinois, worked the last desperate year of the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, and then spent thirty years at Bell Labs, where he built the error-correcting codes that let computers catch and fix their own mistakes, work that later won him the Turing Award. His own epitaph, by his own choosing, was a line about method rather than result: the purpose of computing is insight, not numbers.

The talk was called "You and Your Research," and it was never written for publication. Someone in the audience taped it, a colleague transcribed it, and the transcript circulated inside Bell Labs and then well beyond it for decades before Hamming's own book, "The Art of Doing Science and Engineering," gathered it as the closing chapter, reissued by Stripe Press in a lecture-hall edition slim enough to read in one sitting. It is the second stop on this shelf, after last week's tour of Patrick Collison's own web pages, and it answers a narrower version of the question those pages kept circling. Collison asked why some institutions produce more than others. Hamming asked the version one level down: inside any institution, however good, why do so few of the individually capable people in it do work that lasts?

## Chapter Two: The Stooge at Los Alamos

Hamming's answer began, by his own account, in envy. He had been brought there to run the computing machines so the physicists could get back to the actual science, and he watched them do it at close range: Richard Feynman, Hans Bethe, who was his boss, Enrico Fermi passing through, Robert Oppenheimer running the whole enterprise. He was, he said plainly, the stooge who kept the machines fed. Physically he was the same species as these men. Something else was different, and he wanted to know what.

That is not a scientist's question so much as a clinician's. Rather than build a theory of genius from first principles, Hamming did what a diagnostician does with a disease of unknown cause: he watched, he asked pointed questions of people who had already done great work, he read biographies and autobiographies hunting for the pattern that recurred across physics, mathematics, and the coding theory that was his own field. He was honest, from the first minute of the talk, about what kind of evidence this was. He was not testing a hypothesis against a control group. He was interviewing survivors and inferring the disease from what they had in common. That method carries its own weakness, and it stays relevant all the way to the end of this hour, because Hamming himself names it once, in passing, and the rest of the talk proceeds as if he hadn't.

## Chapter Three: The Prepared Mind

The first objection Hamming anticipated was the easiest one to raise and, he thought, the easiest to answer badly: that great science is simply luck. He didn't deny that luck exists. He denied that it explains the pattern. Consider Einstein, he said: not one good idea but a whole career of them, publishing across radically different problems for decades. Consider Claude Shannon, who did not stop at information theory but had already done substantial work in cryptography before it, work still classified when Hamming gave the talk. A single stroke of luck does not repeat itself decade after decade in the same person. Hamming reached for Louis Pasteur's old formula and made it the spine of the whole lecture: luck favors the prepared mind. The particular discovery may be chance. That a person keeps discovering things is not.

He offered Einstein again as the clean case of preparation without luck. Around the age of twelve or fourteen, long before any physics training, Einstein asked himself what a beam of light would look like if he could travel alongside it at its own speed. Electromagnetic theory said a light wave cannot have a stationary crest. But ride alongside one at the speed of light and a crest would appear to sit still. A boy noticing that contradiction is not the same event as a man resolving it into special relativity thirteen years later, but the first event is a necessary seed for the second, and most people who could pass the same physics exam never noticed the contradiction at all. This is the gap Hamming's whole talk lives in: not who can solve a problem once handed it, but who goes looking for the problem before anyone hands it to them.

## Chapter Four: Brains Is Not Enough

Raw intelligence, in Hamming's account, sits closer to a necessary condition than a sufficient one, and he had two colleagues at Bell Labs to prove it. Bill Pfann, who invented zone melting, a purification technique now standard in semiconductor manufacturing, came into Hamming's office with an idea he could barely articulate and equations he couldn't quite finish. Hamming did the mathematics for him and taught him to run the computers himself. Pfann went on, with almost no recognition inside his own department at first, to collect most of the major prizes in the field, and the shyness and inarticulateness fell away once the ideas started landing. A colleague named Clogston arrived with so little apparent promise that Hamming would have let him go; only the department head's patience kept him on. Clogston went on to develop the Clogston cable, after which, in Hamming's phrase, one success brought him confidence, and confidence brought a steady stream of good ideas afterward.

The trait underneath both stories, Hamming argued, was courage: the willingness to trust a half-formed idea long enough to finish it. His clearest example was Shannon's own founding theorem of information theory. Shannon wanted a method for coding messages so they could survive noise. He didn't know how to construct a good code directly, so he asked an almost absurd question instead: what would the average of every possible random code do? He then proved that the average random code was arbitrarily good, which meant at least one actual good code had to exist somewhere in that average. Hamming's verdict on the maneuver was blunt: only a man of complete courage could have dared think that far past what he could prove directly. Courage, on this account, is not a personality trait so much as a willingness to keep working on an idea after the part of your mind that wants a guaranteed answer has already voted no.

## Chapter Five: What a Bad Office Can Teach You

Working conditions, Hamming insisted, do not behave the way anyone expects. He pointed to the Cambridge Physical Laboratory, some of whose best physics was done in what amounted to shacks, and then told a story on himself. When he arrived at Bell Labs it was clear he would never get the small army of programmers that the era's absolute-binary machines seemed to demand. He spent a long while resenting the shortfall before turning the question around: if the machines could do practically everything else, why couldn't they be made to write programs themselves? The limitation that looked like a defect pushed him early into what became automatic programming, years ahead of most of the field. A fault, looked at from a different angle, is often the asset in disguise; you are simply never in a position to see that while you're still angry about the fault.

Underneath conditions sat drive, and Hamming's clearest lesson on drive came secondhand, from his department head, Bode. Hamming had discovered that his colleague John Tukey, whose command of the field left him in awe, was in fact slightly younger than he was, and stormed into Bode's office to ask how anyone his own age could know that much. Bode leaned back and told him he would be surprised how much he'd know too, if he worked as many hours as Tukey did. Knowledge and productivity, Bode said, behave like compound interest: two people of near-identical ability, one working ten percent harder than the other, and over years the harder worker's output is not ten percent greater but something closer to double. Hamming took the remark home and, he admitted, neglected some evenings with his wife because of it. Edison had said something cruder about the same fact: genius is ninety-nine percent perspiration and one percent inspiration. Hamming's addition was that the perspiration had to be aimed; plenty of his hardest-working colleagues at Bell Labs had less to show for it than he did, because effort misapplied is still misapplied.

He added one more trait that looks, on the surface, like a contradiction of everything said so far: tolerance for ambiguity. The best scientists believe their own theory enough to keep building on it, and simultaneously distrust it enough to catch the small facts that don't quite fit. Darwin wrote in his own autobiography that he forced himself to record every piece of evidence that seemed to contradict his theory the moment he caught it, because otherwise his mind would let the inconvenient fact slide and remember only the comfortable one. Believe a theory completely and the cracks stop being visible. Doubt it past a certain point before you've built anything, and there is never enough standing yet to show a crack.

## Chapter Six: The Attack You Actually Have

This brings the talk back to the chemistry table, and to the definition Hamming built the rest of his argument on. An important problem, in his usage, is not defined by the size of its consequences. Time travel, teleportation, and reversing gravity were, by his own account, never worked on at Bell Labs despite guaranteeing a Nobel Prize and unlimited funding to whoever solved them, because nobody had so much as a plausible first move against any of the three. What makes a problem important is that a person can see a route into it: a reasonable attack, in his phrase, not a reasonable payoff. Most working scientists, he argued, spend nearly all their time on problems they privately believe are neither important now nor likely to lead anywhere important later, because an attack on them is obvious and safe, never mind whether the outcome would matter to anyone.

To force himself off that path, Hamming adopted a private discipline he called Great Thoughts Time: an hour set aside, generally at the end of the week, reserved for nothing but the largest questions in his field, with the smaller safe problems banned from the conversation entirely. One product of those hours was a prediction he made out loud to Bell Labs vice presidents years before it looked plausible: that the ratio of experiments done on a computer to experiments done in the physical laboratory, then roughly one in ten, would flip to nine in ten. He was treated, in his own telling, as the crazy mathematician with no sense of reality. The laboratories the company built anyway, on the assumption he was wrong, stood mostly unused within his own working lifetime. Great Thoughts Time was not daydreaming. It was reserving deliberate hours to ask which of the safe, fundable, obviously attackable problems in front of him were actually pointed somewhere, and which were just comfortable.

## Chapter Seven: The Door

The most quoted single observation from the talk is also the one Hamming himself hedges hardest. He noticed, watching colleagues at Bell Labs over years, that the people who kept their office doors closed got more done today and tomorrow than the people who left them open, and were more productive by any short measure you could name. Ten years on, the closed-door workers somehow didn't know which problems in the field were the right ones, and the open-door workers, interrupted constantly, kept picking up the one fragment of information that mattered later. He could not prove which way the causation ran. A closed door might simply be a symptom of a mind that was already closing, rather than the cause of one closing. He said so, in the same breath as the claim, and then moved on without resolving it.

His own best story for what an open door actually buys came from an ordinary morning years earlier at the labs' New York site, though he never told it in this particular talk. A relay computer had run his job unattended for a weekend and caught an error in its own checking circuit, which meant it simply stopped rather than delivering a wrong answer, and when the office reopened Hamming had nothing to show for the lost days. His first reaction was irritation at a machine smart enough to catch its own mistake but too dumb to fix it. His second reaction, the one that mattered, was the same move he made with the automatic-programming shortfall: if the machine can detect the error, why can't it correct it too? Three years of work later, in nineteen fifty, he published the error-correcting codes that made his professional name, the ones a generation of engineering students would come to know simply as Hamming codes. The frustration was private. The reframing of the frustration into a general, attackable problem happened in a building full of colleagues he talked to constantly, not behind a closed door working the specific weekend's specific failure alone.

The productivity writer Cal Newport, reading the talk decades later, offered the complication that anyone trying to apply it today has to sit with. Bell Labs in the nineteen sixties and seventies meant an open door produced, at most, one or two unplanned conversations in a day. The distraction available to anyone with a laptop and a notification badge now runs, in Newport's own words, at a level orders of magnitudes larger than what Hamming was ever weighing against a closed door. The advice to stay interruptible was built for an interruption rate that no longer exists anywhere a person might read this sentence.

## Chapter Eight: Selling It, and the Cost of a Bolo Tie

Hamming was unusually direct, for a scientist of his generation, about the fact that doing the work is not the end of the job. Nobody is waiting to rush out and celebrate a result the moment it exists; everyone else is busy with their own work, and a scientist has to write and speak well enough that a reader stops turning pages and lands on his. He described watching colleagues who stayed silent through a hard meeting and then, weeks later, filed a report explaining what should have been done, arriving too late to matter, and he taught himself, at real personal cost, to say the unpopular thing out loud while the decision was still open.

Two stories about personality carried the sharper lesson. Early in his career, dressing in the western clothes he preferred, Hamming noticed he was getting worse service at a rented computing facility than colleagues who looked more conventional. Nobody had ordered it; the secretaries controlling the queue were simply reading him as someone who didn't belong, and adjusting accordingly. He chose to dress the way the room expected rather than the way he preferred, and the service improved immediately. His friend and colleague John Tukey made the opposite choice, dressing however he wanted into important meetings for his whole career, and paid for it every time with a room slower to take him seriously than his actual ability warranted. Hamming's judgment on his friend was not gentle: a genius anyway, who spent decades of needless friction defending a preference that cost him more than it was worth. He drew the line carefully. He was not against asserting yourself. He was against paying a permanent, compounding tax for an assertion nobody actually needed you to make.

The same discipline showed up in how he handled institutions rather than people. A colleague who wanted his office moved back near the library filed the request and got, in return, a demand for a map showing which paths he would take so the company could insure him, followed weeks later by a demand for exactly how his bicycle would be stored and locked. He eventually gave up the fight rather than the job, and went on to become president of Bell Laboratories. Another colleague wrote a testy letter of protest to a professional society about the physical height of a journal's spine relative to the lab's shelving, sent it up for his boss's signature, and never learned whether it was actually mailed. Hamming's own rule for himself was to treat that kind of institutional friction as something to twit occasionally, for the amusement of it, and then drop, because the researchers who let a small grievance escalate into an ongoing war with the system were, without exception, the ones who never produced the work the war was supposedly protecting.

He was equally blunt about the excuses people give themselves for not having done the work in the first place. Look at the history of any real discovery, he said, and you typically find a handful of people who were close enough to have made it and didn't; every one of them has a story ready for why. I had the idea already. I was building the equipment. I would have gotten there eventually. Hamming's advice was to let other people believe whatever excuse gets offered and to refuse the same excuse in private: credit in science goes to whoever actually finished, and the difference between the person who finished and the nine who had the same idea first is rarely talent. It is usually just the finishing.

## Chapter Nine: Seven Years, and Whose Vision

A pattern he never mentioned in the prepared part of the talk came out only when the floor opened for questions, and it is harder to defend than anything that came before it. Hamming told the room he thought a researcher should force a real change of field roughly every seven years, on the theory that everyone eventually exhausts a vein of original ideas and starts coasting on technique, marching in a direction that used to be right long after the world has moved. He had done it to himself deliberately: from numerical analysis to hardware to software and on, refusing even to keep reading the literature in a field once he'd decided to leave it, so that nothing could pull him back. Then he turned the same yardstick on a friend. When Claude Shannon left Bell Labs, Hamming told the audience flatly that Shannon's scientific career was over, and when colleagues objected that Shannon was every bit as sharp as before, Hamming's answer was that sharpness was never the question; what mattered was that Shannon had stopped starting over as a beginner in something new, and a mind that stops doing that stops producing, however fast it can still think.

It is a hard verdict to check. Shannon's decades after Bell Labs were, by any conventional measure, quiet: no theorem to rival his nineteen forty-eight paper, though he kept a legendary workshop of gadgets and games that colleagues who visited him never forgot. Whether that counts as ruin, or simply as a man who had done one era-defining thing and preferred, afterward, to build a rocket-powered frisbee, is a judgment about the value of a life's second half that Hamming makes with a confidence the evidence doesn't obviously support. He is scoring Shannon against Hamming's own definition of a scientific career, and awarding a loss, in a single sentence, to a man not in the room to answer for himself.

The same question period supplied a gentler idea, about the choice between doing the work yourself and building the conditions for other people to do it. Hamming once asked his own department head, Bode, why he had left research to run the department instead of staying a working scientist. Bode's answer was that he had a picture of what mathematics at Bell Labs could become, and the picture was larger than anything he could build with his own two hands, so making it real meant becoming the person who decided who worked on what. Hamming's gloss on the exchange was the closest the whole lecture comes to a career map rather than a list of traits: a project one person can finish alone is one you should stay and finish yourself; a project too big for any single set of hands, his own included, only gets built by someone willing to become the person who decides who works on what. Neither choice beats the other in general. The mistake, in his telling, is drifting into either one without ever working out which size of project you're actually holding.

## Chapter Ten: The Biased Sample

Near the very end of the talk, answering a question about whether any of this is actually worth the personal cost, Hamming said something that undercuts the entire lecture more than any outside critic has managed to since. He admitted that his survey, if you could call it that, was drawn entirely from people who had already succeeded. He had never gone and asked the scientists who worked with their doors open, believed in important problems, and showed real courage, and still produced nothing lasting, how any of it had felt from the inside. It's a biased sample, he said, and moved straight on to his answer anyway.

He offered that admission about one narrow question, whether greatness is worth the personal cost of pursuing it. It is true of the entire talk. Every story in "You and Your Research" is a story about someone who worked hard, took risks, and won. There is no comparable account of the Bell Labs scientist who kept meticulous Great Thoughts Time, left his door open for thirty years, defined an important problem the way Hamming recommends, and still produced nothing anyone remembers. That scientist almost certainly existed, in numbers, inside the same building, during the same decades. Hamming never interviewed him, because there was no reason to; he wasn't famous enough to be worth the question. A talk built entirely from the survivors cannot tell you which of the survivors' traits actually caused their survival and which merely traveled alongside it, any more than a hospital chart review of patients who recovered can tell you, without a control arm, which of the treatments they happened to receive actually did the work.

The online annotated edition of the transcript, maintained by the writer Gwern Branwen, catches two smaller instances of the same problem inside the talk's own evidence. Hamming's favorite line, attributed to Newton, that others would get his results too if they thought as hard as he did, turns up nowhere in Newton's actual writing and reads, on the annotator's own inspection, nothing like him; it is most likely a line Hamming half-remembered and made Newton's for the sake of the authority. And Hamming's harshest single verdict in the whole lecture, that the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton ruined more good scientists than any institution ever created, judged by what its fellows did before arriving against what they did after, runs into an obvious rival explanation the annotation names directly: the Institute recruited people at the exact moment they were already at the peak of a career, which means a falloff afterward looks less like an institution's failure and more like an ordinary statistical fact about peaks. In two thousand seven, the medical researchers Thomas Erren, Paul Cullen, Michael Erren, and Philip Bourne condensed the talk into ten rules for a journal audience of working scientists, rule nine telling a reader to commit single-mindedly to a problem and rule ten telling the same reader to leave the door open; their own gloss is that the open door serves the commitment, feeding a mind that is already immersed rather than competing with it, which restates Hamming's hedge in tidier language without actually settling which one governs on a given working day.

None of this makes the talk worthless. It makes it something narrower than it presents itself as: not a proof that these traits cause important work, but a rich, honestly-told description of what important work has looked like from the inside, gathered by someone who was there and cared enough to ask. That is still valuable. It is just not evidence in the sense the confident, declarative sentences keep implying it is.

## Chapter Eleven: What an R01 Is For

Strip away the parts of the talk that only make sense inside a monopoly-funded industrial laboratory with lifetime employment and no grant cycle, which is most of the working-conditions material and a good deal of the door-open advice besides, and one working tool survives the trip into a modern research career essentially intact: the definition of an important problem as one you have an actual attack on, rather than one whose stakes are simply large.

That distinction does real, specific work for anyone who sits on the other side of a funding decision from where Hamming stood. A study section reading a grant application is not asking whether the disease under study matters; almost every disease that gets proposed against matters enormously to somebody, so stakes alone were never going to be the filter. The section is asking, whether it says so explicitly or not, whether this particular investigator has a plausible route from the money to an answer nobody currently has. The same test applies one level earlier, when a fellowship director decides which of a dozen plausible projects a trainee should spend the two or three protected years of a career-development award chasing. The question to put to a fellow is not which question is biggest. It's which question the fellow, specifically, right now, with the data and collaborators actually available, has a real move against. Hamming kept a working list of ten to twenty such problems for his own field at any given time, revised as attacks appeared or failed, so a list already existed the moment a genuine opening showed up rather than being invented under deadline pressure.

Run down a list of your own open problems and ask honestly of each one whether you actually have a move or are simply carrying it around out of loyalty, and the talk holds up as a diagnostic that has outlived the laboratory it was written in. Treat it instead as license, as permission to read every institutional safeguard as the moral equivalent of the man filing his bicycle-storage paperwork, and it produces the same ego-driven, system-fighting researcher Hamming spent an entire section of his own talk warning against. The lecture supplies both readings and does not, on its own evidence, tell you which one you are giving in to on any particular day.

Hamming's shelf touches the one this feed left last week at exactly this joint. Patrick Collison's own question, still open, was why an institution as large and consequential as the National Institutes of Health has almost no serious literature examining it as an institution making choices, only literature examining the science it funds. Hamming's talk supplies one small piece of the missing account: a description, from inside a very different kind of institution, of what it actually felt like to distinguish a real attack from a comfortable one, written by someone with thirty years and a body of durable work behind the claim. It is not a study of NIH. It is the closest thing available to a first-person account of the exact judgment a study section is trying, mostly without a shared vocabulary, to make on paper.

Go find the list you already keep, the ten or twenty problems in your own field that you privately believe matter and privately suspect you'll never quite get to. Pick the one nearest the top and ask, honestly, whether you have an attack on it or only the reasons everyone already agrees it should have one. If you don't, that's not a failure; it's information about where to spend the next year of reading rather than the next grant cycle of writing.
