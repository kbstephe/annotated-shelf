## Chapter One: A Memo to a Network That Did Not Exist

In April of nineteen sixty-three, a psychologist named J. C. R. Licklider sat down at the Pentagon and typed a memo apologizing for a scheduling conflict. He addressed it, in the header, to "Members and Affiliates of the Intergalactic Computer Network." At the moment he wrote that phrase, the network in question consisted of four separately funded research groups, each with its own machine, none of which could exchange so much as a single character with any of the others. He proposed that the several projects ought to behave as though they shared one blackboard: pooling software, comparing notes, treating a problem solved at one site as solved everywhere. He knew this required equipment nobody had built and a kind of trust nobody had tested. He wrote the memo anyway, addressed it to a name three sizes too large for the actual machines involved, and sent it out under the letterhead of an ordinary federal budget line.

Buried a few sentences into that same memo, without any apparent sense that it undercut the joke of the title, sits a plainer sentence. The whole effort exists, Licklider wrote, "to bring into being the technology that the military needs." That line is easy to skip past on a first read, because everything around it sounds like a man having fun with government stationery. It is also the fact this hour keeps circling back to. The money paying for computer science departments that did not yet exist, for graduate students who would spend federal contracts on projects with no obvious weapon at the end of them, for an office whose own director would rather talk about symbiosis than about strategy, still came from the same defense budget line that funded interceptor missiles and nuclear early-warning radar. Whether loosening that leash bought something different in kind from what the leash was built for, or simply bought a friendlier voice reading out the same order, is the question the rest of Licklider's own career, and the network his successors eventually built from his memo, has to answer.

## Chapter Two: What Eighty-Five Percent of Thinking Actually Is

Licklider was born in nineteen fifteen in St. Louis, the only child of a Baptist minister, and studied at Washington University there before a doctorate in psychoacoustics at the University of Rochester, finished in nineteen forty-two, on how the mammalian brain converts vibrating air into the sensation of pitch. He joined MIT in nineteen fifty as an associate professor and founded its Acoustics Laboratory, work that had nothing obviously to do with computers at all. What pulled him toward them was a defense project called SAGE, the continental air-defense system built to track incoming Soviet bombers on radar and route the results to human operators fast enough that a decision could still matter. Licklider ran the human-factors side of that effort, and one small design choice inside it stayed with him for the rest of his working life: SAGE's consoles used cathode-ray screens and a light pen, letting an operator point directly at a blip on the glass rather than type its coordinates into a teletype first. A person and a machine were reading the same picture together, each contributing what the other could not, and Licklider began to suspect that the arrangement was not a wartime workaround but a preview of how anyone might eventually want to work with a computer at all.

In nineteen fifty-seven he left MIT for the acoustic consulting firm Bolt Beranek and Newman, chasing computers more directly, and by nineteen sixty had turned that suspicion into a paper, "Man-Computer Symbiosis," built around a small experiment he had run on himself the year before. Licklider tracked his own working hours across several months and found that roughly eighty-five percent of what he had been calling his thinking time was actually spent "getting into a position to think": searching for a document, plotting a graph by hand, converting one experimenter's units into another's so that six incompatible studies could sit on the same axis. Once the numbers were finally lined up, the pattern they revealed took seconds to see. Getting them lined up had eaten an afternoon. Elsewhere in the same paper Licklider described a military situation with the identical shape at a far higher cost: a battle-planning problem where feeding the numbers into a machine, waiting for the program to run, and reading the printed answer back out took long enough, by his own count, that the battle itself would be over before the second step of planning it had even begun. Speed was not what either situation actually lacked. What both lacked was a person and a machine working the same problem at the same time, rather than a person handing a machine a question and waiting, idle, for it to hand back an answer. Worse, Licklider admitted, his own choices about which problems to even attempt were shaped less by what struck him as intellectually promising than by which questions happened to be cheap to calculate by hand, an admission that a working scientist's curiosity was being rationed, without his noticing it happen, by clerical capacity rather than by anything resembling judgment.

The fix he proposed borrowed its name from biology rather than engineering. A fig tree, he opened by observing, is pollinated by exactly one species of wasp and by no other living thing on earth; neither the tree nor the insect can complete its own life cycle alone, and the arrangement between them is not cooperation in any sentimental sense but two organisms locked into each other's survival. Licklider wanted the same tight coupling between a human mind and a machine: not a tool a person picks up and puts down, but a working partnership in which the computer did the plotting, the searching, and the unit conversion in the background while the person supplied the judgment about which question the whole exercise was for. He drew a careful line, inside the same paper, between two things people kept confusing: building a machine that mechanically extended what a person could already do unaided, a faster adding machine standing in for a slower pencil, and building a partnership that let a person and a machine attack a problem neither one could have solved alone in the time available. Automation was the first kind of extension. Symbiosis, in his own usage, meant only the second. In nineteen sixty that was closer to a hope than a working machine. Two years later, the federal government handed him a budget line and a title that let him try to build it.

## Chapter Three: The Office That Changed Its Own Name

The office in question had been created inside the Advanced Research Projects Agency, the outfit President Eisenhower had stood up in nineteen fifty-eight after Sputnik convinced Washington that American science had fallen dangerously behind, and its founding charter called it, without any ambiguity, Command and Control Research: a direct descendant of the same SAGE lineage Licklider had already spent years inside, tasked with extending computerized air defense into other military decision problems. ARPA's director, Jack Ruina, hired Licklider to run it in October of nineteen sixty-two. Licklider's first act as director was to rename his own office the Information Processing Techniques Office, a change that looked, on an organizational chart, like nothing more than tidier language for the same job.

It was not tidier language. It was a different job. Licklider's own later explanation for the switch was almost dismissive in how plainly he stated it. "It really wasn't a command and control research program," he said. "It was an interactive computing program." The distinction mattered because computers in nineteen sixty-two were nowhere near capable enough to run any actual battlefield decision yet, regardless of what the founding charter had promised the Pentagon. The fastest route to a machine any general could trust with a real decision, Licklider argued, ran straight through funding the most basic research into how a person and a computer could work together at all, wherever the most promising people doing that work already happened to be sitting. The tanks and radar screens could wait for the foundation to exist. What the office actually needed to buy first was the foundation.

That reasoning gave Licklider a mechanism unlike either of the two this shelf has already tested against Bush and Braben's opposite bets. He was not, like Bush organizing wartime research, working through university departments whose own peer structure vetted a scientist's competence before a dollar moved. He was not, like Braben two decades later, spending an oil company's entirely discretionary money with no charter to satisfy at all. Licklider held a specific line item inside a permanent, uniformed, appropriations-bound federal bureaucracy, one that existed to serve a defense mission whether or not its own director found that mission the most interesting part of the job, and he used the discretion built into that line item the same way Braben would later use a long lunch: to find a particular person doing work that mattered and simply pay for it, on terms close enough to no terms at all that a university physicist could barely tell the money had a uniform attached to it.

Robert Taylor, who would later inherit the office himself, described the man he inherited it from as the most unlikely great man anyone could hope to encounter, gentle, curious, and outgoing, someone whose favorite kind of joke was one told at his own expense rather than anyone else's. That temperament was not incidental to the method. A face-to-face funding decision runs entirely on whether the person across the table believes the evaluator is actually listening, and a Pentagon office staffed by a man that easygoing about his own authority made an unusually persuasive place to be heard, especially for researchers whose ideas were furthest from anything a written proposal could make sound credible on paper.

## Chapter Four: Departments That Did Not Have a Name Yet

What that discretion actually bought, in its first eighteen months, was a strange list for a defense agency to be funding. At MIT, Fernando Corbató had already built an informal system called the Compatible Time-Sharing System, letting several people use one large computer at once instead of queuing behind a stack of punch cards; Licklider turned that experiment into a two-million-dollar contract in July of nineteen sixty-three, Project MAC, with Robert Fano running it and Corbató's own group absorbing roughly a third of the budget to build a more ambitious successor system called Multics. At Berkeley, a similar time-sharing effort called Project Genie got its own line of support.

At Stanford, John McCarthy got funding to build an artificial-intelligence laboratory around a research question with no product anywhere in sight. McCarthy had coined the phrase "artificial intelligence" six years earlier, in nineteen fifty-six, organizing a summer workshop at Dartmouth College that drew a handful of researchers to spend two months arguing about whether a machine could ever be made to reason, a meeting that produced no working system and no funding of its own at the time, only a name for a field that did not exist yet. By the time McCarthy moved to Stanford in nineteen sixty-two, Licklider's money let him found a laboratory around that same unproven question years before any university would have hired a faculty line for it on the strength of results alone, because there were no results yet to hire on the strength of.

Corbató already had a working system before Licklider's money arrived; McCarthy had a research question no existing department was organized to house. What the funding actually purchased, in each case, was permission for that work to keep happening at a scale no single university budget would have risked on its own, aimed at problems too basic, too far from any deliverable, and too far outside any accreditation an existing academic department could vouch for. Computer science as a field with its own doctoral programs at MIT, Stanford, Berkeley, and Carnegie Mellon largely dates its own founding to money that arrived under Licklider's signature before any of those universities had a department to put the check into.

The single strangest bet on that early list belonged to Douglas Engelbart, a researcher at the Stanford Research Institute who traced his own project back to a single afternoon in nineteen fifty, driving to a new job newly engaged to be married, when it occurred to him that the world's problems were growing too complicated for any one unaided mind to keep up with them, and that whatever he did with his career ought to help fix that gap rather than add one more narrow contribution to it. Twelve years and one obscure engineering report later, in October of nineteen sixty-two, Engelbart published "Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework," arguing that a person's own capacity to reason could be extended by machines the same way a lever extends muscle, an idea he illustrated with sketches of a pointing device and a way of linking one document's ideas directly to another's that existing funders found difficult to justify against any near-term military use at all. Licklider read the report, recognized in it almost the same argument he had made two years earlier from the opposite direction, human limitation as the thing an engineer should design around rather than treating the machine's limitation as the only one that counted, and added ARPA money on top of what Engelbart already had from the Air Force and NASA, giving his Augmentation Research Center years of runway to chase an idea about extending human intellect that would not produce its own famous public demonstration, mouse and all, until six years after Licklider had left the office that funded it.

## Chapter Five: A Blackboard for Machines That Could Not Yet Talk

Return, now, to that April memo and its imaginary intergalactic network, because its actual argument is more concrete than its title lets on. Each of the four research groups Licklider had funded, he wrote, needed a software base and a hardware facility more elaborate than any one of them could build alone in a reasonable amount of time, so rather than let each site reinvent the same basic tools in isolation, he proposed they behave as though they shared one blackboard: comparing notes constantly, treating a technique proven at one site as available everywhere, a kind of resource-sharing across MIT, Berkeley, Stanford, and a handful of other funded labs that no wire on earth yet made technically possible.

What Licklider could offer in nineteen sixty-three was the destination and the reason to want it. What he could not offer was the actual switching technology that would make it work, because packet switching, the method of breaking a message into small independently routed pieces rather than reserving one continuous circuit the way a telephone call does, arrived from two engineers who had never worked for him at all: Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation, thinking through how a communications network might survive a nuclear strike on any single node, and Donald Davies in Britain, arriving at close to the identical idea for entirely civilian reasons. Licklider's own memo names a need and a rough shape for the answer years before anyone knew how to build it, which is a different kind of contribution from building it, and the distance between the two is where this hour's real argument about him has to live.

The same habit of naming a destination he could not yet reach shows up again in a book he published two years later, "Libraries of the Future," which imagined a scholar someday searching a library's entire holdings from a terminal rather than walking its stacks, decades before any database existed with the storage or the indexing to make that search practical. Read next to the memo, the book makes a pattern out of what could otherwise look like a single lucky guess: Licklider's actual talent was not predicting which specific piece of engineering would solve a problem, since he was wrong or simply silent about that part almost every time, but describing a problem's shape clearly enough that someone else, years later, could recognize the actual solution the moment it arrived.

## Chapter Six: Handing Off a Bet He Would Never See Land

Licklider left ARPA in nineteen sixty-four for a three-year stint at IBM, before either the packet-switching method or a single working link existed to test his memo's proposal against. Before he left, he did something Braben's own account of Venture Research never managed: he picked, by name, the person who would run his office next. Ivan Sutherland took over IPTO in nineteen sixty-four at twenty-six years old, fresh from an MIT doctoral thesis called "Sketchpad," a program that let a person draw directly on a computer screen with a light pen and have the machine hold a drawn shape's own geometric relationships, a rectangle's corners staying square as you dragged one side, years before any commercial drafting or design software could do the same. Sutherland brought a budget that had grown to roughly fifteen million dollars a year and kept funding the same kind of open-ended, person-first bets Licklider had started.

Sutherland in turn handed the office to Robert Taylor, who arrived at his own version of the network idea from a more irritable direction than Licklider's memo had. Taylor's Pentagon office held three separate computer terminals, one wired to a System Development Corporation machine in Santa Monica, one to Project Genie at Berkeley, one to the Multics system at MIT, each running its own login procedure and its own command language, so that reaching a colleague on one system while working on another meant standing up, walking across the room, and starting over from a blank screen. Taylor's own conclusion, stated afterward about as plainly as a founding insight gets stated, was that there ought to be one terminal that could reach all three, and that the fastest way to get one was to connect the machines themselves rather than keep buying more terminals. That irritation is the same problem Licklider's nineteen sixty-three memo had already named from a gentler distance, arrived at again by a different man for a more immediate reason.

Not every site wanted to be connected. Wes Clark, an engineer who preferred building a computer for one person's exclusive use over sharing a large machine among many, regarded both time-sharing and networking as a step in the wrong direction, and kept his own university's computers off the network for years afterward on exactly that conviction, an act of quiet institutional resistance made almost too fitting to invent, since the campus in question was Washington University in St. Louis, the same undergraduate institution Licklider himself had attended a generation earlier. A network only becomes useful once enough of it exists for the benefit to outweigh the cost of joining, and until it reached that size, a lab with a full machine and a long queue of its own graduate students waiting for time on it had a real reason to prefer keeping its cycles to itself rather than sharing them with strangers running unfamiliar jobs from three states away.

Taylor got the budget to build it in nineteen sixty-six. Three years later, on the twenty-ninth of October, at UCLA, a graduate student named Charley Kline sat down to type the network's first real message, a login command meant to open a session on a machine at the Stanford Research Institute, the same institute housing Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center, an appropriate site for the network's earliest working link given how directly Engelbart's own funding traced back to Licklider's reading habits years earlier. The letter L reached the far end cleanly. The letter O followed it without trouble. The letter G never made it: the receiving system crashed a moment before it could be displayed, so that the first word ever carried across the network Licklider's memo had named six years before consisted, by accident, of exactly two letters, "LO," short for a login that would not actually complete until an hour later, once the system had been restarted. It read, in hindsight, like the network apologizing for its own late arrival before it had managed to say anything else at all. By nineteen seventy-one the four original nodes had grown to fifteen, twenty-three separate host machines strung across universities, defense contractors, and NASA, proof that Taylor's irritable solution to three mismatched terminals in one Pentagon office scaled into something considerably larger than the problem that had produced it.

Larry Roberts, the engineer who did the actual technical design work translating Baran's and Davies's packet-switching ideas into a working system, put the credit question about as generously as it can be put: "The vision was really Lick's originally." Licklider was, by that point, back at MIT as a professor, running Project MAC himself from nineteen sixty-eight to nineteen seventy, five years removed from the office whose earlier version he had renamed, watching from a research building rather than a Pentagon office as three successors he had helped select finished building the thing his own memo had only been able to name.

## Chapter Seven: What a Renamed Office Actually Bought

Reading that hand-off as pure vindication skips past three separate objections, each sharper than it first looks. Paul Edwards, the historian whose book "The Closed World" remains the standard account of computing's Cold War entanglements, reads "Man-Computer Symbiosis" itself as a product of what he calls closed-world discourse: a set of habits inherited from a run of postwar gatherings known as the Macy Conferences, where mathematicians, psychologists, and engineers spent the late nineteen forties arguing that minds and machines could be described in the same information-processing vocabulary, the founding move of the field Norbert Wiener would soon name cybernetics. Edwards traces a direct line from that vocabulary to Licklider's own psychoacoustics training and on into a habit of mind that turned political and military problems into engineering ones by casting the computer as a partner in thought rather than a mere calculator. That framing, Edwards argues, made an inherently military funding relationship easier to accept as a purely intellectual one than it would have looked stated plainly. Edwards is not accusing Licklider of insincerity. He is pointing out that the open door Licklider built for researchers was cut into a wall that a different set of interests had put up for a different reason, and that Licklider's own memo, with its casual admission about bringing into being the technology the military needs, concedes the point without ever meaning to argue it.

A second objection runs closer to the one this shelf already leveled at Braben two episodes ago. Steven Hill's demographic count against Venture Research found a program that, whatever its founder's stated openness, kept recognizing the same narrow kind of candidate a single evaluator already knew how to see. Licklider's own funded list runs through MIT, his own former employer; Stanford, home to colleagues he already knew from psychoacoustics and engineering circles; Berkeley and Carnegie Mellon, both already near the top of any list of serious American science faculties before his money arrived. A program built on one official's face-to-face trust, whether housed inside an oil company's discretionary account or a federal appropriations line, still tends to keep funding whoever that official already has the professional instincts to recognize as promising, and Licklider's own instincts ran almost entirely through a handful of institutions he had already spent a career inside.

The third objection is the plainest caution against telling this story as one man's foresight paying off on schedule. Janet Abbate's history of the internet's actual construction treats its emergence as a contested, multi-actor negotiation rather than a single visionary's prophecy fulfilled by grateful successors, and the timeline backs her reading more than Roberts's generous credit line lets on. Licklider left in nineteen sixty-four, five years before a single letter moved between two machines. The switching method that made the network technically possible came from Baran and Davies, neither of whom Licklider ever hired or funded. Taylor still had to fight his own institutional battle inside ARPA to get a network built at all, over real internal resistance from people who thought each lab's separate terminal was working fine. Crediting one psychologist's nineteen sixty-three memo for a network assembled five years later by people solving problems he never touched flatters a tidy story more than it describes what actually happened.

## Chapter Eight: Three Ways to Make Trust Outlive the Truster

None of those three objections empties the achievement out. What they narrow is the claim, from a single man's foresight predicting the future correctly, to something smaller and in some ways more interesting: a specific institutional trick for making a funder's own judgment survive that funder's departure, tested here against the two other mechanisms this shelf has already examined. Vannevar Bush embedded his trust in a university department's own peer structure, a mechanism durable enough to outlast his own tenure but built for, and dependent on, a wartime clock that made rapid, high-stakes bets briefly acceptable in a way peacetime funding rarely tolerates. Braben trusted one irreplaceable man's unaided read of a stranger across a table, a mechanism that produced real results for a decade and then evaporated the day the one executive shielding it from BP's ordinary scrutiny walked out the door, because nothing besides that one relationship had ever been built to survive without him.

Licklider's version, sitting inside the least promising home either Bush or Braben would have predicted for a genuine outlier, a permanent peacetime defense bureaucracy answerable to appropriations committees, worked past his own departure for a specific and checkable reason neither of the other two men managed: he did not simply pick good projects and hope his replacement kept picking them the same way. He picked, by name, the two people who would run the office next, choosing successors whose own sense of which strange proposal deserved money resembled his closely enough that the bets kept getting made for years after he was no longer in the room to make them himself. Patrick Collison's own question about organizations with no natural way to die, the one that structured the very first episode on this shelf, gets its plainest answer yet from this office's own history: the Command and Control Research charter is decades dead, but the actual habit it briefly housed, funding a specific person for years at a stretch and staying out of their way, survived its own founding director by being handed, deliberately, to people who would keep doing it. The agency Licklider once worked inside still runs a version of the same trick today, on purpose rather than by accident: every program manager DARPA hires now arrives with a fixed three-to-five-year term built into the job from the first day, an expiration date stamped on the position itself rather than left to depend on whichever individual happens to be occupying it, so that the office's habit of betting on strange people outlives any one manager's own tenure by policy instead of by luck.

## Chapter Nine: The Harder Bet a Program Officer Makes

That distinction matters for anyone spending someone else's money on another person's unproven judgment, and it asks a different question than the one this shelf already put to a fellowship director two episodes back. Braben's record raised the question of how much unstructured runway to hand a single promising trainee on the strength of one good conversation. Licklider's own record raises a harder and less comfortable one: not how much rope to give this year's most promising person, but who, well before any formal committee gets asked to vote on it, gets trusted to keep making that same unglamorous, high-trust call once the current director's own term runs out. A program that only works through one person's private judgment carries an expiration date stamped on it the moment that person is hired, whether the person is an oil executive with a discretionary account or a psychologist with a federal line item, and Braben's own unit is the proof of what happens when nobody thinks to hand the judgment itself to a successor before the walls come down. Licklider thought to hand it twice, to two different people, years before he needed to, and the network his own memo could only name got built because both of them kept making his bet long after he had stopped being the one making it.

Choosing that kind of successor is a harder bet than choosing a fellow, because a fellow's failure costs a career two years and a wrong turn, while a successor's failure costs the entire program the very habit that justified funding it in the first place, one safe committee-friendly choice at a time, with no single decision ever looking like the mistake that ended it. Braben never had to make that choice at all, since Venture Research answered to nobody but him for its whole ten years, and the choice was made for him the day Bob Malpas left the building. Licklider made it on purpose, twice, while he still had the standing to make it well, and the two accidental letters a graduate student managed to transmit between two machines in nineteen sixty-nine are what that foresight actually bought, five years and two changes of leadership after he had walked out of the office that started it.

He returned to the office he had renamed only once more, briefly, in nineteen seventy-four, an older man steering something that had long since outgrown the four isolated machines his nineteen sixty-three memo had first addressed. He died in nineteen ninety of complications after an asthma attack, in Arlington, Massachusetts, a death that made little noise outside a small circle of people who already understood what his signature on a two-million-dollar contract, or a joke about an intergalactic network, had actually bought them. Most of the people whose working lives his choices reshaped never learned his name at all. Next time, the vision leaves Licklider's own hands entirely, for a research center outside Palo Alto where Robert Taylor spends the money differently, and a machine called the Alto turns interactive computing from a federal research bet into something a single person might actually own, in the second part of Waldrop's "The Dream Machine." Thanks for listening.
