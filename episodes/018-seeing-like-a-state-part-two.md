## Chapter One: The Farm as a Factory

Part one ended on a question: what happens when the aesthetic that built Brasília is pointed not at where people live but at how they eat. Scott's first answer is the Soviet countryside between nineteen twenty-nine and nineteen thirty-three, and he opens it somewhere the listener will not expect, in the American West.

In the nineteen twenties the most admired farm in the world was in Montana. Thomas Campbell ran ninety-five thousand acres of wheat on land leased from the federal government, with tractors, combines, and a labour force organised in shifts, and he described what he was doing as industrial production that happened to take place outdoors. The Soviet leadership, which had never run a farm and distrusted everyone who had, found this irresistible. Lenin had already written admiringly of Taylorism, the American doctrine that any job could be broken into timed motions and reassembled by an engineer, and had proposed that the Soviet state adopt it wholesale; the German war economy of nineteen seventeen, in which the state had directed industry as a single machine, was his other model. The idea that agriculture could be the same, a factory with fields for a floor, ran straight through the Bolshevik imagination, and Campbell was invited to the Soviet Union in nineteen twenty-nine to advise on the largest farm ever attempted, a state grain enterprise near Rostov, hundreds of thousands of acres, that was named, without irony, Gigant.

Scott's point in beginning there is that collectivisation was not, at its root, a Marxist idea. It was a high-modernist one, and it was shared. The American agronomists who visited the Soviet Union in those years were often as enchanted by the scale of the state farms as the Soviets were by the Montana wheat; the difference was that the Americans had no power to impose the vision on unwilling farmers, and the Soviets did. The four conditions from part one were all present at once for the first time in the book: the administrative ordering of nature and society, the ideology that made it a mission, a state willing to use unlimited coercion, and a peasantry that had already been broken once, in the civil war, and had no organised means of refusing.

What was imposed had two logics, and Scott is careful to keep them apart. The first was the factory logic: enormous farms, uniform crops, mechanisation, a work force treated as labour rather than as farmers, a plan issued from Moscow specifying what was to be sown where. The second was older and simpler and, in his reading, the one that actually drove the policy: legibility for appropriation. The Soviet state needed grain, to feed the cities and to sell abroad for the machinery its industrialisation required, and it could not extract it from twenty-five million scattered peasant households whose harvests it could not see and whose surpluses they hid. It could get grain from a few hundred thousand collective farms with a manager, a ledger, and a procurement quota. The kolkhoz, the collective farm, was, before it was anything else, a device for making the harvest visible and collectable. Scott's word for what the peasants received in exchange is precise: it was a second serfdom, with the state as the landlord, an internal passport system to keep the farmers on the land, and a quota that came first, before the farm's own food.

The outcome is known and the shelf should say it plainly, because Scott does. Between nineteen thirty-two and nineteen thirty-three, in Ukraine, the Volga, Kazakhstan, and the North Caucasus, somewhere between five and nine million people starved to death, while grain continued to be procured and, in some years, exported. Livestock numbers fell by half or more as peasants slaughtered animals rather than surrender them. The gigantic farms, Gigant included, proved unmanageable and were broken up within a few years. What survived, for sixty years, was the collective farm as a procurement machine, and what kept it fed, Scott notes, was the thing the plan had left out: the private plots, the small gardens each household was eventually permitted to keep, which occupied a few percent of the land and produced, by the later decades, a large fraction of the country's vegetables, fruit, eggs, and meat. The illegible remainder fed the legible system.

## Chapter Two: The Village Drawn on a Map

The Tanzanian case is the one Scott thinks is most instructive, because the people who ran it were not monsters. Julius Nyerere was a schoolteacher, a Catholic, a democrat by the standards of post-colonial Africa, and by any account a man who wanted his country's peasants to be better off. Ujamaa, the policy he announced in the Arusha Declaration of nineteen sixty-seven, means something like familyhood in Swahili, and its founding idea was that Tanzania's scattered rural population should live together in villages where the state could bring them schools, clinics, clean water, and agricultural advice, and where they could farm cooperatively. Nobody who reads the declaration doubts the sincerity of it.

For six years the villages were voluntary, and not many people moved. So in nineteen seventy-three the government made it compulsory, and between then and nineteen seventy-six, in what Scott describes as by most accounts the largest forced resettlement in independent Africa to that date, at least five million Tanzanians were moved, often by lorry, sometimes with their old houses burned behind them so that they could not return, into villages laid out by officials. The layouts are the detail that connects this chapter to Brasília. The new villages were drawn on a grid, houses in rows along a road, identical plots, the fields in blocks beyond, so that from the road, and from the district office, and from the capital, a village looked like a village should look: orderly, countable, visitable. Scott's title for the chapter is Compulsory Villagisation in Tanzania: Aesthetics and Miniaturisation, and the second word carries the chapter. Unable to make the whole countryside legible at once, the state built miniatures of legibility, model villages that could be shown to visiting ministers, and then required the country to live in them.

What the grid could not see was how Tanzanian farmers had been feeding themselves. Across much of the country the practice was polycropping: several crops planted together in the same field, at different heights, maturing at different times, some for food, some for cash, some to fix nitrogen, some as insurance against the failure of the others. To an agronomist trained on temperate monocultures this looked like a mess, and the state's agricultural officers, most of whom had that training, said so. The villages were laid out for single crops in straight rows, which the extension services could inspect, and the farmers were moved away from the land they knew, its soils and its water and its risks, to land the planners had selected for its position on the road. The result was what a listener who has heard part one will predict: yields fell, food had to be imported, the villages' cooperative fields were neglected while the farmers walked back, sometimes for hours, to their old plots, and by the end of the decade the state had stopped enforcing most of what it had built. Nobody starved in millions. What was lost was slower and, for the book's argument, more exact: a body of practice that had taken generations to build, dismantled in three years by people who did not know it was there, because the only thing they had been trained to see was the row.

And Scott adds the observation about Tanzania that stops it being a story about Africa. Almost every element of ujamaa had been tried before, by the British colonial administration in the nineteen forties and fifties, with the same aesthetic and the same failures, and the independent government had inherited the files. The colonial officers had wanted legible villages for tax and control; the socialist government wanted them for services and cooperation; the grid was the same grid. The politics changed and the way of seeing did not, and that is the book's argument in one country: high modernism is not a political programme but a set of eyes, and it can be worn by a colonial governor and the man who replaced him.

## Chapter Three: The Word for What Was Lost

Now the chapter the whole book has been walking toward, and the word the shelf will keep. Scott needs a name for the thing the forestry tables, the Soviet plan, and the Tanzanian grid all failed to see, and he finds it in Greek. Mētis is the quality Odysseus has: cunning, practical, adaptive intelligence, the knowledge of how to respond to a situation that is never the same twice. The Greeks set it against technē, the kind of knowledge that can be written as rules and taught in a classroom, and Scott's claim is that most of what keeps any complex human activity working is the first kind and that the state, by its nature, can only see the second.

The examples are the best in the book, and two of them belong in the ear. The first is the harbour pilot. A ship's captain knows how to sail the open sea, and that knowledge is largely technē: charts, instruments, rules of navigation, the same everywhere. When the ship reaches a harbour, the captain hands the wheel to a pilot who knows this harbour, its shoals and currents and the way the wind comes off that particular headland at that particular hour, and the pilot's knowledge is not in any book, because it cannot be. It is the accumulated experience of a lifetime spent in one place, revised daily, and it is worth more, in that harbour, than the captain's whole education. The second example is a farmer's rule, and Scott uses it because it looks like nonsense. An old piece of New England planting advice, attributed to the Native American practice the settlers learned from, says to plant corn when the oak leaves are the size of a squirrel's ear. An agronomist would replace that with a soil temperature and a calendar date. But the calendar date is wrong in a late spring and the soil thermometer requires a thermometer, and the oak leaf integrates, without instruments, everything about the season that matters to a seed, in the one place where the seed is going to be planted. The rule is not primitive. It is a compression of local experience into a form a person can carry, and it is more accurate than the formula that would replace it, in the cases where accuracy matters most.

That is the argument, and it needs to be stated carefully because it is easy to state badly. Scott is not saying that local knowledge is always right or that science is always wrong; he says so several times, and his critics would say he does not say it loudly enough. He is saying that mētis is a different kind of knowledge, that it lives in practice rather than in documents, that it is destroyed when the practice is disrupted, and that a state which can only read documents will not detect the destruction until the second rotation. The forest's ants, the peasant's private plot, the Tanzanian field with five crops in it, the harbour pilot: none of them can be put in a report, so nobody can defend them in the meeting where the report is discussed.

Here the shelf's own instruments engage, and the join is with the last two books. Graeber's interpretive labour was the work the powerless do to make themselves legible to the powerful. Mētis is what that work costs when it succeeds: the villager who learns to describe his fields in the planner's categories has stopped describing the fields, and the planner now has a document that says the wrong thing in the right format. And Bullshit Jobs, two episodes ago, found a workforce paid to produce documents nobody reads. Scott's book suggests why that workforce exists. An institution that can only see documents will pay to have the world converted into documents, whether or not the conversion loses the thing that mattered, because to the institution the document is the thing. The box ticker is a legibility machine. What he ticks is the row the state can see.

## Chapter Four: What Scott Actually Recommends

The book's last chapter is short and it is the one the book's admirers on the right tend to skip, because it does not say what they want it to. Scott does not recommend abolishing planning, or the state, or expertise. He recommends four rules for anyone who has to intervene in a complex system they do not fully understand, and they are worth stating exactly, because they are the closest the shelf has yet come to an antidote that survives its own diagnosis.

Take small steps. In a system you cannot see whole, the intervention should be small enough that its effects can be observed before the next one is made. Favour reversibility. Prefer the change that can be undone if it proves wrong; a grid can be drawn on a map in an afternoon and cannot be un-drawn from a countryside in a generation. Plan on surprises. Assume the system will respond in ways the plan did not anticipate, and build the plan so that the surprises can be accommodated rather than suppressed. And plan on human inventiveness: assume the people inside will adapt whatever is imposed on them, and design the imposition so that their adaptations improve it rather than sabotage it, which means leaving them room to adapt at all.

A clinician will recognise those rules, because they are the rules of prescribing. Start low, go slow; prefer the drug you can stop; expect the side effect you did not predict; and assume the patient will do something unintended with the prescription, so write it in a form that survives that. Scott's rules are the same instinct scaled up to a state, and the reason they are radical is that they run against the grain of everything a planning institution is built to do. A ministry is rewarded for a plan that is large, complete, and irreversible, because that is what a plan looks like when it is presented to a minister, and a plan with room in it for the villagers to change it is, from the ministry's chair, an unfinished plan.

And the four rules are also, and this is the observation that ties the series together, the reverse of the four conditions. Against the full administrative ordering of society, small steps. Against an ideology that makes the plan a mission, plan on being wrong. Against an authority willing to use unlimited force, prefer what can be undone. And against a prostrate civil society, count on the people to invent, which is only possible if they have been left standing. Scott's antidote is not a different plan. It is the withdrawal, one at a time, of the conditions under which any plan becomes a catastrophe.

## Chapter Five: The Case Against, With the Historians Attached

The critics should be heard at full strength, and this book has better critics than most, because it made large claims in fields where people had spent their lives.

The first count comes from the Soviet historians, and it is the one that should worry an admirer most. Mark Tauger, who has spent a career on the harvests of the nineteen thirties, reviewed the book for H-Net in nineteen ninety-nine under a title that gives his objection away, The Moral Agronomy of the Peasant versus the Moral Economy of the Town. Tauger's charge is that Scott's account of collectivisation leaves out the harvests. The famine of nineteen thirty-two and thirty-three, in his reconstruction, was set off by a genuine crop failure, drought and disease and weather, on top of which the state's procurement policy and its export commitments in the middle of a world depression turned shortage into mass death. Legibility and high modernism are part of the story; they are not, he argues, the mechanism, and a book that makes them the mechanism is telling a morality tale in which the state is always the actor and the weather never is. He adds that the collective farms, for all their horrors, had by the end of the decade restored grain output to something like pre-revolutionary levels through mechanisation and inputs, which is not a fact Scott's argument has a place for. The same omission, Tauger says, recurs in Tanzania: the food shortages of the nineteen seventies had causes, drought and the oil shock among them, that were not villagisation, and Scott does not weigh them.

That is a real objection, and it is the historian's version of the one this shelf keeps raising: what is the denominator, and what would have happened anyway. Scott's method is the case study, and a case study can show that a mechanism operated. It cannot, on its own, show how much of the outcome the mechanism explains, and the book does not try. The listener should hold the collectivisation chapter as a demonstration that the state's way of seeing made the famine possible and made it worse, and should not hold it as the finding that it caused the famine, because that claim needs a counterfactual Scott does not supply.

The second count is about the deck. Scott Alexander, in a long and admiring review that has done more than any academic review to carry the book to a new generation, ends by objecting that the book stacks the dice against high modernism. Every case is a failure. The Green Revolution, which applied the very kind of top-down agronomic science Scott distrusts and roughly doubled cereal yields across Asia, is not in the book. Nor are sewers, vaccination, the abolition of the medieval city's actual filth; the standard measures that disarmed the landlord are conceded and then set aside. And Alexander puts his finger on the problem the mētis chapter never resolves: how do you tell evolved local wisdom from people resisting a beneficial change for ordinary reasons of habit and interest? The oak leaf is charming. So, presumably, were a great many local practices about disease that killed people for centuries until a legible, uniform, state-imposed intervention stopped them. Scott's framework can name the second kind of case; the book does not contain one.

The third count is the one the first episode of this pair promised, and it comes from an economist. Brad DeLong, reviewing the book, made the observation that its argument about local knowledge and the limits of central planning is Friedrich Hayek's argument, made in the nineteen forties, and that Scott, who mentions Hayek barely at all, seems unable to make contact with his own intellectual roots. If mētis is dispersed knowledge that no central authority can collect, DeLong says, then the institution that already exists to aggregate dispersed knowledge without anyone collecting it is the price system, and a book that arrives at Hayek's diagnosis and then declines Hayek's prescription owes the reader a reason. Scott's reason, given at the end of the book and at length in a Cato Unbound exchange in twenty ten, is that large market actors see like states too: the supermarket chain that requires a tomato bred to survive shipping rather than to taste of anything has done to the tomato what the forester did to the forest, and it did it with no ministry involved. The political theorist Henry Farrell took DeLong up on that point, arguing that market standardisation and state standardisation advance together and that DeLong's revealed-preference reply, that people bought the rubber tomato because it was cheap, misses how the institutions that sell it shape what people are able to prefer. The dispute is not settled, and this shelf's own finding leans toward Scott: the Davies series found the corporation running on procedures no one controls, seeing its customers as rows in a system, in ways the Unaccountability Machine episodes catalogued with no state anywhere in the story. But DeLong's count stands as a demand the book never met. If you believe in mētis, you owe an account of which institutions preserve it, and Scott's book is far better at naming the ones that destroy it.

And a fourth, briefer count, from the political scientist David Laitin among others: the evidence is selective. Scott chose the cases that show the mechanism, and told them beautifully, and a reader who wants to know how often the mechanism operates, out of how many state projects, has been given a museum rather than a survey. This is the same objection as Tauger's in a different accent, and the shelf has met it twice this season, in Graeber's testimonies and in Freeman's single group. The case series proves the condition exists. It does not tell you the rate.

## Chapter Six: The Lean

Set the critics beside the shelf's most recent instrument and something uncomfortable appears. Three episodes ago Orwell audited Burnham by lining up his forecasts and reading the direction of the misses, and found that a mind enchanted by power reads the winners as the future. Run the same audit on Scott. Every case in the book resolves the same way: the state plans, the plan fails, the local knowledge was right. The misses, if they are misses, all lean toward the village. And Scott's later career confirms the direction: his next books were on the peoples of upland Southeast Asia who had spent two thousand years evading states altogether, and then a history of the earliest states as a catastrophe for the people who fell under them, and the title of one of them was, in so many words, The Art of Not Being Governed. This is a man whose instrument detects one kind of error superbly and is not built to detect its opposite.

That is not a refutation. Orwell's audit, the fourth episode of this run established, cannot distinguish a lean from a long-horizon call, and the thing that separates them is whether the forecaster has a mechanism. Scott has one, and it is a good one: institutions that can only read documents will destroy what does not fit in documents, and will not know they have done it until the second rotation. What the lean tells you is where to be careful. When Scott says the state's vision failed, believe him; he has shown you the mechanism a dozen times. When he implies the local practice would have been fine if left alone, check, because that is the claim his instrument cannot test and his temperament will not.

## Chapter Seven: Verdict, Dose, and the Question for Monday

The verdict is that this is the book on the shelf that goes deepest into the question the series actually cares about, and that it should be read with its critics in the same week. Every earlier book found an organization failing at something it could see: multiplying its staff, promoting its wrong people, absorbing its complaints. Scott's finding is prior to all of those. The organization fails first at seeing, and the failure is not a defect but the price of being an organization at all, because a body that governs at scale can only govern what it has simplified, and the simplification discards, by construction, the knowledge that lived in the detail. That finding survives Tauger and Alexander and DeLong. What does not survive them is the book's silence about the other direction: the cases where the simplification saved lives, and the question of which institutions, if not the state, keep mētis alive. Hold the mechanism. Distrust the museum.

The dose is diagnostic and specific. Read the forestry chapter and the mētis chapter whole; read the Brasília chapter for pleasure; read the collectivisation chapter with Tauger's review open beside it, and read Alexander's review after, for the objection the book most needs and least wants. Then read Scott's four rules and pin them somewhere. They are the only antidote on this shelf so far that does not require anyone to be better than they are.

And the question for Monday comes straight from the four rules, pointed at whatever your institution is currently planning. Take the plan, the reorganisation, the new pathway, the dashboard, the merger of two services into one, and ask the four questions in order. Is it small enough that you will see what it does before the next step is taken? Can it be undone in less time than it took to build? Has anyone written down what would count as the plan being wrong? And what have the people who will live inside it been left free to change? If the answers are no, no, no, and nothing, then you are looking at a scientific forest in its first rotation, and the yields will be magnificent, and you should find out now who is going to be reintroducing the ants.
