## Chapter One: The Second Rotation

In the last decades of the eighteenth century the states of Prussia and Saxony had a problem that every treasury recognises. Wood was the fuel, the building material, and the ship of the age, and the crown's forests were producing it in amounts nobody could state. A forest, to the people who lived beside it, was a place of grazing, foraging, firewood, medicine, game, thatch, bark, and resin, used by dozens of overlapping customary rights that no clerk had ever written down. To the fiscal officer in the capital it was a source of revenue with an unknown yield, and therefore a source of anxiety. And so the officer's profession invented a discipline, scientific forestry, whose first act was to redescribe the forest as one thing. Not a habitat, not a commons: a quantity of saleable timber, standing, per hectare, per year.

The redescription started as an accounting method and became a landscape. Foresters sampled plots, counted trees, sorted them into size classes, and built tables from which the yield of a whole forest could be computed from its area, and the tables were so much easier to use on a uniform forest that the profession began to grow uniform forests. Old mixed woodland was cleared. In its place, in rows, at fixed spacing, all of one age, went a single species selected for how fast it turned sunlight into board feet, most often Norway spruce. The underbrush was cleared because underbrush is not timber. The dead wood was removed because dead wood is not timber. What resulted was a forest a clerk could read from a desk: so many trees, so many years, so much revenue, and the first rotation, the first crop of trees to grow from seedling to harvest, was magnificent. Yields came in as predicted. The method was adopted across Europe, then exported to the colonies and to the United States, and the foresters who had designed it were, in the ordinary way of these things, promoted.

The second rotation failed. Trees planted in the same rows on the same ground grew slower, sickened more, and blew down more readily, and the decline was steep enough that German forestry acquired a word for it, Waldsterben, forest death. It took the better part of a century to understand why, and the reason is the book in one paragraph. The old forest had been doing an enormous amount of work that did not appear in the tables. The dead wood and the underbrush had been feeding the soil; the mixed species had been sharing the pests and the wind between them; the birds and the insects and the fungi that a spruce plantation could not support had been the forest's maintenance crew. The tables had not measured any of that because none of it was timber, and the forest had been rebuilt to match the tables. What the foresters had been managing, for one rotation, was a bank balance drawn on capital they could not see. When the capital ran out they were reduced to what Scott calls, with some relish, restoration forestry: reintroducing, by hand, in boxes, the ant colonies and spiders and birds' nests whose removal nobody had recorded, because their presence had never been written down.

Hold that story, because the book you are about to hear about is a long argument that the forest is what a state does to everything it looks at. The series has spent sixteen episodes inside organizations asking where their competence goes. This book turns the question around and asks what an organization can see in the first place, and it answers that the seeing is the problem, before any of the failures the shelf has catalogued have a chance to occur.

## Chapter Two: An Anarchist at Yale

James C. Scott, for the listener who needs the biography, spent his career at Yale as a professor of political science and anthropology, and spent his intellectual life on the losing side of the encounter between states and the people they govern. His early books, The Moral Economy of the Peasant in nineteen seventy-six and Weapons of the Weak in nineteen eighty-five, were fieldwork among Southeast Asian villagers, and the second of them is where the book you are hearing about was born. He had gone to Malaysia to study how peasants resisted, expecting revolts, and found instead foot-dragging, false compliance, pilfering, slander, sabotage, and a stubborn refusal to be counted, which he came to see as the ordinary politics of people who cannot afford open politics. Seeing Like a State, published by Yale in nineteen ninety-eight, is the view from the other side of that encounter: what the state was trying to do, why it kept failing, and why it never stopped trying. Its subtitle is How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed, and the word certain is doing careful work, which the second part of this pair will come back to.

One more thing about the author, because it shapes the reading. Scott called himself, half-seriously, an anarchist, and wrote a later book called Two Cheers for Anarchism, and the two cheers rather than three are characteristic. He was not against states. He was against a particular kind of confidence states acquire, and he was equally suspicious of large corporations, which he thought saw like states too. The distinction has consequences, because the book has been claimed since by people to Scott's right, who read it as an argument for markets against planning, and Scott spent a good deal of his later life explaining that he had not written that book. Whether he had, in fact, is one of the questions for part two.

## Chapter Three: Legibility

The book's first word for what the foresters did is legibility, and it is the word the shelf will keep. A state, in Scott's account, has one problem before all others: it cannot see its own territory. It wants to tax, conscript, and prevent rebellion, and every one of those requires knowing who is where, owning what, producing how much, and the society it faces has been built by people with no interest in being known. Local practice is dense, particular, and illegible from the capital. Land is held in overlapping customary tenures that make sense to the village and to nobody else; people are known by names that change with the season, the trade, and the father; grain is measured in baskets whose size depends on the district and, in bad years, on the goodwill of the person filling them. To govern that, the state has to simplify it, and the simplification is not a description of what is there. It is a replacement.

The examples are the pleasure of the book, and three of them belong in the ear. The first is the surname. For most of history most people did not have one; they had a given name and a set of local descriptors, John the smith, John by the mill, John son of Tom, which worked perfectly for people who knew John and were useless to a tax collector who did not. Permanent, inherited surnames were, in almost every case Scott traces, a state project: the Florentine catasto of the fourteen twenties, which needed to attach households to their tax assessments across years; the Spanish administration of the Philippines, which in eighteen forty-nine issued a catalogue of surnames and assigned them to the population alphabetically by district, so that a modern map of Filipino surnames still reproduces the order of the pages; the Prussian and Napoleonic states requiring their Jewish subjects to adopt fixed family names as a condition of civil status. A surname is a handle the state can hold you by. Once you have one, you can be found next year.

The second example is measurement. A pre-modern bushel was not a quantity; it was a relationship. The basket was heaped or levelled, struck or shaken down, according to who was paying whom and what the custom of the district had negotiated over generations, and every one of those variations encoded something a local person understood and an outsider could not. The metric system, born in the French Revolution, was among other things an act of state vision: one metre, one litre, one kilogram, everywhere, forever, so that a report from any district could be read against one from anywhere else. Scott is careful to say that this was often a liberation. A landlord who could shrink the peasant's bushel at will was a landlord the metre disarmed. But the same reform that disarmed him made the peasant's grain readable to Paris, which had its own uses for it.

The third is the cadastral map, the survey that draws property as bounded parcels with a single owner and a number, so that the parcel can be taxed and, when the time comes, sold. Scott's argument is that the map does not record land tenure. It creates a land tenure that the map can record. Every overlapping right, the widow's gleaning, the village's grazing after harvest, the neighbour's path, is either written onto the map as a formal encumbrance, which rarely happens, or vanishes from the legal world, which usually does. The forest again: the map is the yield table, and the countryside is rebuilt to match it.

Here is where this book joins the shelf's running argument, and the join is close. Two episodes ago The Utopia of Rules described interpretive labour: in any relationship of unequal power, the party with less power carries the burden of understanding the other, translating themselves into the other's categories, because the party with more power does not have to bother. Scott has found the state-sized version. Legibility is interpretive labour imposed from above at the scale of a whole society, the population obliged to present itself in forms the capital can read, and the capital under no obligation to learn the population's forms in return. Graeber called the result structural stupidity, and Scott's forests are what structural stupidity looks like when it has a budget and a surveying team. But Scott adds the half Graeber did not have. The simplifications are not merely a convenience for power. They are, most of the time, the only way a large state can function at all. A state that could not count would not be a gentler state; it would be one unable to vaccinate, could not run a court, could not deliver a pension. The problem is not that the map is simpler than the territory. Every map is. The problem arrives when the people holding the map forget that, and begin to treat the territory's departures from the map as errors in the territory.

## Chapter Four: The Grid

Scott has a pair of maps early in the book that I would put in front of anyone who wants the argument in one glance. The first is Bruges around fifteen hundred: a tangle of streets that curve, fork, narrow, and dead-end, a city that has grown the way a coral grows, by accretion, each street the trace of some decision made by people who lived on it. The second is Chicago in the eighteen nineties: a grid, every block the same, every street numbered, so regular that a stranger can find any address in the city from the address alone. Scott's point is not that one is good and one bad. It is that they are legible to different people. Bruges is legible to its residents and opaque to everyone else; a stranger without a guide is lost within three turns, and so, usefully, is an invading army or a tax assessor. Chicago is legible to the stranger, the assessor, the surveyor, the fire brigade, and the real-estate lawyer, and it is legible from the map, which means it can be governed from the map by people who have never walked it.

The grid was, in the American case, a deliberate instrument of exactly that kind. Manhattan's commissioners' plan of eighteen eleven laid twelve avenues and one hundred and fifty-five cross streets over an island that was still mostly farms, hills, and streams, and the commissioners were candid that the reason was that right-angled houses are the cheapest to build and the easiest to sell. The plan ignored the topography entirely; the hills were to be levelled and the streams filled, and they were. What resulted was a city that could be subdivided, taxed, and traded in identical parcels by anyone with a copy of the plan, which was the intention, and a city whose one great irregular space, Central Park, had to be carved out later, against the grid, once it became obvious what the grid could not provide. The grid is the forestry table drawn on land: a redescription of a place as a set of interchangeable units, which then becomes the place.

And there is a darker case that Scott raises and does not dwell on, and the shelf should, because it is the example that stops the argument being merely about efficiency. A legible population is a population that can be found. The Netherlands entered the Second World War with one of the best civil registration systems in Europe, complete, accurate, and cross-referenced by address and religion, built over decades for the ordinary and benign purposes of a modern state. When the occupying authorities wanted to locate the country's Jews, the registers gave them the list. In France, whose records were worse, and in Belgium, where the registry offices were in some cases sabotaged by their own staff, the proportion of the Jewish population who were found and deported was much lower. Nobody who built the Dutch registers intended that use. The registers did not care what was intended. Legibility is a capacity, and a capacity, once built, belongs to whoever holds the state, a truth about states rather than about the Dutch, and it is the reason Scott's suspicion of the map is not a sentimental preference for medieval streets. The tangle that frustrates the assessor frustrates the police just as well.

That is the cost side of the ledger, and it should be stated as plainly as the benefit side was a moment ago. The metre disarmed the landlord and the surname enabled the pension, and the same instruments located the deportees and levelled the hills. There is no version of the instrument that does only the first. The question the book is really asking, from here on, is what determines which use a state makes of the vision it has built, and Scott's answer is the four conditions that the next chapter names.

## Chapter Five: High Modernism

Scott gives that forgetting a name, and the name is the second word the shelf will keep: high modernism. He means by it something more specific than modernity and more dangerous than planning. It is a faith, at its height between roughly eighteen ninety and the nineteen sixties, that the same scientific rationality which had produced the railway and the vaccine could be applied to the design of whole societies, and that the designer, armed with that rationality, was entitled to override the objections of the people being designed, because the objections were, by definition, pre-scientific. High modernism is not left or right. Scott's examples include Lenin and Le Corbusier, Robert McNamara and Walther Rathenau, the Soviet agronomist and the American highway engineer, and what they share is an aesthetic rather than a politics: a love of straight lines, grids, uniformity, and the view from above, and a corresponding contempt for the tangle that any living settlement presents when you walk through it at ground level.

Now the book's method becomes visible, and it should be named because it is the thing Scott is best at. He does not argue that high modernists were wicked. Most of them were sincere, some were brilliant, and their goals, housing for the poor, food for the hungry, a city without slums, were goals any decent person shared. His argument is that the aesthetic did the damage regardless of the intention, because the aesthetic determined what the planner could see, and that turned out to be the plan. The tangle at ground level, which is where all the information about how a place actually works is stored, registered to the high-modernist eye as disorder, and disorder was what the plan existed to abolish.

Scott then supplies the framework that the rest of the book is a series of applications of, and it is worth stating exactly because the second part of this pair will need it. Legibility alone is not a catastrophe. Every modern state simplifies, and most survive it. High modernism alone is not a catastrophe either; an architect with a grid and no power builds an unpleasant building. The disasters the book is about require four things together: the administrative ordering of nature and society, the high-modernist ideology that makes the ordering a moral mission, an authoritarian state willing and able to use its full coercive power to realise the design, and a prostrate civil society that lacks the capacity to resist. Take away any one and the failure is smaller, or recoverable, or never happens. Put all four together and the plan gets built exactly as drawn, and the forest's second rotation arrives for people.

## Chapter Six: The Straight Street

Before the book gets to the cases where all four conditions were met, it stops in cities, and the city chapter is the one I would have a listener keep if they kept one, because it is where the argument can be checked against a place you can visit.

Begin with Paris under Haussmann, between eighteen fifty-three and eighteen seventy. Napoleon the Third's prefect cut boulevards through the medieval city, straight, wide, and long, and the official reasons were circulation, sanitation, and light, all of which were real; the Paris he inherited was crowded, sick, and dark. But the boulevards had a second logic that Scott reads out of their geometry. A straight wide street is a street a regiment can march down and a cannon can fire along, and the quarters the boulevards cut through were, not by coincidence, the quarters that had thrown up barricades in eighteen thirty and eighteen forty-eight. Legibility again: the state could now see, and reach, parts of its own capital that had been dark to it. The modern Paris everyone loves is, among other things, the map imposed on the city so that the city could be governed from the map.

Then Le Corbusier, who is high modernism with a drafting pencil. His Plan Voisin of nineteen twenty-five proposed demolishing the centre of Paris north of the Seine and replacing it with eighteen identical cruciform towers in a park, connected by motorways, with the functions of the city, living, working, shopping, moving, sorted into separate zones so that each could be designed to its single purpose. The plan was never built, and that is the only reason we can still visit Paris. But its principles were, in whole or part, in a great many places, and Scott's reading of the principles is exact: they are the yield table applied to human settlement. A city is redescribed as a set of quantities, dwellings per hectare, cubic metres of air per person, minutes of travel to work, and then rebuilt so that the quantities are optimal. The redescription leaves out the same things the forestry tables left out. It leaves out everything that happens in a street because streets have corners, and shops have doorsteps, and children play where adults can see them, and a neighbour notices a stranger, and a thousand small unplanned encounters keep a neighbourhood safe, useful, and alive. Le Corbusier's contempt for the street was explicit: he thought the corridor street a relic, and he thought the people who liked it were sentimental.

The book's counter-witness is Jane Jacobs, whose Death and Life of Great American Cities in nineteen sixty-one is Scott's anthology of what the plan cannot see, and here the series' two most recent books meet. Jacobs walked the streets Le Corbusier would have demolished and catalogued what they were doing: mixed use, so that the street was populated at every hour by different people for different reasons; short blocks, so that paths crossed; old buildings beside new, so that rents varied and the cheap ones housed the things a neighbourhood needed but could not pay much for; density, so that eyes were on the street. None of that was in any plan, because it had not been planned. It had accreted, the way the old forest accreted, by the trial and error of people who used the place daily and adjusted it a doorstep at a time. Jacobs never gave the missing thing a single name. Scott does, and he saves it for the second half: the practical, local, unwritten knowledge of how a particular place works, mētis, after the Greek word for the cunning of Odysseus, and which the second part of this pair will spend a chapter on.

## Chapter Seven: The City Built in Four Years

Brasília is where Scott lets the aesthetic build its perfect object, because in Brasília it was allowed to. In nineteen fifty-six the Brazilian president Juscelino Kubitschek decided to move the capital from Rio to an empty plateau in the interior, and to do it within his term. The plan was Lúcio Costa's, a great cross of two axes laid on the empty ground, one monumental, for government, one residential, curved like a wing; the buildings were Oscar Niemeyer's, and both men were disciples of Le Corbusier. The city was designed whole, on paper, before anyone lived there, with the functions sorted into sectors, a hotel sector, a banking sector, a residential sector of identical superblocks, each superblock a set of identical apartment buildings on stilts in open ground, so that from the air the whole city read as a single legible diagram, which it does to this day. It was inaugurated in nineteen sixty, four years after the decision. It is, by common consent, one of the most beautiful things ever photographed from an aircraft.

What Scott describes is what it is like on the ground, and he leans on the anthropologist James Holston, who lived there and wrote The Modernist City. The plan had abolished the street. There were no corners, because the superblocks were set in open space; no doorsteps, because the buildings stood on pillars; no crowds, because the sectors had sorted the crowd's reasons for existing into separate districts miles apart; no small shops beneath the apartments, because commerce had its own sector. The residents developed a word, brasilite, Brasília-itis, for the condition of living there, and Holston's informants described it as the absence of the small pleasures of city life, the crowd, the chance meeting, the sense that a place was being used. People who could afford to left for the satellite towns that grew up, unplanned and illegible, around the plan's edge, which is where most of the capital's population has lived ever since. The planned city was built, exactly as drawn. The city that people wanted to live in built itself, next to it, the way the underbrush comes back in the plantation.

And here is the observation that makes Brasília the book's hinge rather than one more example. The plan did not fail because it was badly executed. It was executed superbly, on time, by gifted people, and the failure is the execution. Every previous book on this shelf has found competence leaking out of an organization somewhere between intention and result: Parkinson's paperwork, Peter's final placements, the sinks that absorb what nobody wanted to know. Scott's cases invert that. Brasília is what happens when nothing leaks, when a plan meets an authority strong enough to carry it out without friction and a population unable to resist. The competence was all there. It was pointed at a picture of a city rather than at a city, and no amount of competence corrects that, because the correction would have had to come from the ground, and the ground was the thing the picture could not see.

## Chapter Eight: What the Shelf Now Has, and What It Still Needs

Set this half of the book beside the last two episodes and a shape appears that neither Graeber nor Orwell had on their own. Orwell caught Burnham predicting a continuation of whatever was happening, and diagnosed the lean: a mind enchanted by power reads the present as the future. Scott's high modernist has a lean of the same family running the other way. He does not read the present as the future; he reads the plan as the present, and everything in the present that fails to match the plan as noise. Burnham's error was to trust the map of the winners. The planner's error is to trust a map he drew himself, which is a harder error to catch, because the map is very good, and because the person best placed to catch it, the villager or the resident or the forester's labourer, has been defined in advance as the person whose knowledge does not count.

And Graeber's structural stupidity, which two episodes ago looked like a moral failing of the powerful, now looks like something closer to a design constraint. A state cannot govern what it cannot read, and the tangle cannot be read without being simplified, and the simplification will always leave out the things that are hardest to write down, which are, as a rule, the things that keep the place alive. That is not a claim about bad people. It is about what a large organization is, and it lands, for this series, as a limit on every antidote the shelf has yet to consider. Whatever repair the later books propose, rotation, rival power, formal addresses for complaints, the repair will be administered by an institution that sees like a state, and its first act will be to make the problem legible enough to repair, which is where the trouble began.

The question part one leaves on the table is the one the four conditions pose, and it is a question about people rather than trees or streets. Brasília cost money and comfort; nobody starved in a superblock. What happens when the same aesthetic, backed by the same authority, facing the same prostrate society, is applied not to where people live but to how they eat, to the fields and herds that feed a country, by planners who have never farmed and are certain that farming is a science? Scott has two answers, one Soviet and one Tanzanian, and they are the darkest material this shelf has handled. He also has a word for what the planners destroyed, and a chapter arguing that the word names the kind of knowledge a society can least afford to lose. And then the critics arrive, some of whom think Scott has written the book he denied writing, and one of whom, an economist, suggests that the book's real hero is a man Scott never mentions, Friedrich Hayek. That is part two.
