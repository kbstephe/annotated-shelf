description: "Jon Gertner's history of Bell Labs, read against the inventors' own Nobel lectures and Shannon's 1948 paper: a failed amplifier that redirected a research program, a telephone monopoly that paid for it, and whether any institution can now afford to do the same."

In 1936 a new physics doctorate named William Shockley arrived at Bell Telephone Laboratories and was assigned to a course in vacuum tubes. His supervisor, Mervin Kelly, used the course to tell him about a wish. Kelly wanted every telephone call in the country switched electronically, with no moving metal contacts anywhere in the exchange. Shockley did not take up vacuum tubes. He was given freedom to work on basic solid-state physics instead, and twenty years later, standing in Stockholm to collect a Nobel Prize, he said that the conversation had left him continually alert for any solid-state effect that might bear on switching.

The sentence describes an employer who handed a young man a goal and then left him alone. Most accounts of scientific freedom treat those two things as opposites. The question for this episode is whether Bell Labs showed they could be combined on purpose, or whether it was a one-time accident of money and personalities that the rest of us have been trying to copy ever since.

The book is Jon Gertner's The Idea Factory, subtitled Bell Labs and the Great Age of American Innovation, published in 2012. Gertner is a journalist, and the book follows a handful of people through the Labs from the nineteen-twenties to the nineteen-seventies. I did not hold the book; the library copies are lending-only. What I held is better for some purposes and worse for others: the Nobel lectures that Shockley, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain gave on the eleventh of December 1956, and Claude Shannon's 1948 paper, A Mathematical Theory of Communication. Where I say what Gertner argues, it is from my own knowledge of the book, and I will say so in the sentence.

## Chapter One: The Amplifier That Did Not Work

Begin with a failure, because the inventors told it themselves, in detail.

By the end of the Second World War there was a long-standing hope of building a solid-state amplifier, a triode made from a semiconductor instead of a glass tube. Bardeen's lecture lays out the two routes people had imagined. One was to slip a grid into the thin layer at a metal and semiconductor contact, the way a vacuum tube has a grid. The layer was about a ten-thousandth of a centimetre wide, and nobody could fit a grid in it. A German pair had made it work in a crystal where the layer was a centimetre thick, but only at frequencies below one cycle per second, which is a very slow amplifier.

The second route was Shockley's. Take a thin slab of semiconductor, lay a metal plate beside it like one plate of a capacitor, and put a voltage on the plate. The voltage should pull charge into the slab, and if that charge could move, the slab would conduct better or worse as the voltage changed. Shockley's calculations said the effect should be large enough to amplify. He and several colleagues tried it with films of germanium and silicon, at different times, using different materials. Every attempt gave nothing.

Here is what the failure did. The researchers had a prediction from solid theory that was not borne out, and they had a choice. They could improve the films, or they could ask why the theory was wrong. Bardeen proposed an answer, published in 1947: electrons were stuck at the surface in localized states, and most of the charge induced by the plate was getting trapped there before it could do any conducting. Surface states had been predicted as a possibility, Bardeen says, but almost nothing was known about them by experiment. So the program changed shape. In his words, the decision was made to stress research on surfaces, beginning in 1946.

The order ran like this. The device failed, the failure produced an explanation, the explanation was itself a research question, and the program was reorganized around the question. Nobody at the Labs set out to find the transistor effect. Bardeen writes that the transistor principle, in which both electrons and holes play a role, was not anticipated at the start of the program and was discovered in the course of basic research on surface properties.

That is a claim about the Labs' method, and it runs against the way these places are usually described. The usual account is that Bell Labs aimed researchers at telephone problems and results followed. The inventors' own account is that a telephone problem, the amplifier, was posed first, that it failed, and that the failure steered them into pure science, which then produced something better than the amplifier they had wanted. The practical goal supplied the first question. The basic research supplied the answer to a different one.

## Chapter Two: The Drop of Water

To see how the answer arrived, follow the experiments, because Bardeen's lecture reads almost like a lab notebook at this point.

If surface states existed, a strong electric field at the surface should be able to push through them in some conditions. Brattain was measuring how the electrical potential at the surface of a semiconductor changed when light fell on it. He tried different atmospheres and different temperatures. Then he filled the gap between his electrode and the crystal with a liquid dielectric and saw a large effect. He and the physical chemist Gibney, who had joined the group, then tried electrolytes, salt solutions, and found big changes in the surface barrier from a voltage applied across the liquid. Ions piling up at the surface made a field strong enough to penetrate the trapped charge.

That was a way to apply a field at will. Bardeen and Brattain then built the arrangement the field-effect idea had wanted. A fine metal point touched a block of silicon, and around the point, insulated from it, sat a drop of electrolyte with an electrode in it. Earlier work by two Bell colleagues, Ohl and Scaff, had shown that a particular oxidation could give silicon an inversion layer, a skin just under the surface that conducts by the opposite kind of carrier. The current through the point could be changed by changing the voltage in the drop. Since almost no current flowed through the drop, this was an amplifier. Not a good one: they saw current and power gain but not voltage gain.

Then they switched to germanium of the other type, with no reason to expect anything in particular. Bardeen says they had no prior knowledge that a germanium surface would grow an inversion layer of the type they found, and the experiment showed that a large part of the current flowing through the point was holes moving in such a layer, a hole being a missing electron that carries current as if it were a positive particle. Now there was voltage gain as well. The drop of water was the problem; it was far too slow, so the gain existed only at very low frequencies. They wanted to replace the electrolyte with a metal electrode insulated by a thin oxide.

Gibney prepared a germanium surface by anodizing it and then evaporating several gold spots onto it. None of the spots made the high-resistance contact the plan required. Bardeen writes that they decided to see what effects would be obtained anyway. A point contact was placed very near one gold spot and biased in the reverse direction, and the gold spot was given a positive bias. If the old theory had been right, raising the bias should have reduced the current at the point, as it had with the electrolyte. It raised it. The effect was in the opposite direction to what the earlier setup produced.

The reading they gave this result was that holes were flowing from the gold into the germanium and on to the point, adding to its reverse current. Bardeen calls it the first indication of the transistor effect. It gave voltage gain but no power gain. They estimated that power gain would come if the two contacts were about five thousandths of a centimetre apart. For the first try, the method was to evaporate gold onto a wedge and then cut the gold at the tip of the wedge with a razor blade, which left two contacts that close. That worked. The easiest way to repeat it turned out to be two sharpened point contacts pressed very close together on the crystal.

So the first working transistor was, at its business end, a razor cut in a film of gold. And it arrived by way of an experiment whose result went the wrong way for the theory the experimenters were testing. A surprise in a sign is easy to lose in a lab that is looking for confirmation. The group had a theorist, Bardeen, standing at the bench with an experimentalist, and the combination turned a wrong-signed result into an observation about holes.

## Chapter Three: Who Was in the Room

Now widen the frame, from the bench to the people around it, because that is where Gertner's book spends its time, and where the lectures give independent evidence for his claims.

Bardeen lists the semiconductor group as it stood in 1946. Shockley directed it. Brattain worked on surfaces and rectification, Pearson on bulk properties, Bardeen himself on theory. Gibney, a physical chemist, joined later, and so did a circuit engineer. The list contains a theorist, three kinds of experimentalist, a chemist and an engineer. No one skill in it could have done the work alone, and the lecture thanks a series of outsiders as well.

The outsiders matter more. Brattain's lecture opens with a paragraph of thanks that names three people outside the group, Ohl, Scaff and Henry Theuerer. Scaff and Theuerer were metallurgists who supplied the silicon and germanium. Ohl, Brattain says, first discovered a p-n junction in a melt of silicon they prepared for him. Brattain adds that one needs to be humble about the award on remembering how fortunate he was to be in the right environment at the right time, and prize winners are expected to say such things, so it needs checking: can the environment he describes be seen doing work in the rest of the lectures?

It can. Bardeen says outright that the theoretical foundation had been built in the nineteen-thirties by Wilson, Frenkel, Mott, Schottky and others, and that the practical advance of the nineteen-forties was purification and control of germanium and silicon, driven by wartime radar detectors. Bell Labs had a silicon group going since before the war. When the transistor group needed a crystal pure enough to make a clean experiment, the people who could make it were down the hall. The reason that point is easy to miss is that it is a point about plumbing. A university team with the same ideas would have had to grow its own germanium.

Gertner's argument, as I know it, is that the Labs deliberately arranged this. Kelly, who ran the place's research and later the whole Laboratories, is his central figure, and he built, and wrote about, what he called an institute of creative technology: physicists, chemists, metallurgists and engineers under one roof and one budget, so that a problem could be walked from one specialty to the next. In Gertner's telling Kelly even cared about the design of the Murray Hill building, with its long corridors, so that people would bump into each other. I take that from memory of the book and do not vouch for the details. The Shockley lecture does support the principle, though. Shockley names the four men who had directed research and then run the Laboratories, Arnold, Jewett, Buckley and Kelly, and observes that each had a physics doctorate and a thorough indoctrination in the research view. The company that employed the scientists was run by scientists.

There is a second way to read that, and it is less flattering to the Labs. Shockley's lecture is from a man who intends his audience to believe that applied goals do not corrupt research. He says he does not think a specific practical goal degrades its quality, and he picks examples from projects motivated by practicality to prove it. He is arguing a case of his own. A Nobel lecture is not a neutral document, and this one gives the winner's preferred theory of how he won. I will come back to the other things such a lecture leaves out.

## Chapter Four: The Problem That Was Not a Product

In the summer of 1948, within weeks of the Labs' announcement of the transistor, the Bell System Technical Journal began publishing a two-part paper by a mathematician named Claude Shannon. It is the clearest case in the Labs' history of a result that could not have been ordered, and its first page says why.

Shannon opens by crediting two earlier papers in the same journal, by Nyquist in 1924 and Hartley in 1928, so the question had been circulating at the Labs for twenty years. Then he narrows it. The fundamental problem of communication, he writes, is reproducing at one point, exactly or approximately, a message selected at another point. Messages often have meaning. Those semantic aspects, he says, are irrelevant to the engineering problem. What matters is that the actual message is one selected from a set of possible messages, and the system must work for every message in the set, since nobody knows which will be chosen.

That move, setting meaning aside, is what let him treat the telephone line, the telegraph key and a page of English as the same kind of thing. He chose a logarithmic measure for the amount of information, and gave three reasons: it is practically useful, because time, bandwidth and the number of relays all grow with the logarithm of the number of possibilities; it matches intuition, because two punched cards should hold twice what one does; and it is mathematically convenient. With base two, he says, the unit may be called a binary digit, or bit, a word suggested by his colleague John Tukey. A relay or a flip-flop circuit with two stable positions stores one bit, and a bank of N of them stores N.

Everything in the paper goes on to be about what to do under noise, and the central results are existence results. For a channel with a given capacity, there is a way to encode the source so the message can be sent at a rate approaching that capacity, and not above it. He shows that for a noisy channel it is possible, under general conditions, to find a coding system that sends binary digits at the capacity rate with as small a frequency of errors as desired. Before the paper, engineers took it as given that cutting errors meant cutting speed or raising power. The theorem says there is a ceiling on the rate and, beneath the ceiling, errors can be driven toward zero by coding alone.

The theorem does not say how. Shannon proves that good codes exist and does not build one, and the decades after his paper were largely a search for codes that approach his limit. The Labs again supplied part of that search: Richard Hamming, who earlier in this series gave the talk about important problems, invented his error-correcting codes at the Labs in these same years, by his own account out of annoyance at a weekend computer run that kept stopping on errors. That is from the Hamming episode and my memory of his own telling, and the point here is the setting.

What did the telephone company get from a paper whose first page declares meaning irrelevant? In the short run, nothing it could sell. Gertner's account, which I know only second-hand, is that Shannon was close to the free-roaming ideal of the place: hired in the early nineteen-forties, given the room to think about a question for years, famous in the corridors for riding a unicycle. In the long run, the company's entire business was moving messages through noisy wires, and a theory of the limits of doing so was worth more to it than any single product. It could say what a line could carry and what no cleverness could improve. The Labs paid for an answer, and the answer had a buyer only because the same company ran a very large version of the question.

## Chapter Five: Two Rules of Selection

Set the two events side by side and ask what the institution contributed to each. They are close together in time, from the same building, and nearly opposite in shape.

The transistor came out of a team, a purified material, an experimental accident and a long chain of small decisions. If the whole group had been replaced by an equally capable group, the discovery would probably still have happened, a little later, because Bardeen's lecture is full of other people nearly there. Purdue's group under Karl Lark-Horovitz was studying germanium for the radar program. Patents from the nineteen-twenties and thirties by Lilienfeld and others had proposed field-effect devices, and Bardeen says those people apparently did not succeed. The wartime crystal work was happening at several American laboratories. Bell Labs may have won by a matter of months, or years, I cannot tell from these documents. What the Labs supplied was speed, and the right crystal.

Shannon's paper looks different. It has a rhetorical structure that reads like a single mind: the opening definitions, the choice of log base, the careful sequence from noiseless to noisy channels. The ideas were not wholly new, since Nyquist and Hartley had pushed in this direction. But it is hard to picture another author producing, in 1948, the particular package of entropy, capacity and the coding theorems. Here the institution's contribution was permission, and the permission was to work for years on a question without a product attached.

So there are two kinds of work in one building, and the building was good for both. For team work with a clear target and a materials bottleneck, a place that has all the specialties and can grow its own crystals is simply faster. An individual's work with a distant payoff needs a place that does not demand quarterly justification. Gertner's sections, in my memory of them, are the ones that let both sorts of person stand next to each other, the committee-built transistor and the private mathematics, and that make you wonder how one employer put up with such different people. The easy answer is Kelly's genius. The less easy answer, which the evidence favours, is that nobody was pressing the Labs to choose between them.

## Chapter Six: The Money

Which brings up the question that Gertner puts at the center of his closing chapters and that none of the Nobel lectures touches: who paid.

The Labs belonged to AT&T, which ran the American telephone system as a regulated monopoly. Its prices were set by regulators on the basis of its costs, and as I understand the standard account, research expenditure was among the costs. In practice every telephone customer paid a small amount toward the Labs. Nobody in the system had to show that a given year's work would pay back, because the rate base absorbed it. That is a form of funding with almost no parallel: large, steady, insulated from the market the work was nominally for, and spent mostly at the discretion of scientists.

I should be plain about the status of this part. The mechanics of rate regulation and the details of the 1956 antitrust settlement are not in anything I held. I know the outline, which is that AT&T agreed in a consent decree to license its patents, including the transistor's, on reasonable terms, and to stay out of the computer business. Gertner treats that settlement as both a gift and a ceiling: the transistor spread to every maker of electronics in the world, which is why the industry grew the way it did, and the Labs' parent was fenced out of the largest market its own invention created. If that account is right, the monopoly paid for the discovery and the settlement gave it away.

One consequence, which I think follows whether or not the details are exactly right: the Labs' discoveries were not captured by the people who funded them. The telephone customers paid, the world got the transistor and the information theory, and AT&T collected a fraction. Seen from the company's side that looks like a very expensive public good. From the world's side it was a subsidy for science on a scale few governments matched. It lasted as long as the company was a monopoly, and the breakup in 1984 ended the arrangement that had underwritten it. The Labs continued afterward, and still won Nobel Prizes, but no longer held its former position among American laboratories.

That makes the structural claim of the book, as I read it: the thing could not have been designed on paper in advance, and cannot be rebuilt on paper now, because its first condition is a customer base that cannot leave, a regulator that lets the cost through, and a firm patient enough to spend it. This is a different kind of argument from the one in the Skunk Works episode. There the condition was a customer with a blank check and a war to win, a buyer who could say yes in one conversation. Bell Labs had the opposite, a customer that said nothing at all, since the people who paid and the people who were served were the same subscribers. In both places the founders' rules cost little. What cost a great deal was a patron who could not, or would not, ask what the money bought this quarter.

## Chapter Seven: What the Lectures Leave Out

A Nobel lecture is a tidy document, and what it omits is conflict. Reading the three together, you would think the group was a happy family. Bardeen's lecture says Shockley was the general director and that he instigated much of the research on injected carriers in bulk material that followed. Brattain speaks of his colleagues with gratitude. None of the three mentions that, as Gertner and the other histories tell it, the relationship between Shockley and the two men who built the first working device fell apart in the winter the discovery was made.

From memory of the book: Shockley, the group leader, was not named on the first patent applications for that first device, because the lawyers found that earlier field-effect patents by Lilienfeld covered the part of the work in which he had played the lead role. He responded by working in private on a different design, the junction transistor, and had a theory of it within weeks. Bardeen and Brattain were, in effect, sidelined; Bardeen left the Labs for a university a few years later. Shockley left in the nineteen-fifties to start his own company in California, and, as Gertner tells it, the tight culture he built there so alienated his best employees that about eight of them left together to found Fairchild Semiconductor, and from Fairchild, by various routes, came most of Silicon Valley.

That sequence is Gertner's, and I did not hold it. Set it against the argument. The same Labs that allowed Bardeen and Brattain their razor-blade experiment could not manage the personality of the man whose proposal had begun the whole program, and could not keep any of the three together afterwards. The institution was good at starting things and weak at what came next. Its patience with talent stretched to an extremely difficult manager, until the difficulty took first-rate people out of the building. The exits were not a failure of the idea factory so much as a measure of what it could not hold. They carried the knowledge into companies that were better at turning it into products than a regulated telephone company was permitted to be.

The Labs seeded its own competition, through its human failures as much as through its patent licences. What it did best was produce knowledge and people that others could use. What it did worst was keep them.

## Chapter Eight: The Case Against

Now put the objection as hard as it goes.

The first objection is survivorship. A book about a lab that made the transistor and information theory is bound to portray the lab's habits as causes. For every habit Gertner credits, long corridors, the mixed disciplines, the patient funding, there were other well-funded laboratories with the same habits that produced little. The lectures cannot answer that, because Nobel committees do not give lectures from the places that tried and failed. A reader should ask what the control group looked like. I do not know of a systematic comparison of corporate laboratories of that era, and the book, as I recall it, is a narrative and does not offer one.

The second is priority. I said above that Bardeen's lecture names several groups close to the transistor. If the effect was almost discoverable elsewhere, the Labs' claim to have been uniquely able is weaker, and the case that the institutional design mattered rests on a few months' head start. The defensible version of the claim is that the design made the discovery faster and made the subsequent engineering, the junction transistor, the purified crystals, the manufacturing, happen inside one organization. That is a real advantage, and it is not a miracle.

The third is the one Shockley's own lecture raises. He insists that a practical goal does not degrade research. That might be true of him and of that decade, and the Labs' record fits it. But the Labs' practical goal was unusually soft. The telephone company wanted better switching and better transmission in a general sense, with no deadline, and it had no competitor who would take its customers if the answer came late. A goal that sits that loosely on the work is, in effect, a gentle form of freedom. So the defence of applied research that Shockley offers works only for applied research with the pressure turned down, and for a firm that could afford that.

The fourth is the nostalgia in the genre. A book subtitled the great age of American innovation invites the reader to wish for the age back. The question of whether the American economy today produces fewer such places is a real one, and defenders of the thesis point to Xerox PARC, covered earlier in this series, as the same pattern failing to pay its parent, and to the major technology companies' research arms as partial successors. But the evidence that the present is poorer is itself thin. The Labs' output is compared with the best results of fifty years, selected by hindsight, against the present's unsorted sample.


## Chapter Nine: What Survives

What does a reader take from this that does not depend on owning a telephone monopoly?

Start from the one mechanism I can document from text I held, the Bardeen sequence. A theory-driven attempt fails. The group does not rescue the device. It writes down why the theory failed, and the explanation, surface states, becomes the next program. A tool built for the failed attempt, the electrolyte field, becomes the instrument that finds the real thing. And an experiment that goes the wrong way by sign is read as information instead of error. Almost nothing in that sequence needs Bell's money. It needs a group that has a theorist and an experimenter at the same bench, a habit of treating a null result as a statement about the world, and a leader who permits the program to turn in a direction nobody planned.

The part that does need the money is the time. The first failed field-effect test and the first working point contact are separated in Bardeen's account by experiments run over a couple of years, in a group whose members changed. A funder who wants a deliverable in twelve months would have cancelled the program in its first year, with an honest report that the main hypothesis had failed. In that sense the Labs' real asset was a funder who would read such a report as progress.

Dose. The Idea Factory is a good book to read for its people, and I can recommend it on the basis of its reputation and its subject, though I did not read it. The more useful reading, for the mechanism, is the three short Nobel lectures, in the order Bardeen, Shockley, Brattain, skipping the parts on the physics of surfaces and the mathematics of carriers. Bardeen's account of the sequence of experiments runs to a few pages and shows a discovery happening step by step. Read the first two pages of Shannon as well, which are plain English and introduce the bit. The book's later chapters on the decline are the least supported by documents, so read them with scepticism.

The application is for anyone responsible for a group of early-career researchers, a fellowship director, say, who decides how a trainee's protected time gets spent. Bardeen's sequence suggests a test. At the end of each year, ask every project what its worst failed experiment was, and what that failure said about the assumption underneath the plan. If a project cannot name a failed experiment, it has probably not been testing anything; if it can, and the answer changed the plan, it is behaving like the surface-states group. The bet to place is that, across a year of a fellow's work, the most useful thing is a documented change of direction caused by a result that went the wrong way. Do not penalise the fellow for the failure. Penalise the one whose plan in December reads exactly like the plan in January.
