## Chapter One: The Phone Call

In March of nineteen ninety, a physicist named Donald Braben picked up a phone at British Petroleum and was told that his unit was being closed. He has described the decision, in the decades since, as arriving at what he calls the peak of its capabilities: ten years into a program that had funded some of the more unusual physics, chemistry, and biology being done anywhere in Europe or North America, with no product line to defend it and no annual report a shareholder could read and understand. Nobody inside BP had ever asked the unit to justify itself the way a corporate research budget normally gets justified, because escaping that kind of justification was the one thing the unit had been built to do. That was the whole premise, and for ten straight years an oil company had funded it anyway.

What ended that afternoon was called Venture Research, and its founding idea was almost insultingly simple to state and almost impossible, in Braben's account, to get any other institution to try since. Find scientists whose current thinking runs against the field's own settled assumptions. Do not ask them to write a formal proposal, because a proposal that a stranger could evaluate on paper is, definitionally, not yet a proposal for anything strange enough to matter. Fund them for years, not quarters, with no milestones and no requirement to explain, in writing, on a schedule, what the money bought. Then wait, for longer than any normal funding cycle tolerates, to see what happens.

Braben laid out that argument at length in a book first published in two thousand eight and reissued by Stripe Press twelve years later, titled Scientific Freedom. It reads, at first pass, like an old man's grievance about a system that stopped valuing people like the ones he used to fund. It is also, underneath the grievance, the clearest existing account of what a funding process looks like when it removes every safeguard peer review was built to provide, and a decade-long experiment in whether removing them buys anything worth the risk. Whether it did is where this hour actually needs to go, and Braben's own confident answer is the least useful place in the record to start.

## Chapter Two: A Club With No Meetings

Braben trained as a physicist, earning his doctorate from the University of Liverpool in nineteen sixty-two studying the energy structure of sodium isotopes, and spent the following decades moving through British science administration before BP handed him, in nineteen eighty, a budget and an unusual mandate: find people to bet on, and place the bet without asking permission from anyone who would need it explained first. By the time he wrote Scientific Freedom he had become one of peer review's most persistent public critics, and an honorary professor at University College London, where he later tried to keep a version of the Venture Research idea alive on a much smaller scale after BP let it go.

The book's argument starts from a historical claim rather than a policy one. Braben counts something like three hundred scientists across the twentieth century whose individual work did not just add a result to an existing field but reset the questions the whole field was asking afterward, a set he calls, informally, the Planck Club: Planck breaking classical physics open with the quantum, Einstein doing it twice more, Bohr rebuilding the atom, Barbara McClintock finding genes that jump from one chromosome location to another. McClintock's own case supplies the plainest illustration of the trait Braben claims every one of these three hundred shares. She published the jumping-gene result in nineteen fifty, working alone on maize plants at Cold Spring Harbor, and the genetics community's response was not controversy so much as indifference: the result sat mostly unread and unbelieved for a generation, because nothing in the field's existing framework had room for genes that moved. Only after molecular biology built the machinery to see mobile genetic elements everywhere did the nineteen fifty result look, in hindsight, exactly as important as McClintock had always insisted it was, and only then, in nineteen eighty-three, did the same community that had shrugged it off award her an unshared Nobel Prize for a discovery over thirty years old.

That gap, three decades between the result and the recognition, is the uncomfortable implication Braben wants a reader to carry forward. Peer review works by asking a scientist's closest disciplinary neighbors to judge whether a proposed piece of work is likely to succeed and worth the money. Neighbors are good at spotting whether an idea fits what the field already believes is possible. They are close to the worst possible judges of an idea whose entire premise is that the field's current beliefs are wrong, because the reviewer's own expertise, the very thing that qualifies them to sit on the panel, is built on the assumptions the proposal is trying to unsettle. A system engineered to reward proposals that already look credible to experts will, as a structural matter rather than a failure of any individual reviewer's judgment, keep underfunding the work most likely to eventually justify a seat in Braben's club, and McClintock's thirty years is what that underfunding looks like when a result is strong enough to survive being ignored.

## Chapter Three: No Form to Fill Out

Venture Research's answer to that problem was not a better proposal form. It was the absence of one. Braben and a small internal panel, drawn from inside BP rather than the applicant's own field, sought out working scientists directly and talked with them, at length, in person, rather than scoring whatever they submitted on paper. The two questions Braben has said mattered most were plain enough to ask a stranger over a long lunch: what would you do, given resources without limit, that you are not currently doing; and is your answer the kind of thing a Nobel committee might eventually reward. Nobody involved wrote a specific-aims page. Nobody budgeted a timeline broken into deliverable milestones. Selection ran on Braben's own read of a candidate's honesty about their own uncertainty and their willingness to keep going once an experiment told them something they had not expected, a quality he has described, half admiringly and half as a warning to himself, as looking for credible heretics rather than merely difficult ones.

Funded researchers then received support for years at a stretch, with no interim report required and no defined output specified in advance, an arrangement close to the exact photographic negative of a modern federal grant, where an applicant writes a proposal detailed enough that reviewers can score its feasibility, then reports back on a fixed schedule against the very aims that were promised before any of the actual research happened. A scientist who wanted to keep a Venture Research grant running past its first term did not submit a renewal application either. They had another conversation with Braben, in which the only real question was whether the work in front of them still felt as unfinished and as important to continue as it had the first time he asked.

Over its ten years, Venture Research spent somewhere in the neighborhood of twenty million pounds, roughly two million a year, working out to something on the order of a hundred to two hundred thousand pounds behind any single project in a given year, on something like three or four dozen researchers and small teams across Europe and North America. Measured against what a single national funding agency spends in a single afternoon, the whole operation ran on what amounts to pocket change, the fact Braben most wants a reader to register first, and the fact that matters least once the rest of the record gets checked.

The Nobel-committee question hides subtler work than it first appears to do. Asking whether a candidate's answer might eventually be recommended for the field's highest existing prize sounds like a way to filter for ambition. It is also, underneath that framing, a request that the candidate's unproven idea already resemble the kind of achievement an existing elite institution has trained itself to recognize as great, a strange test to build into a program whose entire premise is that existing institutions cannot recognize greatness until decades after the fact. A criterion aimed at impressing a committee that already exists tends, whatever its intent, to keep selecting for people who already look like the sort of person that committee has rewarded before. That tension resurfaces later this hour, once names and numbers enter the record.

## Chapter Four: A Very Small List, Spread Very Wide

What that pocket change actually reached is a stranger and more varied list than the physics-lab image of the program usually suggests. Jack Heslop-Harrison and Michael Bennett spent years of Venture Research funding mapping how plant chromosomes package and organize their own DNA, work that fed directly into the genome-sequencing techniques molecular biology now takes for granted. Martyn Poliakoff, later one of the more visible advocates for chemistry done with less waste and fewer toxic solvents, did early work under the same funding on reactions run in supercritical fluids, years before green chemistry had the name or the constituency it has now. Gene Stanley and José Teixeira spent their grant years on one of condensed-matter physics's odder standing puzzles, why liquid water's density and viscosity behave so strangely as it supercools, a question with no product at the end of it and no natural home in a grant cycle built around predicted deliverables.

Each of those projects shares nothing with the others except the shape of the bet placed on it: a specific person, already respected inside a narrow field, given years to chase a question that would have read, on a conventional proposal form, as either too basic to justify the money or too underdeveloped to score well against a specific-aims page. None of them produced a headline in nineteen eighty-five or nineteen ninety. Poliakoff's own case took the longest to pay off in any form a funding agency would recognize: supercritical carbon dioxide as a solvent had no constituency in nineteen eighties chemistry, no environmental regulation yet pushing industry to want it, and no journal eager to feature reactions run in a fluid most working chemists had never handled. It took the better part of two more decades, and considerable work well beyond anything Venture Research funded, before green chemistry became a recognized subfield with its own conferences and its own graduate courses, Poliakoff among the researchers now credited with having pushed it there. Several of the other projects look, from the distance of four decades, like the kind of patient foundational work a system optimized for three-year deliverables reliably starves. The honest reading of that list is not that BP found the next Einstein six times running. It is that a funder willing to back a person rather than score a plan will end up, almost by construction, holding a portfolio that looks unfocused and unfashionable at every point along the way, and that unfocused, unfashionable portfolios are what work aimed at a field's blind spots is supposed to look like from the outside, while it is still happening.

A grant committee scoring any one of these proposals on its own merits in nineteen eighty would have struggled to make the case for funding it. Chromosome mapping with no sequencing technology yet built to use the map, a green solvent with no green-chemistry field to publish it in, a low-temperature puzzle in water with no application anyone could name: each reads, on paper, exactly like the kind of speculative basic research a review panel is trained to rank behind a narrower, better-justified proposal from someone working the same budget line. Braben's bet was that the panel's training was the problem, not the proposals.

## Chapter Five: What a Decade of Trust Actually Bought

The clearest small case of the mechanism doing just what Braben claims it does belongs to two physicists at the University of Nottingham, Stan Clough and Anthony Horsewill, who spent roughly three years under Venture Research funding studying something almost nobody outside their own subfield had reason to care about: the way a methyl group, three hydrogen atoms hanging off the end of a molecule, can rotate between equivalent positions at temperatures cold enough that ordinary heat cannot supply the energy needed to climb over the barrier holding it in place. Quantum mechanics lets it happen anyway, tunneling through the barrier rather than over it, and the effect shows up as a faint splitting in nuclear magnetic resonance spectra and as a distinct peak in neutron scattering data, detectable only with instruments run at very low temperature over long, patient measurement campaigns. It had no obvious industrial application, no chance of a headline, and no natural home in a three-year grant built around a specific promised deliverable. Clough and Horsewill got the years anyway, published a careful sequence of results through the early nineteen nineties mapping how the tunneling frequency shifted with the local chemical environment around the rotor, and moved a small, genuine question a real distance forward. Nobody outside solid-state physics has heard of the result, and nobody involved in Venture Research ever claimed anybody should have.

At the other end of the same list sit people whose reputations needed no defending at all. Edsger Dijkstra, already one of the founding figures of computer science by the time BP found him, held Venture Research funding continuously for twelve years, longer than any other recipient in the program's history, doing work Braben has never itemized in any detail beyond noting that it ran that long because nobody ever asked Dijkstra to justify a single year of it against a plan written before the work began. Dudley Herschbach, who would go on to share a Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on the dynamics of chemical reactions, appears on the same funding rolls. Between an obscure Nottingham physics result nobody outside the field remembers and a Nobel laureate whose reputation the fund merely rode alongside, the honest range of what two million pounds a year actually bought runs the entire distance from modest, competent science that did exactly what any grant should do, to funding whose real return on investment is unclear because the recipient's own brilliance was never in question before BP ever wrote a check. Sorting which end of that range proves Braben's argument, and which end simply confirms that giving already-eminent people money tends to produce eminent-looking results, is a harder question than the book itself ever quite stops to ask.

## Chapter Six: The Discipline a Loose Structure Still Needs

That gap between the modest case and the eminent one is where Braben's method runs into the same underlying problem Vannevar Bush wrestled with running a wartime research committee, on the other side of an ocean and four decades earlier. Bush needed a way to tell a genuine outside expert with something valuable to say from a confident amateur who could talk a fast-moving, loosely governed wartime program into wasting scarce materials on a scheme that would never work, and he settled the question through institutional standing: a scientist embedded inside a university department, answerable to colleagues who had already vetted their competence over years, earned the trust a stranger walking in off the street with the same idea never could. Braben faced the identical filtering problem with almost none of Bush's institutional scaffolding to lean on, since half his entire pitch was that the department, the peer group, the whole apparatus of institutional vetting was exactly what had grown too conservative to recognize the people who deserved funding in the first place. His filter had to run through something even riskier than a résumé: one man's face-to-face read of a stranger's honesty and nerve, repeated a few dozen times across a decade, with no committee standing behind the call and nobody but Braben himself to answer for it going wrong.

That is a different kind of bet altogether from Bush's contract-instead-of-conscription arrangement, not a smaller version of it. Bush kept accountability inside the system, just relocated it from a military chain of command to a scientist's own university department and its normal professional incentives. Braben tried to remove accountability from the system almost entirely, and replace it with the judgment of a single, specific, fallible person sitting across a table. The question the rest of this book, and the rest of this hour, actually turns on is not whether that judgment was good. By most outside measures, it plainly was, often enough to matter. The question is what happens to an argument for funding science this way once the one irreplaceable ingredient, one person's trustworthy nerve for reading strangers, gets asked to do work at any scale larger than a single company's discretionary budget and a single evaluator's calendar.

## Chapter Seven: A Company's Patience Runs Out

Venture Research's own end supplies the first piece of evidence, and Braben's book treats it almost as an afterthought rather than the load the whole argument was carrying the entire time. The unit survived inside BP for a decade in large part because a senior executive named Bob Malpas had shielded it from the kind of scrutiny an oil company routinely applies to every other line item in its budget. Once Malpas left the company and a new chairman moved to concentrate BP's spending back onto its core petroleum business, Venture Research had no defense available to it, because its entire founding premise had been that it would never generate the kind of defensible progress report a nervous board could point to and understand. Braben's own account is that no specific project had failed. BP simply wanted a simpler story to tell its own investors, and an oil company funding tunneling physics and computer science for reasons nobody outside the unit could summarize in a sentence was not that story.

Braben has always framed the closure as a failure of one company's nerve, a decision made by people who lost their taste for something that was working, and the years afterward did little to soften that verdict for him. He kept trying. Once he moved to University College London he assembled a smaller version of the same idea on whatever discretionary money he could find, and one of its longest-running successes, a biochemist named Nick Lane studying the deep chemical origins of life inside early cells, eventually turned a few years of unaccountable seed funding into several million pounds of conventional grant support once the work had matured enough to compete for it on paper. That later case concedes something the rest of the book never quite says out loud: even Braben's own attempt to keep the model alive needed the ordinary, peer-reviewed system waiting downstream to absorb the work once it stopped looking strange, which suggests the two approaches were never really rivals so much as different stages of the same pipeline, one built to recognize a question before it has proof, the other built to fund the proof once the question has one.

Read a little less generously than Braben reads it himself, the BP ending reveals something closer to a structural property of the whole model than a one-off failure of nerve. A funding arrangement whose entire value proposition rests on trust, ask us for nothing and we promise not to ask you for anything either, has no institutional memory to fall back on once the specific relationships holding that trust together change. Malpas leaves, and the trust leaves with him. There was no board resolution to point to, no accumulated case file of results to defend the unit on its own record, because building that kind of defensible record was the exact bureaucratic overhead the whole program had been designed from the start to avoid.

## Chapter Eight: What the Record Actually Shows

Braben's case for reviving something like Venture Research rests on treating its results as settled history rather than a contested count, and the contest turns out to matter a great deal. Steven Hill, who directs research policy at Research England and spent years running the funding-assessment machinery Braben's book argues against, went looking for the demographic detail Braben's own writing leaves out and found it revealing on its own terms. Of the twenty-six researchers behind the roughly thirteen projects the book credits with real transformative results, only two were women, and both of them worked on the same single project. The internal selection panel that made every one of those calls numbered three people, two of whom Braben has never named publicly. Hill's own conclusion is blunt: the actual shortage in British research is not a shortage of freedom for people who already look credible to three unnamed gatekeepers, it is a shortage of routes in for the very researchers a small, non-anonymous panel is least equipped to see. A process built entirely on personal recognition will, as a near mathematical certainty, keep recognizing as credible the kind of person its own members already know how to recognize, and in nineteen-eighties Britain that machinery reliably produced one very narrow kind of winner.

José Luis Ricón, reviewing the book for his blog Nintil, pushes on a different seam and finds it just as unstable. Every claim in Scientific Freedom about which funded projects counted as a genuine success rests on Braben's own list, compiled and graded by the same person who chose the recipients in the first place, with no comparison offered to the applicants Venture Research turned down or to what an ordinary funding agency might have produced with the identical twenty million pounds. Ricón, who knows the actual Planck Club names Braben invokes elsewhere in the book well enough to recognize most of them on sight, had never come across several of the specific showcase results the book itself calls transformative, Clough and Horsewill's tunneling work among them, a gap that either means the results were narrower than Braben's own language for them suggests, or that transformative, measured honestly, needs a standard less dependent on the one man doing the counting.

Put those two critiques beside the plainest arithmetic problem in the whole proposal, and the case against sharpens further. Venture Research ran on roughly two million pounds a year and a caseload of a few dozen relationships that fit, quite literally, inside one physicist's actual capacity to sit across a table from a candidate and form a personal judgment about their nerve. The National Institutes of Health fund several hundred thousand investigators in a single year. There is no version of extended face-to-face trust-building that survives being multiplied by four orders of magnitude without becoming, at that scale, the same anonymous, procedural, committee-run process Braben spent the whole book arguing against. Stripped of its author's own civilizational framing, Venture Research was never a replacement for peer review. It was a boutique exception that only works at the scale of one company's spare budget and one trusted evaluator's calendar, a useful thing to know how to run in miniature, and a much smaller claim than the book is selling.

That leaves the one alternative Braben's own argument almost refuses to consider. Agencies built on program managers making high-risk bets under fixed-term contracts, DARPA's model among the oldest and best documented, have their own record of funding work that looked like a bad bet on arrival and paid off regardless, including an early, skeptically received wager on the messenger-RNA platform that would go on to become one of the fastest vaccine technologies ever fielded. Newer philanthropic experiments, built specifically to fund the kind of multi-year, non-hypothesis-driven infrastructure work that neither a startup investor nor a standard grant will touch, keep a defined budget and an actual end date without pretending either one defeats the purpose. Braben has called Britain's own attempt at a DARPA-style agency mistaken, on the grounds that any milestone at all reintroduces the caution he built Venture Research to escape. That reads less like a conclusion reached by weighing the evidence and more like a preference for the one method he personally knows how to run, since the agencies he dismisses have their own genuine breakthroughs to point to, achieved with the very kind of accountability structure his book treats as fatal on principle rather than in practice.

## Chapter Nine: A Smaller, More Honest Version of the Bet

None of that empties the book out entirely. What survives the reckoning is not Braben's largest claim, that removing accountability altogether is the one true mechanism for finding scientific genius; the evidence for that runs thinner than his own confidence in it, and the DARPA and Focused Research Organization counterexamples show plenty of working settings between full accountability and none. What survives is a narrower, far more portable observation: a funder willing to bet on a specific person's character and determination, rather than on a written plan scored by that person's closest disciplinary peers, can sometimes reach work an anonymous committee process is structurally unlikely to recognize, because a proposal built to challenge a field's own assumptions will always read, on the page, as weaker than one built to confirm them.

That observation does not need Braben's stakes, an entire century's supply of scientific revolutions, to matter for a much smaller decision. It shows up every time someone with the standing to make a call alone, without a committee's shared verdict to hide behind if it goes wrong, has to decide how much runway to give a specific person whose actual pitch is barely more developed than a conversation. A fellowship director choosing how to spend an early-career researcher's protected time is making that same call, on a scale Braben would recognize immediately: give the trainee with the wide-open, still-unformed question two unstructured years to see where it goes, or steer them toward the better-scoped, more predictable project that is more likely to produce a paper on the schedule the institution expects.

Making that call well borrows Hill's warning as much as it borrows Braben's method. A director relying on nothing but a private read of a trainee's nerve is running Venture Research's exact mechanism at the scale of one relationship, with every one of its blind spots intact: the temptation to recognize promise most easily in whoever already looks and sounds like a familiar kind of success, the total absence of anyone else's judgment to catch a bad call before it costs someone two years of an early career rather than an oil company's spare change. Braben's book does not resolve which choice is right in any given case, and pretending it does would be dishonest to what the record actually shows. What it does more usefully is strip away the comfortable assumption that a well-run committee would have caught the same promising trainee anyway, since the history of the Planck Club, McClintock's thirty years included, argues that committees built to reward what already looks credible are the reviewers least likely to recognize the work still too strange to look like anything yet. That argument cuts both ways at once, toward more nerve and toward more scrutiny of whose nerve is actually being trusted, and a fellowship director who only hears the first half has read the book worse than one who takes in the second.

## Chapter Ten: The Actual Cost of Guessing Wrong

Read Scientific Freedom for the chapters describing how the selection conversations actually worked and for Braben's own account of BP's decision to end them, since those are the parts of the book a reader can act on without first accepting the century-spanning claims about civilizational stagnation that the rest of the argument leans on more heavily than the evidence supports. The book's real subject, underneath its author's grander framing, is a single decision that recurs at a far smaller scale than a national funding agency ever will: whether to fund the proposal that looks safe to score, or the person whose actual case rests on nothing more defensible than your own read of their nerve.

Braben got to make that call a few dozen times, backed by an oil company's spare budget and a decade of one executive's protection, and it worked out often enough that a careful reviewer counting the actual results still finds several worth the twenty million pounds. Most of the people reading this were never handed that budget or that cover. What they do get, on a smaller and more exposed scale, is the same choice, minus the company standing behind it if the bet fails, and minus, per Hill's count, any guarantee that the person making the call has thought hard about who their own instincts are actually built to favor.

The next time a trainee's whole case for two protected years amounts to little more than a conversation, ask honestly which is actually driving the decision to send them toward the safer, better-scoped project instead: a genuine judgment that it is the better bet, or the simple fact that the safer choice comes with a committee to share the blame if it does not pay off. Braben spent a decade of BP's money finding out that the second kind of caution can starve a real result for as long as anyone lets it. He never quite reckoned with how easily the first kind of judgment, exercised alone, can end up rewarding the one kind of person it was already primed to trust.
