## Chapter One: A Hundred Feet at a Time

On the evening of the twenty-eighth of April 1961, a sixty-one-year-old vice admiral landed in Boston and was driven north to the naval shipyard at Portsmouth, on the river between New Hampshire and Maine. The officer who met him, Commander Dean Axene, was due to take a new submarine to sea for the first time the next morning. She was called the Thresher, the first of a class designed to run quieter and dive far deeper than anything the navy had built, and the talk on the drive was about the agenda for her trials. The agenda treated the first descent to her full operating limit, what the navy calls test depth, as a routine dive. Axene and the yard's senior nuclear officer had asked for a stepwise descent to be written in. They had been told there was no need.

The admiral was Hyman Rickover, and the dive was none of his business. His authority on that ship covered the reactor and the machinery it fed. The hull, the piping that carried seawater, the schedule: all of that belonged to the Bureau of Ships and the yard. He arrived at the yard furious anyway, and he demanded that the submarine go down a hundred feet at a time, stopping at each level so the crew could check every saltwater pipe and work the valves and pumps. The yard's officers fought him. He and they were still at the table at two in the morning, and a few details were unsettled when the lines came off a few hours later.

The next day, deep but not yet at the limit, a strain gauge on the hull reported far more stress than the designers had predicted. Rickover thought the instrument was probably wrong. He ordered the dive stopped regardless. Back in port, the gauge proved to be faulty.

Just under two years later the Thresher went to test depth again, after nine months in that same yard, with nobody from Rickover's office aboard, because nothing in the reactor plant needed watching. She did not come back. One hundred and twenty-nine men were on board.

Rickover ran the navy's reactors from the late nineteen forties until he was removed in 1982, a few days after his eighty-second birthday. By then his programme had logged more than twenty-three hundred reactor-years without a reactor accident. That record and that submarine belong to the same man and the same method, and the method had a slogan: responsibility must rest with one person you can point to. This episode asks how far such a principle reaches, and what lies on the other side of the line where it stops.

## Chapter Two: Engineering Duty Only

Who was he? He was born in January 1900 in Makow, a town in Russian Poland, and came to America at six. His family settled on the West Side of Chicago. He worked afternoons and weekends as a Western Union messenger through high school, got into the Naval Academy in 1918 through an uncle's political connection, barely passed the entrance examination, and graduated a hundred and seventh in a class of five hundred and forty.

In the mid nineteen thirties Rickover was assistant engineer of the battleship New Mexico during a fleet-wide competition for fuel economy. The chief engineer left him to it. The ship went from sixth place among the battleships to first and stayed there two years running. He did it by attending to the main plant and then to everything else that burned oil: the fresh water distillers, the heating, the lighting. Officers wore overcoats in the wardroom. His men went round the ship unscrewing light bulbs and shutting off shower heads. Every later trait is visible: the result, the method of going after each small thing, the indifference to whether anyone was comfortable, and a crew that by Duncan's account enjoyed the hunt.

The account here comes from two histories by government historians whom Rickover himself invited in: Nuclear Navy, by Richard Hewlett and Francis Duncan, from 1974, and Duncan's later Rickover and the Nuclear Navy, which carries the story to the retirement. Duncan's preface says he came to think Rickover a great man, so this is an insider's book and I have read it as one. The two best-known biographies, Polmar and Allen's critical one and Theodore Rockwell's admiring one, I could not open, and where their arguments appear they come from memory.

In 1937 he gave up the chance of command at sea and transferred to a small corps of officers designated for engineering duty only. During the war he ran the electrical section of the Bureau of Ships, which grew under him from twenty-three people to three hundred and forty-three, and which kept its technical work in its own hands when other sections had passed theirs to field offices and settled for administering contracts. He read every battle damage report and visited every damaged ship he could reach. In 1946 the bureau sent him with a handful of others to Oak Ridge to learn what a reactor was. Nobody had been put in charge of naval propulsion at either the navy or the new Atomic Energy Commission, and by work and manoeuvre he ended up holding a post in both at once.

The navy then tried to get rid of him. Selection boards passed him over for rear admiral in 1951 and again in 1952, which under the rules meant retirement the following year, while his submarine was on the building ways. The press and Congress took it up, and the navy gave in. Congress forced his next two promotions as well. From 1964 his tenure had to be renewed every two years, and it was renewed for eighteen.

So here is an officer whose own service tried more than once to retire him, kept in place by legislators, holding two offices in two agencies. Listeners to the Robert Moses episodes will recognise the architecture: a man who cannot be removed by either of his masters, because each would need the other's consent. Moses used that position to build roads nobody could stop. What did Rickover use it for?

## Chapter Three: Ireland

In the Idaho desert in the spring of 1953 stood a submarine's engine room with no submarine around it. Rickover had insisted that the first reactor be built as a working propulsion plant, arranged inside a section of hull as it would sit at sea, with a real turbine behind it. He wanted the ship's machinery on land, where it could be broken without drowning anyone. That prototype, called Mark One, reached a self-sustaining reaction at the end of March and made its first steam for the turbine on the last day of May.

The next step was a run at full power. The plan called for forty-eight hours, enough to collect the data the physicists needed. Then calculations showed twenty-four would do, and the run was shortened. Rickover learned of the change on his way to the site on the twenty-fifth of June and reversed it. The plant would run for a hundred hours. That night, on a cot in a Quonset hut, he had an idea: put a chart of the North Atlantic on the control room wall, and at the end of each four-hour watch let the crew mark where the ship would be.

He had to return to Washington before it ended. Around the sixtieth hour, things began to go wrong. Generators misbehaved. The nuclear instruments turned erratic. One of the big pumps that pushed coolant through the reactor started making a noise nobody liked. The engineers in Idaho asked for permission to shut down. Rickover, on the telephone, refused each request. The crew kept marking the chart. When their reckoning put the ship off the Irish coast, they shut the plant down by the book.

Read as a stunt, this is a man gambling a prototype for a headline. Hewlett and Duncan read it otherwise, and their case is in where the trouble came from. The failures at hour sixty were in the steam plant and the mechanical equipment, the conventional parts, the technology the navy had been buying for decades. Nothing went wrong with the reactor core or its controls, which had absorbed years of worry. He kept the plant running because he judged the reactor itself was in no danger, and because a hundred hours would find out which pump and which generator would fail a crew in mid-ocean. A day-long run would have proved the physics and told him nothing about the ship.

Two rules that he laid down early are both in that story. Engineering, and not science, would govern the programme. And he and his own engineers, never the contractor, would make the technical decisions; if a decision went wrong there would be no committee to absorb it. The same willingness cut the other way three years later. The second submarine, the Seawolf, had a reactor cooled by liquid sodium, a design with real advantages on paper. Its steam generators leaked. Rickover told the commission he would pull the sodium plant out of the boat and replace it with pressurised water; sodium systems, he wrote, were costly to build, complicated to operate and slow to repair. The design was his own programme's, and he scrapped it.

The finding from Idaho stayed with him for thirty years. The new and frightening part was the part people were careful about. What failed was the valve, the weld, the pump: the ordinary thing whose makers believed they already knew how to make it. A man who believes that will need a way of seeing ordinary things, in other people's factories, a long way from his desk. How do you see them?

## Chapter Four: Pinks

Rickover liked to say he had no organisation, and in 1980, with three hundred and fifty-nine people in his Washington office, he issued an elaborate chart to prove it. The title, the date and his signature were in English. Every box was labelled in Chinese characters.

The joke covered a design. He had read the management texts of the nineteen thirties and once kept their precepts framed over his desk; he gave them up as unusable. The textbook said nobody could supervise more than five or six people whose work interlocked. He had thirteen technical section heads and six project officers reporting straight to him, plus a business group, and he reached past any of them to whichever junior engineer knew most about a problem. That was the visible part. Underneath it was a set of rules about paper.

Every working day, a pink carbon copy of every letter drafted in the office, finished or not, went to his desk. Most were drafted by the youngest engineers. No section chief was permitted to intercept or alter one. So the head of the programme saw what the newest people thought before their supervisors had shaped it, and the supervisors knew he had.

Outside Washington he kept seventeen field offices, at the laboratories, at the prototype sites, and at every shipyard that built or overhauled a nuclear ship. The representative at each yard telephoned him on fixed days of the week whether or not there was anything to say. If there was nothing, the call still happened, and the log showed that the man had called. Each week a letter came in from every site, and the rules for that letter were exact. The assistants who had walked the ship or the shop floor wrote their own reports. The head of the office did not see them until they were signed. He could add a comment. He could strike out a paragraph he thought trivial, but the struck words had to stay legible, and every annotation carried a clear signature. He could not change a word.

Consider what that removes. In the corporations Robert Jackall studied for Moral Mazes, bad news travels upward by being softened at each level, until what reaches the top is a mood. Every layer has a reason to edit, and nobody ever decides to lie. Rickover made the editing visible. A supervisor who thought a subordinate's finding was nonsense could say so, in his own name, beside the finding, and the admiral would read both.

He added one more rule of interpretation. A report that had nothing in it made him suspicious. Perhaps everything was fine. Perhaps the man was not looking. Andy Grove, in an earlier episode, wanted every indicator paired with a second that catches the gaming of the first. Rickover's version was to keep several channels that could not be reconciled before they reached him: the project officer with his schedule, the technical section with its component, the field man on the telephone. Disagreement among them was what he was reading for.

A programme that monitors lay counsellors in distant districts through forms and a dashboard, as the one this show's listener runs does, has the same problem in a milder climate. Each level of supervision between the session and the dashboard is a level that can tidy. The pink copy suggests asking which document in the chain reaches the centre in the words of the person who watched the session, and what a supervisor is allowed to do to it on the way.

The cost fell first on him. The mail arrived every few hours and could not wait. A messenger with a briefcase met him on the pier at the end of sea trials. He had the office post brought to his hospital bed after surgery. It fell second on the field men, who were forbidden, with their families, to socialise with the contractors they watched, and whose job he described as needing godlike qualities: tell the yard what is wrong, never tell it what to do, and make something happen anyway. Some left exhausted and bitter. Yet when he retired, his hundred most senior people had been with the programme fifteen years on average.

A system like that depends entirely on who is in it. He would not hire experienced people from other parts of the navy or from industry, with three exceptions that Duncan lists by trade. He took them young and trained them himself. So the method needs a gate, and the gate is the most notorious thing about him.

## Chapter Five: The Interview

Nobody could command a nuclear ship unless Rickover had personally admitted him to the programme, and by the nineteen sixties that meant several hundred interviews a year. The procedure, as Duncan gives it, was more structured than the legend. Each candidate first saw three senior engineers separately, for about twenty minutes each. The engineer used an office that was not his own, so the telephone could not interrupt, and afterwards wrote down the questions he had asked, his evaluation and a recommendation. The candidate then went in to the admiral, who had the three appraisals in front of him.

The academic screening was done by then. What Rickover was after was whether a man could take an unexpected question and answer it straight. A midshipman asked about a poor class standing who replied with the Academy formula, no excuse, sir, got a tirade, because that was a ritual and it answered nothing; admitting laziness would have gone better. A man who claimed he would repair a condenser pump by leadership was in trouble. A man who had argued himself into an absurd position and held it, believing this showed firmness, was removed to another room to reconsider. Some went back in three or four times. The questions could be personal: was he engaged, and who had proposed? According to Hewlett and Duncan, a candidate might be made to back a promise with a deed, such as singing a solo or losing thirty pounds. The stories of a chair with shortened front legs and of candidates shut in a broom closet come from other books and from memoirs, and I have them only from memory; the official historians say that the published accounts, though often incredible, were usually close to fact.

The admirer's defence should be heard first. A reactor operator's worst failing is the confident answer that conceals ignorance, and a culture of naval courtesy trains young officers to give it. Twenty minutes of being contradicted by an old man with power over your career is a cheap way to find out who bluffs. Rickover held that standing procedures cover the normal case and that in an emergency the operator must understand the plant well enough to depart from them. He was selecting for people who knew what they did not know. And the three prior interviews, written up independently, are better practice than most hiring.

Now the clinician's questions. What was the denominator? Duncan reports that about a tenth of those admitted later failed the schools, mostly on academics. About those turned away there is nothing, and there lies the test of any selection instrument: nobody followed the rejected officers to see whether they were worse. The rater was not blind, since he read three recommendations before the candidate sat down. He sometimes overruled them in each direction, and no record tells us whether his overrides did better than his engineers. It was an instrument with one rater, never validated, defended by pointing at the outcomes of those it let through.

It had casualties. Hewlett and Duncan, who admired him, wrote that for a few candidates the interview left "scars that would last a lifetime."

And it had a cost that the navy stated under oath. The gate was narrow, and the fleet was growing faster than the gate would pass people. In 1963 the chief of naval personnel testified that Polaris submarines were being launched faster than qualified officers could be found for them, that nuclear-trained officers were overworked and denied broader assignments, and that experienced submarine captains from the diesel boats were being turned away in numbers. Rickover's answer was that those captains carried habits a reactor would punish and that the navy must adapt to the technology. The personnel chief was speaking to a court of inquiry, and the court was sitting because a submarine had been lost.

## Chapter Six: Silver

Why would a submarine with a flawless reactor sink? Begin with a pipe joint. A nuclear submarine's machinery is cooled by seawater, so pipes open to the ocean run through the engine room, and at depth they must hold the same pressure as the hull. The navy joined them in two ways. A weld melts the two pipes into one piece of metal, and an X-ray can show whether it is sound. A silver-brazed joint is cheaper: the fitter heats the pipes and flows a silver alloy into the gap. Done well it holds. In 1960 there was no reliable way to tell from outside whether it had been done well.

In November of that year the submarine Barbel, built at Portsmouth, was near test depth when a brazed joint in a seawater line let go. Her captain drove her to the surface in three minutes, having taken on about eighteen tons of water. The yard had installed a pipe of the wrong material. In May 1961, on the Thresher's first full dive, a small brazed joint failed and filled a compartment with spray, and the crew found a second failed joint while repairing the first. In August another new boat, the Snook, flooded in the engine room during her trials; again a mix-up of materials, in a joint that had passed inspection.

Days after the Snook he signed an order: within the reactor compartment of every submarine, any pipe exposed to sea pressure would have its brazed joints cut out and welded. His reasoning was that radiation limited access to that compartment, so its piping had to be right the first time. Then he stopped, because the reactor compartment was where his authority ended. For the rest of the ship he wrote letters. One, to the chief of the bureau in February 1962, said that seawater and steam systems mattered to the safety of the ship as much as nuclear ones, and recommended that the yard be made to meet the bureau's own welding rules.

The same year the Thresher was shaken deliberately with explosive charges off Key West, to see what combat would do to her. Twenty-eight brazed joints gave way. One of the twenty-eight was in the reactor compartment, a pinhole, in a pipe that did not carry seawater.

She then went back to Portsmouth for an overhaul planned at six months. A new ultrasonic method could now test brazed joints without cutting them, and the bureau told the yard to keep a team testing for as long as the ship was in its hands. By late November the team had examined a hundred and forty-five of the old joints in the larger seawater lines. Of those, 13.8 percent failed the minimum standard. To test more, insulation would have to come off pipes, and the overhaul was already late. On the fourth of December the yard stopped testing the old joints. The shipyard commander knew. The bureau was not told of the decision, and was not sent the results.

Earlier, when Rickover's own people had reviewed the yard's X-ray films of reactor welds and found many too poor to read, he had halted all reactor work there until the films were his division's to judge. When the yard reviewed its films for the rest of a sister ship, it reported that the pipe radiographs did not meet the required sensitivity but appeared good enough to have shown a major defect. Duncan sets the two attitudes side by side: a standard as something work should come close to, and a standard as the least that will be accepted.

So by early 1963 one hull held two regimes. In one compartment every joint was welded, every film kept, every deviation approved in Washington by a named engineer. Through the rest ran about one suspect joint in seven among those anyone had tested, with the testing abandoned. What would that ship do at depth?

## Chapter Seven: Minor Difficulties

The Thresher's last months add a second strand. In January 1963, with the overhaul dragging, the navy transferred her captain and her executive officer to other boats, because nuclear-qualified commanders were too scarce to leave waiting in a shipyard. The captain was Dean Axene, the man who had met Rickover off the Boston flight, and he asked to stay and was refused. His replacement, Lieutenant Commander John Harvey, was an able officer on his first command. Only three of his officers had been with the ship any length of time. When Harvey sealed the boat at the pier in March to drill the crew, he counted four hundred and fifty-six deficiencies and cancelled the exercise. In a drill that simulated flooding, the crew took twenty minutes to isolate the leak.

She sailed on the ninth of April with her crew and a party of yard workers and contractors. The next morning, far off Cape Cod in eighty-four hundred feet of water, she began her deep dive in stages, reporting by underwater telephone to an escort ship whose rescue chamber could reach eight hundred and fifty feet. At about thirteen minutes past nine the men on the escort's bridge heard a calm voice: "Experiencing minor difficulties. Have positive up angle. Attempting to blow." They heard air under pressure. Some minutes later came a garbled transmission, and then a sound the escort's navigator recognised as a hull breaking up.

A court of inquiry heard a hundred and twenty-one witnesses. Its probable sequence ran like this. A seawater joint failed in the engine room. Spray reached electrical equipment and tripped the reactor's automatic shutdown. Without the reactor there was no power to drive the ship upward, and at that depth a submarine climbs on speed and planes, since air displaces water from the ballast tanks slowly against so much pressure. Harvey ordered the tanks blown anyway, and the air stopped flowing. When the court tried a full blow on a sister ship tied to the pier, the valves froze. Moisture in the high-pressure air had turned to ice on small mesh strainers inside the valves, and blocked them in about thirty seconds. Those strainers appeared in no navy specification. The manufacturer had added them as an extra, to keep grit out.

Anyone who heard the Charles Perrow episode will know this pattern. Three faults, each survivable alone, reached one another by routes no drawing showed: water to a switchboard, a switchboard to the reactor, and a protective part that defeated the last resort. Perrow's claim was that a system both intricate and fast will sooner or later do this whatever the diligence of its people.

The court blamed no individual. The accusation that followed was aimed at Rickover. The navy's senior submarine officer told the congressional committee that reactor operating procedures were rigid, that after an automatic shutdown the crew had to work through fixed steps before restarting, and that those minutes might have been the ones that counted. Add the personnel chief's testimony, and the charge is that the safest part of the ship helped sink it twice over: its procedures cost time, and its narrow gate had stripped the wardroom of experience.

Rickover's reply was partly evidence and partly indignation. On evidence: a computer group had been asked to simulate the sinking, and each simulation was called a run. Rickover and his engineers went to that group and to the acoustic analysts and found that no run matched the sequence the court had called most probable; that the scenario was patched together from several, with the times adjusted; and that a fresh run with the escort's logged times had the submarine reaching the surface. The data could not say whether the reactor had stopped at all. Duncan doubts the flooding theory altogether, since no crew would call a major leak at test depth a minor difficulty. On indignation, Rickover said his manuals already told captains that nothing in them restricted action to save the ship. Yet he did shorten the restart procedure afterwards, while insisting that he had been shortening it since the first boat.

I do not think the charge can be settled, and neither did the court. What can be said is where everyone agreed. In closed session Rickover told the inquiry not to look for a single failed joint. The loss, he said, came from a philosophy of design, building and inspection that the navy had tolerated across its shipbuilding, and the lesson was to change how the work was done.

## Chapter Eight: The Bulkhead

The first answer to this episode's question would be that named responsibility works. Inside the line the record is the best in the history of a dangerous technology, and the one ship lost went down from the other side of it. That is Duncan's reading and it is mostly right. I held it for most of the book, and it leaves something out.

Go back to how the line was drawn. When the Thresher was assigned to Portsmouth, the bureau proposed that the yard also do the detailed design of her reactor plant, with Rickover's preferred contractor working beneath it. He refused any arrangement that diluted his responsibility. After the loss, the Secretary of the Navy asked him to join the group reappraising the submarine's design. He refused that too, on the ground that it would involve him in matters where he had no responsibility; his staff would advise if asked. The two refusals are the same act. A man who will be answerable for everything inside a boundary must decline to be partly answerable outside it, because partial answerability is the thing he has defined as worthless. His sentence, from testimony he gave many times, was that unless you can point at one person when something goes wrong, "you have never had anyone really responsible."

So the principle makes the island and the shoreline together, and the shoreline is where a system accident walks ashore. Perrow's critics observed that whether an event looks like an unforeseeable interaction or an ordinary lapse depends on where the analyst draws the edge of the system. Rickover's career makes that observation concrete. Twenty-three hundred reactor-years with no reactor accident is a true statement about the system he defined. One hundred and twenty-nine dead is the same period reckoned by the ship. A leak, a switchboard, a shutdown, a frozen valve: the chain ran through his compartment and broke nothing in it.

The island also drew on the sea. The nuclear superintendent at Portsmouth took qualified welders and pipefitters out of the yard's general workforce for reactor work. The gate on officers produced the shortage that moved a seasoned captain off the Thresher the winter before she sailed. The official histories do not say excellence in one compartment caused weakness in the next, and I cannot show it. But a yard of nine thousand people had one stock of first-rate welders, and somebody else's pipes got the rest.

What the navy did next did not involve finding another Rickover. Within two months of the loss, the bureau set up a programme called submarine safety, soon shortened to SUBSAFE. Critical piping would be welded and X-rayed, or ultrasonically tested if brazed. The ballast blow system was redesigned. And the yards had to keep records proving, component by component, that nothing vital to keeping the sea out had been skipped or passed below standard. Rickover's commentary was a list: the bureau would now require written procedures, audits, records and no deviation without approval from headquarters, and he had always done every one of those. Certification later became specific to each submarine and expired after a set period. As far as I know, and this is from memory, no submarine certified under that programme has been lost; the Scorpion, which went down in 1968, was not.

The piping got Rickover's paperwork and never got Rickover: the named engineer who signs the waiver, the retained film, the audit that does not need the yard's permission. Last episode sorted checks into those that need the consent of the person checked and those that do not. The second kind crossed the bulkhead, and it crossed without the telephone calls or the interviews. Something in the method was portable. Was the rest of it necessary, or was it one man's temperament passed off as engineering?

## Chapter Nine: Actuarial Reasons

Duncan puts that question to himself near the end of the book. Rickover ran every job the same way from 1922, on a destroyer, on a battleship, in an electrical section, long before there was a reactor to justify it. Duncan takes that as evidence the principles transfer. It is equally evidence that the discipline of technology was a personality that found, in the reactor, the one place where it was correct.

Test the temperament against that question. The abuse first. The official history says in plain terms that he was loud and vociferous, that he cancelled subordinates' family plans, and that good people left because of it. Nothing in either book shows a weld improved by it, and the piping regime that followed the Thresher worked without it. The conservatism is harder to separate. Critics inside the navy charged that his grip on reactor design blocked smaller and cheaper plants; Duncan answers that the rival coolants were each tried or studied and found worse, and that other navies and the world's power stations chose pressurised water too. Refusing the untried was written into his design rules, so here the temperament and the engineering are one thing.

Then there is the fact that nobody could check him. He was audited: the General Accounting Office once went through his travel records because it could not believe one man had been in so many places in so few days, and found that he had. But the two-agency post that protected the programme from the navy protected him as well. With Congress behind him he was extended every two years into his eighties. After he had gone, the Secretary of the Navy censured him for accepting gifts from contractors, chiefly the firm that built his submarines; the Secretary's own statement, as Duncan reports it, was that he found no evidence the gifts had softened Rickover's standards, and when Rickover left office his allegations of fraudulent claims by shipbuilders, that firm's yard among them, were under government review. By then he had spent three decades as the judge of everyone else's deviations.

Why did that position not rot, as it did around Moses? Rickover's aphorism was that technology will not obey an order. A highway built in the wrong place stands for fifty years looking like an achievement. A reactor run by a man who has stopped listening lets him know. He submitted himself to an auditor who could not be flattered, and he held the post only because the plants worked. That is a real difference, and a limited one. Major accidents are rare, so their absence proves less than it seems to. A clinician's rule of thumb says that when you have watched for an event and seen none, the rate could still be as high as three divided by the size of your sample. Three over twenty-three hundred allows roughly one accident in every seven or eight hundred reactor-years. Rickover never rested his case on the zero, and here I think he saw further than his admirers. He counted precursors. He would not accept a control room where alarms sounded in routine running. After Three Mile Island he told the President's commission that the plant had so many alarms, some ringing during normal operation, that its operators could no longer tell a new one from the background.

Did it outlive him? He doubted it would. Duncan records him as frankly pessimistic that the utilities would follow his example, or that the naval programme would hold without his driving. On the second he seems to have been wrong. His successor was appointed for a fixed term of eight years under an executive order that set the dual post down in writing, nearly all his senior engineers stayed, and as far as I know the reactor record is unbroken. On the first he was nearer right. He urged the power companies to create their own technical body to set and enforce standards, and my memory is that an institute of that kind was founded within the year.

So the gate, the pinks, the retained films and the named signatures outlasted him. The shouting did not need to. The Secretary who removed him gave the reason as actuarial.

## Chapter Ten: Dose

Read Duncan's third chapter, on the Thresher, whole; it runs under fifty pages and is the best thing in either book. Follow it with chapter nine, on how the office ran, which closes on his remark that the Devil is in the details, but so is salvation; then the eleventh and last. The middle chapters on surface ships and congressional budgets are for specialists. In Hewlett and Duncan's earlier volume, the chapter on prototypes holds the Idaho run. Both are free from the Department of Energy. For the prosecution, Polmar and Allen is the book to find.

Here is a wager about any study that runs across several sites. Its protocol deviations will not cluster in the primary outcome. That measurement has an owner, a manual and somebody who gets asked about it every week. They will cluster in whatever lies just past that owner's line and feeds it: the scale that was calibrated at set-up, the cold room, the envelope drawer, the form a field worker fills in for a different team. The deviation log will settle the wager either way. If it goes as predicted, the entries will share one feature: for each of them, nobody could have said beforehand whose item it was. That was the condition of every silver-brazed joint outside one compartment of the Thresher.
